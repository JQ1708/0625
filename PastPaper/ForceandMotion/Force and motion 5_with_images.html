Question 33

(0625/41_Winter_2020_Q1) - Forces And Motion

Fig. 1.1 shows an ice-hockey player moving on ice. He is preparing to hit the solid disc called a puck.

The disc of mass 0.16 kg is moving horizontally across the surface of the ice at a speed of 15m/s.

(a) Calculate the magnitude of the momentum of the disc. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Momentum is calculated as p = mv. So, p = 0.16 kg × 15 m/s, which equals 2.4 kg m/s.

(b) The hockey player strikes the disc with his hockey stick and the momentum of the disc changes. The disc gains momentum of 3.0 kgm/s at 45° to the original direction of travel of the disc, as shown in Fig. 1.2.

(i) State the magnitude of the impulse exerted on the disc and the direction, in degrees, of the impulse relative to the original direction of travel. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The impulse is equal to the change in momentum. The magnitude of the impulse is 3.0 Ns, and its direction is 45° to the original direction.

(ii) Determine the magnitude of the new momentum of the disc and its new direction relative to the original direction of travel by drawing a scale diagram. [4]

Tap to reveal answer

By drawing a vector triangle or parallelogram to scale, the resultant (new) momentum is found. The magnitude of the new momentum is between 4.8 kgm/s and 5.2 kgm/s, at a direction between 22° and 28° to the original direction.

Question 34

(0625/42_Winter_2020_Q1) - Forces And Motion

A sky-diver jumps out of a hot-air balloon, which is 4000 m above the ground. At time = 30 s, she opens her parachute.

Fig. 1.1 is the speed-time graph of her fall.

(a) (i) Label with the letter X the point on the graph where the sky-diver opens her parachute. [1]

(ii) Label with the letters Y and Z the two parts of the graph where the sky-diver falls at terminal velocity. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

(i) Point X should be labelled at t = 30 s, where the graph shows a sudden, large deceleration.

(ii) Points Y and Z should be labelled on the horizontal sections of the graph, where the speed is constant (one before opening the parachute, one after).

(b) Describe, in terms of the forces acting on the sky-diver, her motion between leaving the balloon and opening her parachute. [4]

Tap to reveal answer

Initially, the only significant force is weight, causing downward acceleration. As speed increases, air resistance increases, opposing the motion. The resultant downward force decreases, so acceleration decreases. Eventually, air resistance becomes equal to weight, the resultant force is zero, and she falls at a constant terminal velocity.

(c) Calculate the average speed of the sky-diver in the first 4.0s of her fall. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Using the formula for average speed with uniform acceleration, average speed = (initial speed + final speed) ⁄ 2. From the graph, at t=4.0s, the speed is 40 m/s. So, (0 + 40) ⁄ 2 = 20 m/s.

Question 35

(0625/43_Winter_2020_Q1) - Forces And Motion

(a) Fig. 1.1 shows a trolley travelling down a ramp. The trolley has a piece of paper tape attached to it. The tape passes through a machine which makes a dot on the tape every 0.02s.

Fig. 1.2 shows a section of the tape.

(i) State how the dots on the tape show that the trolley was moving with constant speed. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The trolley was moving with constant speed because the dots on the tape are equally spaced.

(ii) When the trolley reaches the point P, the ramp is tilted so that the angle x is greater. Describe and explain the change in motion of the trolley. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Description: The trolley accelerates or its speed increases.

Explanation: A greater tilt angle means there is a larger resultant force acting on the trolley down the ramp, causing it to accelerate.

(b) Another trolley is released from the top of the ramp. Fig. 1.3 shows the speed-time graph for this trolley. Using Fig. 1.3, calculate the distance travelled by the trolley in the first 0.5s. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The distance is the area under the graph. For the first 0.5s, this is a triangle with area ½ × base × height = ½ × 0.5 s × 0.75 m/s, which is 0.19 m.

(c) Fig. 1.4 shows a metal ball at rest in a tube of liquid. The ball is released and reaches terminal velocity at point X. Explain the motion of the ball as it falls from rest until it reaches point X. Use ideas of force and acceleration in your answer. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

Initially, there is a downwards resultant force as weight is greater than the resistive forces, causing the ball to accelerate. As the velocity increases, the resistance from the liquid increases. This reduces the resultant force and the acceleration. At point X, the upwards resistive force equals the downwards weight, the resultant force is zero, and the ball travels at a constant terminal velocity.

Question 36

(0625/41_Winter_2020_Q2) - Forces And Motion, Forces And Energy

A vertical tube contains a liquid. A metal ball is held at rest by a thread just below the surface of the liquid, as shown in Fig. 2.1.

The diameter of the tube is much greater than the diameter of the ball. The ball is released and it accelerates downwards uniformly for a short period of time.

(a) Describe what happens to the velocity of the ball in the short period of time as it accelerates downwards uniformly. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The velocity of the ball increases at a constant rate or steadily.

(b) The ball reaches terminal velocity. Describe and explain the motion of the ball from when it is released until it reaches terminal velocity. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

The initial acceleration is caused by its weight. As speed increases, the drag force increases, which reduces the resultant force and causes the acceleration to decrease. Eventually, there is no resultant force when the drag force equals the weight, and the ball moves at a constant terminal velocity.

(c) The metal ball has a mass of 2.1 g. It falls a distance of 0.80m between being released and reaching the bottom of the tube.

(i) Calculate the gravitational potential energy transferred from the ball as it falls. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Gravitational potential energy is calculated as GPE = mgh. So, GPE = 0.0021 kg × 10 N/kg × 0.80 m, which equals 0.017 J.

(ii) When the ball reaches the bottom of the tube, it has a speed of 1.2 m/s. Calculate the kinetic energy of the ball at the bottom of the tube. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

Kinetic energy is calculated as KE = ½mv². So, KE = ½ × 0.0021 kg × (1.2 m/s)², which equals 1.5 x 10-3 J.

(iii) Explain why the value calculated in (c)(i) is different from that calculated in (c)(ii). [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The GPE lost is greater than the KE gained because some energy was transferred to do work done against friction or drag from the liquid, generating thermal energy.

Question 37

(0625/43_Winter_2020_Q2) - Forces And Energy, Forces And Motion

Fig. 2.1 shows a cliff edge with water below it. A ball falls over the edge of the cliff. The mass of the ball is 160g. The height of the cliff is 115 m.

(a) Calculate the vertical speed of the ball as it hits the water. Air resistance can be ignored. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

By conservation of energy, the potential energy lost equals the kinetic energy gained (mgh = ½mv²). This simplifies to v = √(2gh). So, v = √(2 × 10 N/kg × 115 m), which gives a speed of approximately 48 m/s.

(b) Calculate the vertical momentum of the ball as it hits the water. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Momentum is calculated as p = mv. So, p = 0.160 kg × 48 m/s, which equals 7.7 kg m/s.

(b) Fig. 3.1 shows a model car travelling at constant speed on a flat circular track. The speed of the car is 0.30 m/s. In one complete revolution around the track, the car travels 3.9m.

(i) Calculate the time taken for the car to complete one revolution around the track. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Time is calculated as t = distance ⁄ speed. So, t = 3.9 m ⁄ 0.30 m/s, which equals 13 s.

(ii) On Fig. 3.1, draw and label with the letter F an arrow to show the resultant force acting on the car. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The arrow F should be drawn from the car pointing towards the centre of the circle.

(iii) The speed of the car increases and at point P on Fig. 3.2 the car does not stay on the track.

1. Suggest, in terms of the force acting on the car, why the car does not stay on the track at point P. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

At a higher speed, a larger centripetal force is required to keep the car moving in a circle. The frictional force providing this inward pull is insufficient, so the car cannot follow the curved path.

2. On Fig. 3.2, draw and label an arrow with the letter S to show the direction of motion of the car as it leaves the track at point P. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The arrow S should be drawn as a tangent to the circular path at point P, continuing in a straight line.

Question 39

(0625/41_Summer_2021_Q1) - Forces And Motion

A skydiver of mass 76kg is falling vertically in still air. At time t = 0, the skydiver opens his parachute.

Fig. 1.1 is the speed-time graph for the skydiver from t = 0.

(a) Using Fig. 1.1, determine:

(i) the deceleration of the skydiver immediately after the parachute opens [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The deceleration is the initial gradient of the speed-time graph. Drawing a tangent at t=0 gives a value in the range of 35 to 43 m/s².

(ii) the force due to air resistance acting on the skydiver immediately after the parachute opens. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

The resultant upward force is Fres = ma. The forces acting are weight (down) and air resistance (up). So, Fair - W = ma, which means Fair = ma + W. Using a deceleration of 40 m/s², Fair = (76 kg × 40 m/s²) + (76 kg × 10 N/kg) = 3040 N + 760 N = 3800 N.

(b) Explain, in terms of the forces acting on the skydiver, his motion between t = 0 and t = 6.0 s. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

The upward force (air resistance) is greater than the weight, causing an upward resultant force and thus deceleration. As speed decreases, air resistance decreases, which reduces the resultant upward force and the deceleration. Eventually, the weight equals the air resistance, the resultant force is zero, and the skydiver reaches a new, slower terminal velocity.

(c) Explain why opening the parachute cannot reduce the speed of the skydiver to zero. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Air resistance depends on speed; at zero speed, there is no air resistance. If the skydiver were to stop, the only force acting would be his weight downwards, which would immediately cause him to accelerate downwards again.

Question 40

(0625/42_Summer_2021_Q1) - Measurements And Units, Forces And Motion

(a) Fig. 1.1 shows a sealed weather balloon which is stationary in still air. State whether the overall density of the balloon and its instruments is greater than, less than, or the same as the density of the surrounding air. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The overall density of the balloon is the same as the density of the surrounding air.

(b) At night, the gas inside the balloon cools. The pressure of the air outside the balloon remains the same.

(i) State whether the balloon rises, falls or remains stationary. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The balloon falls.

(ii) Explain your answer. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

When the gas cools, its volume decreases. Since the mass remains constant, the overall density of the balloon increases. It becomes denser than the surrounding air and therefore sinks.

(c) An object is released from the balloon. It starts at rest and eventually reaches a constant speed.

(i) On the axes of Fig. 1.2, sketch a speed-time graph to show this motion. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

The graph should start at the origin and be a curve with a decreasing gradient that becomes horizontal when it reaches constant speed.

(ii) State the values of the initial acceleration and the final acceleration of the object. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Initial acceleration = 10 m/s² (acceleration of free fall).

Final acceleration = 0 m/s² (at constant terminal velocity).
