Question 1 (0625/41_Summer_2023_Q10) - Space Physics

Pluto is a dwarf planet. Fig. 10.1 shows the direction of motion of Pluto as it follows its elliptical orbit around the Sun.

(a) Point X is the point in the orbit closest to the Sun and point Y is the point furthest away. The orbital speed of Pluto varies as it orbits the Sun.

(i) Describe how the speed of Pluto varies as it moves from X to Y and then back to X. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The speed decreases as Pluto moves from X to Y, and then increases as it moves from Y back to X.

(ii) Explain, in terms of energy transfers, why the speed of Pluto varies in this way. [3]

Tap to reveal answer
  • As Pluto moves from X to Y (away from the Sun), its Kinetic Energy (KE) is transferred to Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE), causing its speed to decrease.
  • As Pluto moves from Y to X (towards the Sun), its GPE is transferred back to KE, causing its speed to increase.
  • The total energy (the sum of GPE and KE) of Pluto remains constant throughout its orbit.

(b) The average temperature on the surface of Pluto is 43 K.

(i) Convert this temperature to a value in degrees Celsius (°C). [1]

Tap to reveal answer

To convert from Kelvin (K) to Celsius (°C), you subtract 273.
Temperature (°C) = 43 - 273
The temperature is -230 °C.

(ii) Pluto has a white surface, as shown in Fig. 10.2. As Pluto rotates, the white surface alternately faces towards and away from the Sun. Explain how this affects the temperature of Pluto as it rotates on its own axis. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

A white surface is a poor absorber and a poor emitter of thermal radiation (or a good reflector). This means it absorbs less heat when facing the Sun and loses less heat when facing away, which results in a smaller variation in temperature as it rotates.

(c) A radiation detector used in a laboratory detects a background count rate of 30 counts/min. A radioactive source is placed in front of the radiation detector. The initial reading on the detector is 550 counts/min. The half-life of the source is 25 minutes. Calculate the expected reading on the detector after 75 minutes. [4]

Tap to reveal answer

1. First, find the initial count rate from the source only:
Initial source count rate = Total count rate - Background count rate = 550 - 30 = 520 counts/min

2. Next, determine the number of half-lives in 75 minutes:
Number of half-lives = Total time⁄Half-life = 75 mins⁄25 mins = 3 half-lives

3. Calculate the source's count rate after 3 half-lives:
Count rate after 3 half-lives = 520⁄2³ = 520⁄8 = 65 counts/min

4. Finally, add the background radiation back to find the total expected reading:
Expected reading = Source count rate + Background count rate = 65 + 30 = 95 counts/min

(d) State two safety precautions taken when moving, using or storing radioactive sources in a laboratory. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Any two of the following:

  • Limit the time of exposure.
  • Store sources in lead-lined boxes.
  • Maintain a safe distance from sources.
  • Handle sources with tongs to avoid direct contact.

(ii) The average density of Jupiter is much less than that of the Earth. The gravitational field strength at the surface of Jupiter is greater than that at the surface of the Earth. Tap to reveal answer

Density: Jupiter has a low density because it is primarily composed of gas, which is less dense than the materials making up the rocky Earth.

Gravitational field strength: Despite its lower density, Jupiter has a much larger volume, giving it a significantly larger mass than Earth. Gravitational field strength is proportional to mass, so Jupiter's large mass results in a stronger gravitational field.

(d) The average density of Jupiter is 1300 kg/m³ and its volume is 1.4 x 10¹⁵ km³. Calculate the mass of Jupiter. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

1. First, convert the volume from km³ to m³:
1 km = 1000 m, so 1 km³ = (1000 m)³ = 10⁹ m³
Volume in m³ = (1.4 x 10¹⁵) x 10⁹ = 1.4 x 10²⁴ m³

2. Use the density formula: density = mass⁄volume, so mass = density x volume
Mass = 1300 kg/m³ x (1.4 x 10²⁴ m³)

3. Calculate the mass:
Mass = 1.8 x 10²⁷ kg

Question 6 (0625/41_Winter_2023_Q10) - Space Physics

The Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies. Each galaxy contains many billions of stable stars.

(a) Stable stars transfer energy into space by emitting electromagnetic radiation from their surfaces. Describe what happens in the core of a stable star to release energy that is eventually transferred into space. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

In the core of a stable star, energy is released through nuclear fusion. In this process, hydrogen nuclei fuse together under immense pressure and temperature to form helium nuclei, releasing a vast amount of energy.

(b) On the Earth, light from a distant galaxy is observed and analysed by astronomers. This information is used to determine the speed at which the galaxy is moving away from the Earth.

(i) Describe how the observed light is different from when it was emitted. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The observed light from a galaxy moving away from us has a longer wavelength than when it was emitted. This phenomenon is known as redshift, as the light is shifted towards the red end of the electromagnetic spectrum.

(ii) State the quantity that astronomers use to determine the speed at which the galaxy is moving away. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

Astronomers use the change in wavelength of the starlight (the amount of redshift) to determine the galaxy's recessional speed.

(c) The Hubble constant H₀ is equal to 2.2 x 10⁻¹⁸ per second.

(i) Calculate the distance from the Earth of a galaxy that is moving away at a speed of 1.3 x 10⁷ m/s. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

1. Use Hubble's Law: v = H₀d, rearranged to d = v⁄H₀

2. Substitute the values:
d = (1.3 x 10⁷ m/s)⁄(2.2 x 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹)

3. Calculate the distance:
d = 5.9 x 10²⁴ m

(ii) Calculate an estimate for the age of the Universe. Give your answer in years. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

1. The age of the Universe can be estimated as the reciprocal of the Hubble constant: Age ≈ 1⁄H₀
Age in seconds = 1⁄(2.2 x 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹) = 4.545 x 10¹⁷ s

2. Convert seconds to years:
Number of seconds in a year ≈ 3.15 x 10⁷
Age in years = (4.545 x 10¹⁷ s)⁄(3.15 x 10⁷ s/year) ≈ 1.4 x 10¹⁰ years (14 billion years)
