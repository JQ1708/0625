Question 1 (0625/41_Summer_2017_Q8) - Magnets And Currents

A bar magnet is made of metal.

(a) Suggest a metal from which the bar magnet is made. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The bar magnet is made from a hard magnetic material such as steel.

(b) Fig. 8.1 shows the bar magnet being inserted into a coil of wire. The N-pole and the S-pole of the bar magnet are marked.

The coil is connected to a galvanometer.

(i) Explain why the galvanometer deflects as the bar magnet is being inserted into the coil. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

As the magnet moves, its magnetic field lines are cut by the coil. This change in magnetic flux linkage induces an electromotive force (e.m.f.) across the coil, causing a current to flow and the galvanometer to deflect.

(ii) Explain what determines the direction of the reading on the galvanometer. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The direction of the induced e.m.f. is determined by the direction of movement of the magnet and which pole of the magnet enters the coil. The induced e.m.f. will create a magnetic field that opposes the change causing it.

(c) Describe a method for demagnetising a bar magnet. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

One method is to place the magnet inside a coil carrying an alternating current (a.c.). Then, with the current still on, the magnet is slowly removed from the coil to a great distance, or the current is slowly decreased to zero.

Question 2 (0625/42_Summer_2017_Q9) - Magnets And Currents

Fig. 9.1 shows a horizontal wire PQ placed in the gap between the N pole and the S pole of a magnet.

There is a current in the wire in the direction P to Q. A force acts on the current-carrying wire in the magnetic field.

(a) On Fig. 9.1, draw

(i) an arrow, labelled M to show the direction of the magnetic field in the gap between the poles of the magnet, [1]

(ii) an arrow, labelled F to show the direction of the force on the current-carrying wire due to the magnetic field of the magnet. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

(i) The arrow M should be drawn from the N pole to the S pole, pointing horizontally from left to right.

(ii) Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, the arrow F should be drawn pointing downwards.

(b) State the effect of reversing the direction of the current in wire PQ. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The direction of the force on the wire will be reversed; it will now act upwards.

(c) The magnet is removed and the horizontal, current-carrying wire is left on its own, as shown in Fig. 9.2.

(ii) The current in PQ is increased. State the effect of this change in current on the magnetic field. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The magnetic field becomes stronger, and the field lines become closer together.

Tap to reveal answer

The force on an S pole is opposite to the direction of the magnetic field, so the force will be directed vertically downwards.

Question 3 (0625/43_Summer_2017_Q10) - Magnets And Currents

Fig. 10.1 shows a transformer that consists of two coils P and Q, and an iron core.

There are 200 turns on coil P and 340 turns on coil Q. A 4.0V a.c. power supply is connected to coil P.

(a) (i) Explain why there is a voltage between the two terminals of coil Q. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

The alternating current in coil P produces a changing magnetic field in the iron core. This changing field is linked to coil Q, and through electromagnetic induction, a voltage is induced in coil Q.

(ii) Explain why the core of the transformer is made of soft iron. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

Soft iron is used because it is an easily magnetised and demagnetised material, making it a temporary magnet that can efficiently transfer the changing magnetic field.

(b) (i) Calculate the voltage between the two terminals of coil Q. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Vs ⁄ Vp = Ns ⁄ Np

Vs = Vp × (Ns ⁄ Np) = 4.0V × (340 ⁄ 200) = 6.8V

The voltage is 6.8 V.

(ii) A heater is connected to coil Q. The current in the heater is 3.5A. The transformer is 100% efficient. Calculate the current in coil P. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

IpVp = IsVs

Ip = (IsVs) ⁄ Vp = (3.5A × 6.8V) ⁄ 4.0V = 5.95A

The current is 6.0 A.

(c) A transformer is used to step up the voltage before electrical energy is transmitted over long distances. State and explain one economic effect of transmitting electrical energy at a high voltage. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Transmitting at a high voltage allows for a lower current, which means less energy is wasted as heat in the cables. This makes the system more efficient and cheaper to run, as less fuel is required at the power station, and thinner, less expensive cables can be used.

(a) Fig. 9.1 shows the structure of an alternating current (a.c.) generator.

The coil completes one rotation every 0.020s.

(i) Using the axes in Fig. 9.2, sketch a graph to show how the voltage output of the generator varies with time during a period of 0.040s. [2]

(ii) On your graph in Fig. 9.2, mark a point labelled A to indicate a time when the coil is vertical. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

(i) The graph should be a sine or cosine curve with a period of 0.020s, showing two complete cycles within the 0.040s timeframe. The maximum and minimum voltage values should be equal in magnitude.

(ii) Point A should be marked where the output voltage is zero, as this occurs when the coil is vertical and moving parallel to the magnetic field.

(b) There is an alternating current (a.c.) in a horizontal wire that is buried in a wall. A builder must miss this wire when drilling a hole in the wall. The builder places an instrument against the wall that registers a reading when it is close to the wire. The instrument includes a long coil (solenoid) S that has an iron core and a sensitive voltmeter. Fig. 9.3 shows the circuit of the instrument close to the wire.

(i) Explain why there is a reading on the voltmeter. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

The alternating current in the buried wire creates a changing magnetic field around it. This changing field cuts through the solenoid S, inducing an e.m.f. (voltage) in the coil, which is detected by the voltmeter.

(ii) State the name and the effect of the component labelled X in Fig. 9.3. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Component X is a diode. Its effect is to rectify the alternating current, meaning it allows current to flow in only one direction.

The handle of the brush contains the battery and a coil X. The circuit from coil X to the battery is not shown. The base of the charger contains a coil Y, wound on an iron core, connected to the a.c. mains supply. To charge the battery, the handle is lowered so that coil Y is inside coil X. The alternating current in coil Y sets up an alternating magnetic field. This changing magnetic flux cuts through or links with coil X. By electromagnetic induction, an alternating voltage is induced in coil X.

Question 6 (0625/42_Winter_2017_Q10) - Magnets And Currents

(a) Fig. 10.1 shows a wire that carries a current into the page. The circles on Fig. 10.1 show the pattern of the magnetic field around the wire.

(i) On Fig. 10.1, draw an arrow on each circle to show the direction of the magnetic field. [1]

(ii) State why the spacing of the circles increases as the distance from the wire increases. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

(i) Using the right-hand grip rule, the arrows on the circles should point in a clockwise direction.

(ii) The spacing increases because the magnetic field becomes weaker with increasing distance from the wire.

(b) Fig. 10.2 shows an electric door lock. The slot in the door contains an unmagnetised iron bolt attached to a spring. The slot in the door frame is empty. This slot is surrounded by the coils of a solenoid. In Fig. 10.2 the door is unlocked. The spring is not stretched.

In Fig. 10.3 the door is locked. The spring is now stretched.

The bolt is initially in the position shown in Fig. 10.2. Describe and explain what happens when

(i) the switch S is closed, [4]

Tap to reveal answer

When the switch is closed, a current flows in the solenoid, turning it into an electromagnet. This magnetises the iron bolt. The solenoid attracts the bolt, causing it to move into the solenoid, stretching the spring and locking the door.

(ii) the switch S is reopened. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

When the switch is opened, the current stops, and the solenoid and bolt are no longer magnetised. The stretched spring contracts and pulls the bolt back to its original position, unlocking the door.

Question 7 (0625/41_Summer_2018_Q9) - Magnets And Currents

(a) A student wants to demagnetise a permanent bar magnet. She suggests these steps:

  1. Place the magnet in a long coil.
  2. Switch on a large alternating current in the coil.
  3. Switch off the current.
  4. Remove the bar from the coil.

State and explain whether the steps will always be able to demagnetise the magnet. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

No, this method will not be effective. The magnet should be withdrawn from the coil while the alternating current is still on. Switching the current off while the magnet is inside will leave it magnetised in the direction of the field at the instant the current was switched off.

(b) (i) Fig. 9.1 shows a coil supplied with current using a split-ring commutator.

State and explain any motion of the coil. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

The coil will turn continuously in a clockwise direction. This is due to the force on the current in the magnetic field. The split-ring commutator reverses the direction of the current in the coil every half turn, ensuring the turning force remains in the same direction.

(b) Fig. 9.2 shows a magnet held just below a vertical coil connected to a galvanometer.

The magnet is released.

(i) State any effect on the galvanometer. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The galvanometer needle deflects momentarily and then returns to zero as the magnet passes through and falls away.

(ii) State any effect on the magnetic field produced by the coil. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The induced current in the coil produces its own magnetic field. As the N pole approaches, an N pole is induced at the top of the coil to oppose the motion. As it falls away, an S pole is induced at the bottom of the coil to oppose the motion.
