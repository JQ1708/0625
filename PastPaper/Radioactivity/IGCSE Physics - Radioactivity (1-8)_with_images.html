(a) A radioactive source is tested over a number of hours with a radiation detector. The readings are shown in Table 11.1.

Use the readings to suggest a value for the background count rate during the test, and to determine the half-life of the sample. [4]

(b) Hydrogen-3 (tritium) has one proton and two neutrons. The nucleon number of tritium is three. It decays by emitting a β-particle. Complete the nuclide equation to show this decay. The symbol X represents the nuclide produced by this decay. [3]

...H → ...X + ...β

(c) The arrows in Fig. 11.1 show the paths of three α-particles moving towards gold nuclei in a thin foil. On Fig. 11.1, complete the paths of the three α-particles. [3]

(a) The readings level off at an average of around 19-20 counts/s; this is the background count rate. The initial count rate is 324 counts/s. After subtracting the background, the initial sample rate is approx 304 counts/s. After one half-life, the rate would be 152 counts/s. Adding the background, this is 172 counts/s. This does not align perfectly with the table, however, using the mark scheme's logic of halving the corrected reading (324-20=304; 304/2=152) and adding background back (152+20=172) shows the half-life is approximately 1.0 hour.

(b) The completed equation is: 31H → 32He + 0-1β (where X is Helium, He).

(c) The alpha particle heading directly for a nucleus is repelled and deflected back to the left. The one passing close by is deflected on a curved path away from the nucleus. The one passing far away continues undeflected.

Question 2 (0625/42_Summer_2017_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) The arrows in Fig. 11.1 represent the paths of three α-particles moving towards gold nuclei in a thin foil. The gold nuclei are shown as shaded circles. On Fig. 11.1, complete the paths of the three α-particles. [3]

(b) Fig. 11.2 shows a geologist holding a radiation detector near a rock. She holds the detector in a fixed position and records the readings shown in Table 11.1. Explain the changes in the detector readings. [2]

(c) A technician is handling a solid radioactive sample that emits α-particles and β-particles. The technician wears thick rubber gloves. Explain why this may provide some protection from the radiation, but it is not sufficient protection. [2]

(a) The path of the alpha particle that is aimed directly at the nucleus will be deflected at a large angle to the left. The path of the alpha particle passing close to the nucleus will be deflected on a downward curve. The path of the alpha particle passing far from the nucleus will be undeflected.

(b) The readings fluctuate because radioactive decay is a random process. The detector is measuring the background radiation from sources like the rock, which varies naturally.

(c) The thick gloves will stop the α-particles as they have low penetrating power, providing some protection. However, the gloves are not sufficient protection because the more penetrating β-particles can pass through and reach the technician's skin or other body parts.

Question 3 (0625/43_Summer_2017_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) Fig. 11.1 shows equipment that is used to investigate the effect of a magnetic field on the path of a beam of γ-rays.

(i) Suggest the purpose of the two lead plates. [1]

(ii) A magnetic field, directed into the page, is set up in the shaded region. State and explain what happens to the reading of the detector. [3]

(b) State the relative ionising effects of α-particles, β-particles and γ-rays. Suggest an explanation for the differences. [3]

(a)(i) The lead plates are used to absorb stray radiation and produce a narrow beam (collimate) of γ-rays.

(a)(ii) The reading of the detector shows no change. This is because γ-rays are a form of electromagnetic radiation and have no charge, so they are not deflected by a magnetic field.

(b) Ionising effect: α-particles > β-particles > γ-rays. The difference is due to variations in their properties. Alpha particles are the most ionising because they have the largest charge (+2e) and mass, and a lower speed, causing more frequent and intense interactions with atoms over a shorter distance.

Question 4 (0625/43_Winter_2017_Q10) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) State the nature of γ-rays. [1]

(b) A nucleus of technetium-99 emits only a γ-ray. State any effect of this on (i) the proton number of the nucleus, (ii) the nucleon number of the nucleus. [2]

(c) In a laboratory a radiation detector displays a count rate of 16 counts/minute due to background radiation.

(i) State what is meant by background radiation. [1]

(ii) A sample of a radioactive isotope is placed near to the radiation detector and a count rate of 112 counts/minute is recorded. After 18 hours, the count rate recorded is 28 counts/minute. Determine the half-life of this isotope. [3]

(d) Radioactive isotopes are stored in thick lead containers. State two precautions to be taken when radioactive isotopes are used. [2]

(a) γ-rays are high-energy, high-frequency electromagnetic waves.

(b) (i) There is no change in the proton number. (ii) There is no change in the nucleon number.

(c)(i) Background radiation is the low-level ionising radiation that is always present in the environment from natural and man-made sources.

(c)(ii) Corrected initial count rate = 112 - 16 = 96. Corrected final count rate = 28 - 16 = 12. The count rate has dropped from 96 to 12, which is a factor of 8 (96 → 48 → 24 → 12). This represents 3 half-lives. Total time is 18 hours, so the half-life is 18 / 3 = 6.0 hours.

(d) Two precautions are: 1. Handle with tongs to increase distance. 2. Limit exposure time.

Question 5 (0625/41_Winter_2017_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

The radioactive isotope carbon-14 emits β-particles as it decays.

(a) The decay of carbon-14 produces an isotope of nitrogen (N).

(i) State the nature of a β-particle and state where it is produced. [2]

(ii) Complete the nuclide equation for the radioactive decay of carbon-14. [2]

(b) The half-life of carbon-14 is 5700 years. Explain what is meant by the term half-life. [1]

(c) A workman operates a machine that uses β-particles to determine the level of liquid in a plastic water bottle that is being filled. Suggest why (i) α-particles are not suitable for the same purpose, (ii) γ-rays are not suitable for the same purpose. [2]

(a)(i) A β-particle is a high-speed electron that is produced in and emitted from the nucleus when a neutron decays into a proton and an electron.

(a)(ii) The completed equation is: 146C → 147N + 0-1β.

(b) Half-life is the average time taken for the number of radioactive nuclei in a sample to halve (or for the activity/count rate to halve).

(c)(i) α-particles have very low penetrating power and would be stopped (absorbed) by the plastic bottle itself.

(c)(ii) γ-rays are very highly penetrating and would pass through both the bottle and the liquid with little absorption, so there would be no significant change in the detector reading.

Question 6 (0625/42_Winter_2017_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

The radioactive isotope bismuth-210 (210Bi) decays by β-particle emission to an isotope of polonium (Po).

(a) Complete the nuclide equation that represents this decay. [3]

21083Bi → ...Po + ...β

(b) A radiation detector is placed on a bench in a laboratory where there are no artificial sources of radiation. The detector is switched on. In seven one-minute periods, the detector displays these readings: 24, 22, 25, 25, 21, 20, 24.

(i) Explain why, in the absence of any artificial source, there are readings on the detector. Suggest one origin of this effect. [2]

(ii) Explain why the readings obtained are not all the same. [1]

(iii) The half-life of bismuth-210 is 5.0 days. A sample of bismuth-210 is brought close to the detector and in one minute, the reading displayed is 487. The equipment is left in the same place for exactly 10 days. Predict the reading in a one-minute period at the end of this time. [3]

(a) The equation is: 21083Bi → 21084Po + 0-1β.

(b)(i) The detector shows readings because of background radiation which is always present in the environment. One origin is rocks in the Earth's crust (or cosmic rays).

(b)(ii) The readings vary because radioactive decay is a random process.

(b)(iii) The average background count is about 23 counts/min. The initial sample count rate is 487 - 23 = 464 counts/min. 10 days is two half-lives (10 / 5 = 2). After 10 days, the sample count rate will be 464 / 4 = 116 counts/min. The final detector reading will be the sample rate plus the background rate: 116 + 23 = 139 counts/min.

Question 7 (0625/41_Summer_2018_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

Radon-222 is radioactive. It can be represented as 22286Rn.

(a) For a neutral atom of radon-222, state 1. the number of protons, 2. the number of neutrons, 3. the number of electrons. [3]

(b) A radon-222 nucleus decays by α-particle emission to a polonium (Po) nucleus. Complete the equation for the decay of radon-222. [2]

22286Rn → ...Po + ...α

(c) Radon-222 has a half-life of 3.8 days. At a certain time, a sample contains 6.4 × 106 radon nuclei. Calculate the number of α-particles emitted by the radon nuclei in the following 7.6 days. [3]

(a) 1. number of protons = 86. 2. number of neutrons = 222 - 86 = 136. 3. number of electrons = 86.

(b) The equation is: 22286Rn → 21884Po + 42α.

(c) 7.6 days is two half-lives (7.6 / 3.8 = 2). After two half-lives, the number of radon nuclei remaining is 6.4 × 106 / 4 = 1.6 × 106. The number of nuclei that have decayed (and therefore the number of α-particles emitted) is the initial number minus the final number: 6.4 × 106 - 1.6 × 106 = 4.8 × 106.

Question 8 (0625/42_Summer_2018_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) A radioactive nucleus of uranium-235 decays to a nucleus of thorium and emits an α-particle. Complete the equation. [2]

23592U → ...Th + 42α

(b) A nucleus of uranium-235 undergoes nuclear fission in a reactor.

(i) State what is meant by nuclear fission. [1]

(ii) Suggest why a nuclear reactor is surrounded by thick concrete walls. [2]

(iii) State one environmental advantage and one environmental disadvantage of using a fission reactor to generate electrical energy in a power station. [2]

(c) The thorium produced by the decay in (a) is also radioactive and has a half-life of 26 hours. At a certain time, a pure sample of this isotope initially contains 4.8 × 109 atoms. Calculate the number of atoms of this sample that decay in the following 52 hours. [3]

(a) The completed equation is: 23592U → 23190Th + 42α.

(b)(i) Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus into two (or more) smaller nuclei, releasing energy.

(b)(ii) The thick concrete walls act as shielding to absorb the dangerous ionising radiation produced during fission, protecting the workers and the environment.

(b)(iii) Advantage: It does not produce greenhouse gases like CO₂. Disadvantage: It produces highly radioactive nuclear waste which is difficult and expensive to store safely.

(c) 52 hours is two half-lives (52 / 26 = 2). After two half-lives, 1/4 of the atoms will remain. The fraction that has decayed is 3/4. The number of atoms that have decayed is (3/4) × 4.8 × 109 = 3.6 × 109.
