Question 9 (0625/43_Summer_2018_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) State the type of radioactive emission that causes

(i) the proton number of a nuclide to increase by 1, [1]

(ii) the nucleon number of a nuclide to decrease by 4, [1]

(iii) no change in the proton number and no change in the nucleon number of a nuclide. [1]

(b) The isotope radon-220 is radioactive and it decays by α-particle emission.

(i) Fig. 11.1 shows a beam of α-particles entering the electric field between two charged plates. On Fig. 11.1, sketch the path that the beam of α-particles follows in the electric field. [1]

(ii) The half-life of radon-220 is 56 s. A sample of this isotope contains 7.2 × 106 atoms. Predict the number of α-particles that the radon-220 in the sample emits in the next 168 s. [3]

(a)(i) This is caused by β-particle emission.

(a)(ii) This is caused by α-particle emission.

(a)(iii) This is caused by γ-ray emission.

(b)(i) The positively charged α-particles will be repelled by the positive plate and attracted to the negative plate, following a downward curve.

(b)(ii) 168 s is three half-lives (168 / 56 = 3). After 3 half-lives, the number of atoms remaining is 7.2 × 106 / 8 = 0.9 × 106. The number of atoms that have decayed (and α-particles emitted) is 7.2 × 106 - 0.9 × 106 = 6.3 × 106.

Question 10 (0625/42_Winter_2018_Q10) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) A detector of ionising radiation measures the background count rate in a classroom where there are no radioactive samples present. The readings, in counts/minute, taken over a period of time are shown in Table 10.1.

(i) State two possible sources of this background radiation. [2]

(ii) Explain why the readings are not the same. [1]

(b) With no radioactive sample present, a scientist records a background radiation count of 40 counts/minute. He brings a radioactive sample close to the detector. The count rate increases to 200 counts/minute. After 24 days the count rate is 50 counts/minute. Calculate the half-life of the radioactive sample. [4]

(c) Draw a line between each type of ionising radiation and its property and another line between the property and its use. One has been done for you. [3]

(a)(i) Two sources are cosmic rays from space and radioactive rocks/soil/buildings.

(a)(ii) The readings vary because radioactive decay is a random process.

(b) Corrected initial count rate = 200 - 40 = 160. Corrected final count rate = 50 - 40 = 10. The count rate has dropped from 160 to 10, a factor of 16. Since 24 = 16, this represents 4 half-lives. The total time is 24 days, so the half-life is 24 / 4 = 6 days.

(c) Matching lines: α-particle → It is the most ionising radiation... → Detecting smoke in a fire alarm system. β-particle → Penetration is affected by small changes in the amount of solid... → [No matching use given]. γ-ray → It is highly penetrating... → Remotely detecting leaks in underground water pipes. X-ray → Can pass easily through soft living tissue... → Detecting fractures in bones.

Question 11 (0625/43_Winter_2018_Q10) - Atoms And Radioactivity

Thorium-234 (234Th) is radioactive. It decays by β-emission to form an isotope of protactinium (Pa).

(a) Complete the nuclide equation for this decay. [2]

23490Th → ...Pa + ...β

(b) A pure sample of thorium-234 emits β-particles at a count rate of 2480 counts/second. The half-life of thorium-234 is 24 days. Calculate the count rate for the emission of β-particles from the thorium in the sample after 72 days have passed. [3]

(c) The isotope of protactinium in (a) is also radioactive. It decays by β-emission and has a half-life of 70 seconds. State and explain how this would affect the observed count rate for the sample in (b) after 72 days. [3]

(a) The completed equation is: 23490Th → 23491Pa + 0-1β.

(b) 72 days is three half-lives (72 / 24 = 3). The count rate after 3 half-lives will be 2480 / 23 = 2480 / 8 = 310 counts/second.

(c) The observed count rate would be larger than 310 counts/second. This is because the protactinium that is formed is also radioactive and emits its own β-particles, which would be detected in addition to those from the thorium.

Question 12 (0625/41_Winter_2018_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) Fig. 11.1 shows data about nine elements. Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope with a nucleon number of 14. It decays by emitting β-particles. Use any data you need from Fig. 11.1 to write down the nuclide equation for this decay. [4]

(b) A radioactive sample is placed close to a detector. The radioactive isotope in the sample has a long half-life. The detector records a count rate of 597 counts/s. Fig. 11.2 shows the readings when different materials are placed between the radioactive sample and the detector. Explain whether any α-particles, β-particles or γ-rays are emitted by the radioactive sample. [3]

(a) From Fig 11.1, Carbon (C) has proton number 6. Beta decay increases the proton number by 1 to 7, which is Nitrogen (N). The equation is: 146C → 147N + 0-1β.

(b) α-particles: No. If they were present, the count rate would decrease significantly with paper, but it increases slightly (within random variation). β-particles: No. If they were present, the count rate would decrease significantly with aluminium, but it barely changes. γ-rays: Yes. The count rate is only reduced significantly by the lead, which shows a highly penetrating radiation is present.

Question 13 (0625/41_Summer_2019_Q9) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) Fig. 9.1 shows a beam of α-particles moving towards a thin sheet of gold in a vacuum. Detectors in the region surrounding the thin gold sheet detect the α-particles and determine the number of particles that travel in various directions. State and explain what can be deduced from the following observations.

(i) The majority of the α-particles pass through the gold sheet undeflected. [2]

(ii) A small number of α-particles are deflected as they pass through the gold sheet. [2]

(iii) A very small number of α-particles are deflected through very large angles or return back the way they came. [2]

(b) A beam that consists of both α-particles and β-particles is passed through a region of space where there is a magnetic field perpendicular to the direction of the beam. State two ways in which the deflection of the α-particles differs from that of the β-particles. [2]

(a)(i) Deduction: Most of the atom is empty space. Explanation: Very few α-particles pass close enough to a nucleus to be affected by its force.

(a)(ii) Deduction: The nucleus is positively charged. Explanation: The positive α-particles experience a force of repulsion from the positive nucleus.

(a)(iii) Deduction: The nucleus is very dense and massive (contains most of the atom's mass). Explanation: Only a very dense, massive object could cause such a large change in momentum of the α-particle.

(b) 1. The α-particles and β-particles are deflected in opposite directions because they have opposite charges. 2. The β-particles are deflected much more than the α-particles because they have a much smaller mass.

Question 14 (0625/42_Summer_2019_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) A radon-222 nucleus contains 86 protons and 136 neutrons. It decays by emitting an α-particle and becomes a nucleus of an isotope of polonium. The symbol for radon is Rn and the symbol for polonium is Po. Write down the nuclide equation for this decay. [3]

(b) Carbon-14 is radioactive with a half-life of 5700 years. An animal bone is dug up in an archaeological excavation. The quantity of carbon-14 in the bone is 25% of what it was when the bone was buried. Calculate the time that has elapsed since it was buried. [2]

(a) The completed equation is: 22286Rn → 21884Po + 42α.

(b) A quantity of 25% remaining means the sample has undergone two half-lives (100% → 50% → 25%). The correct calculation for the time elapsed is 2 × 5700 years = 11,400 years. The official mark scheme states 11,000 years, which is likely an approximation or error.

Question 15 (0625/43_Summer_2019_Q11) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) Americium (Am) is a radioactive isotope. A nucleus of americium contains 95 protons and 146 neutrons. It decays by emitting an α-particle to form a nucleus of an isotope of neptunium (Np). Write down the nuclide equation for the decay of americium to neptunium. [4]

(b) Ionisation smoke detectors contain americium and two small electrodes with a small voltage between them. The air between the electrodes is ionised by α-particles so that there is a small electric current between the electrodes.

(i) Suggest and explain the effect of smoke on the current between the electrodes in the smoke detector. [1]

(ii) Suggest two reasons for using an α-particle emitter in a smoke detector. [2]

(a) The americium isotope is 24195Am. The equation is: 24195Am → 23793Np + 42α.

(b)(i) Suggestion: The current decreases. Explanation: Smoke particles will absorb the α-particles, reducing the ionisation of the air and therefore reducing the flow of charge (current).

(b)(ii) Reason 1: α-particles are highly ionising, which is needed to create a sufficient current in the air gap. Reason 2: They have a short range in air and are stopped by the plastic casing, making them safe to use in a home appliance.

Question 16 (0625/41_Winter_2019_Q9) - Atoms And Radioactivity

(a) The chemical symbol of the element lithium is Li. The proton number of lithium is 3. Fig. 9.1 is a representation of a nucleus of a radioactive isotope of lithium that is about to decay.

(i) Write down, using nuclide notation, the symbol that represents this isotope of lithium. [1]

(ii) This isotope of lithium decays by β-particle emission to form another nucleus. Complete Fig. 9.2 to represent this decay by: drawing a diagram of the nucleus formed by the decay, and writing the name of the particle that is identical to a β-particle. [3]

(b) A radiation detector is set up in a laboratory where there are no radioactive samples. On six separate occasions, the detector is switched on for 1.0 minute and the background count is recorded. The counts are: 23, 27, 25, 24, 20, 25.

(i) State why the readings are not all identical. [1]

(ii) Suggest a possible source for this background radiation. [1]

(iii) A sample containing only one radioactive isotope is brought into the laboratory. The half-life of the isotope is 15 hours. The sample is placed near to the radiation detector in this laboratory. The detector is switched on and, after 1.0 minute, a count of 440 is recorded. The sample is left next to the detector and the experiment is repeated 45 hours later. Predict the reading for the count obtained on this occasion. [4]

(a)(i) The diagram shows 3 protons and 5 neutrons. The symbol is 83Li.

(a)(ii) In β-decay, a neutron becomes a proton. The new nucleus will have 4 protons and 4 neutrons. The particle emitted is an electron.

(b)(i) The readings are not identical because radioactive decay is a random process.

(b)(ii) A possible source is radon gas in the air.

(b)(iii) The average background count is about 24. The initial corrected count rate is 440 - 24 = 416. 45 hours is three half-lives (45 / 15 = 3). After 3 half-lives, the sample count rate will be 416 / 8 = 52. The predicted detector reading is 52 + 24 = 76 counts.
