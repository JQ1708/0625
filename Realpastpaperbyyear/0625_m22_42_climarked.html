Question 1 Paper: 0625/42/F/M/22 Topics: Motion, Speed-Time Graphs

A ball rolls down a ramp and onto a horizontal surface. The first section of the horizontal surface is smooth. The second section of the horizontal surface is rough. Fig. 1.1 shows a speed-time graph for the ball.

(a) State the time when the ball reaches the start of the rough section of the horizontal surface. [1]

time = ........................................................... [1]

The ball reaches the start of the rough section at 2.2 s. This is the point where the graph line begins to curve downwards, indicating deceleration due to friction.

Recap

A speed-time graph shows how an object's speed changes over time. A horizontal line (zero gradient) indicates constant speed (zero acceleration). A straight, sloped line indicates constant acceleration (or deceleration). A curved line indicates non-uniform (changing) acceleration.

(b) Explain how Fig. 1.1 shows that there is no resultant force on the ball when it rolls along the smooth section of the horizontal surface. [2]

The smooth section is represented by the horizontal line on the graph (between 1.5 s and 2.2 s). A horizontal line has a gradient of zero, which means the acceleration is zero and the speed is constant. According to Newton's First Law, if the acceleration is zero, the resultant force acting on the ball must also be zero.

Recap

An object remains at rest or continues in a straight line with constant speed, unless a resultant force acts on it. This property of an object to resist changes in its motion is called inertia. Constant speed (or zero speed) means there is no resultant force.

(c) Using Fig. 1.1, determine the acceleration of the ball as it rolls down the ramp. [3]

acceleration = ..................................................... [3]

Acceleration is calculated from the gradient of the speed-time graph. The ramp section is from t=0 to t=1.5 s.

a = Δv / Δt = (change in speed) / (time taken)
a = (12.8 m/s - 0 m/s) / (1.5 s - 0 s)
a = 12.8 / 1.5
a = 8.53... m/s²

acceleration = 8.5 m/s² (to 2 significant figures)

Recap

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, calculated using a = (v - u) / t. On a speed-time graph, acceleration is found by calculating the gradient (rise over run) of the line.

(d) The ball starts from rest at the top of the ramp.

Show that the length of the ramp is 9.6 m. [2]

The distance travelled (length of the ramp) is equal to the area under the graph for that section (0 s to 1.5 s). This section is a triangle.

Area = ½ × base × height
Area = 0.5 × 1.5 s × 12.8 m/s
Area = 9.6 m

Recap

The distance travelled by an object is represented by the area under its speed-time graph. This area can be found by splitting the graph into simple shapes like triangles (Area = ½ × b × h) and rectangles (Area = b × h).

Question 2 Paper: 0625/42/F/M/22 Topics: Forces, Hooke's Law, GPE

Fig. 2.1 shows a spring balance used to measure the weight of a baby. The spring inside the balance extends when a mass is suspended from it. The dial shows the extension of spring as a value of mass in kg.

The spring obeys Hooke's law up to a weight of 175 N.

(a) (i) State Hooke's law. [1]

Hooke's law states that the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force (or load) applied to it, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

Recap

The relationship for Hooke's Law is given by the equation F = kx, where F is the force, x is the extension (change in length), and k is the spring constant. An object that obeys this is said to be elastic. If stretched beyond its elastic limit, it will be permanently deformed.

(a) (ii) State the relationship between the mass of the baby and the force exerted on the spring due to the baby. [1]

The force exerted on the spring (the baby's weight) is directly proportional to the mass of the baby. This relationship is given by the equation W = mg.

Recap

Mass (measured in kg) is a measure of the amount of matter in an object and its inertia. Weight (measured in N) is the force of gravity acting on that mass. The gravitational field strength (g) links them (W = mg). On Earth, g ≈ 10 N/kg.

(a) (iii) The reading on the spring balance is 8.0 kg. Determine the force exerted on the spring due to the baby. [1]

force = ........................................................... [1]

Using the relationship Weight = mass × g:
Force = 8.0 kg × 10 N/kg
Force = 80 N

Recap

To calculate the weight (a force) from a given mass, you must multiply by the gravitational field strength, g. The value of g on Earth is taken as 10 N/kg (or 10 m/s²) in IGCSE.

(b) The limit of proportionality for the spring is at a force of 175 N. Sketch the extension-load graph for the spring. The sketch must continue beyond a force of 175 N. [2]

The graph should show two distinct parts:
1. A straight line starting from the origin (0,0) with a positive gradient, extending up to a load of 175 N.
2. After 175 N, the line should become a smooth curve with a gradient that increases (i.e., it bends upwards, showing more extension for each additional unit of load).

Recap

An extension-load graph shows extension on the y-axis and load (force) on the x-axis. The gradient of the straight-line portion (x/F) is related to the spring constant (k = 1/gradient). The point where the graph stops being straight is the limit of proportionality.

(c) The baby is carried from the ground floor to the bedroom. The vertical height of the bedroom above the ground floor is 3.5 m. Calculate the change in gravitational potential energy of the baby when it is carried from the ground floor to the bedroom. [2]

change in gravitational potential energy = ..................................................... [2]

Change in Gravitational Potential Energy (ΔGPE) = m × g × Δh
From part (a)(iii), the baby's weight (mg) is 80 N.
ΔGPE = (mg) × Δh
ΔGPE = 80 N × 3.5 m
ΔGPE = 280 J

Recap

The gravitational potential energy (GPE) of an object is its stored energy due to its position in a gravitational field. The change in GPE (ΔEp) is calculated as mass × g × change in height (mgΔh). The unit of energy is the joule (J).

Question 3 Paper: 0625/42/F/M/22 Topics: Thermal Physics, Evaporation

Fig. 3.1 and Fig. 3.2 show how a puddle of water changes on a warm windy day.

(a) Describe the process by which the volume of water in the puddle decreases. [2]

This process is evaporation. The water molecules are in constant, random motion. Some molecules, particularly those at the surface of the water, are more energetic than average. If these fast-moving molecules have enough kinetic energy, they can overcome the attractive forces of the other liquid molecules and escape into the air as water vapour (a gas).

Recap

Evaporation is the change of state from a liquid to a gas that occurs at the surface of the liquid and at any temperature below the boiling point. This is different from boiling, which occurs throughout the liquid at a specific boiling point.

(b) State and explain one change in the weather that would cause the volume of water in the puddle to decrease more slowly. [2]

statement ..............................................................................................................................................

explanation .........................................................................................................................................

Statement: Lower temperatures (or a colder day).

Explanation: At a lower temperature, the water molecules have less kinetic energy on average. This means fewer molecules at the surface will have enough energy to escape, so the rate of evaporation decreases.

(Alternative answer: Statement: Less windy weather. Explanation: Wind normally blows away water vapour from above the surface, allowing more water to evaporate. If it is less windy, the air above the puddle becomes more humid (saturated), which slows down the rate of net evaporation.)

Recap

The rate of evaporation is affected by several factors. It increases with:
1. Higher temperature (more energy for molecules)
2. Increased surface area (more molecules exposed)
3. Increased air movement (or draught) over the surface (removes vapour)

(c) Explain, in terms of molecules, how sweating helps to cool your body on a hot day. [3]

Thermal energy from your skin (body) is transferred to the molecules of sweat (liquid water) on the skin's surface. This energy increases the kinetic energy of the sweat molecules. The most energetic molecules escape from the skin (evaporate) and become water vapour. As these high-energy molecules leave, they take the thermal energy with them, lowering the average kinetic energy of the molecules left behind. This lowers the temperature of the remaining sweat and the skin, cooling the body.

Recap

Evaporation causes cooling. This is a crucial concept. Because the most energetic particles are the ones that leave, the average kinetic energy of the remaining particles decreases. Since temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles, the temperature of the liquid decreases.

Question 4 Paper: 0625/42/F/M/22 Topics: Density, Thermal Capacity, Conduction

(a) A sample of sand has a volume of 0.050 m³. The density of the sand is 1900 kg/m³. The specific heat capacity of the sand is 1500 J/(kg °C).

(i) Calculate the mass of the sample of sand. [2]

mass = ........................................................... [2]

Density = mass / volume (ρ = m / V)
So, mass = density × volume
mass = 1900 kg/m³ × 0.050 m³
mass = 95 kg

Recap

Density (ρ) is defined as the mass (m) per unit volume (V). The formula is ρ = m / V. The standard SI unit for density is kg/m³.

(ii) Calculate the thermal capacity of the sample of sand. [2]

thermal capacity = .............................................. [2]

Thermal capacity (C) = mass (m) × specific heat capacity (c)
C = 95 kg × 1500 J/(kg °C)
C = 142 500 J/°C or 1.4 × 10⁵ J/°C

Recap

Specific Heat Capacity (c) is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. Thermal Capacity (C) is the energy needed to raise the temperature of the entire object by 1 °C. Therefore, C = m × c.

(iii) The initial temperature of the sample of sand is 7.0 °C. The sample of sand is heated using an electrical heater. The power of the heating element is 50 W. Calculate the time taken to increase the temperature of the sand to 19.0 °C. [3]

time = ........................................................... [3]

1. Find the temperature change (Δθ):
Δθ = 19.0 °C - 7.0 °C = 12.0 °C
2. Find the total energy needed (ΔE) using the thermal capacity from (a)(ii):
ΔE = Thermal Capacity × Δθ
ΔE = 142 500 J/°C × 12.0 °C = 1 710 000 J
3. Find the time (t) using Power (P) = Energy (ΔE) / time (t):
t = ΔE / P
t = 1 710 000 J / 50 W (since 1 W = 1 J/s)
t = 34 200 s (or 570 min, or 9.5 h)

Recap

The energy required to change an object's temperature is given by ΔE = mcΔθ. Power (P) is the rate of energy transfer, P = ΔE / t. These equations can be combined to solve for time: t = (mcΔθ) / P.

(b) In some countries, the soil is too cold for plants to grow well. In these countries, plants are grown in plastic pots and kept inside. The pots, containing soil, are placed on sand. The sand is heated using an electrical heater, as shown in Fig. 4.1.

(i) Describe, in terms of molecules, how thermal energy is transferred from the heated sand through the base of the plastic pot. [2]

This is transfer by conduction. The molecules (or particles) in the sand vibrate more when heated. These vibrating molecules collide with the molecules on the surface of the plastic pot, transferring energy to them. These molecules, in turn, vibrate more and collide with their neighbours, passing the thermal energy through the base of the pot.

Recap

Conduction is the main method of thermal energy transfer in solids. It occurs when particles vibrate and collide with neighbouring particles, transferring kinetic energy. Metals are especially good conductors because they also have free electrons that can move and transfer energy quickly.

(ii) The heating element in Fig. 4.1 remains switched on. The temperature of the sand remains constant at a value above room temperature. Explain why the temperature of the sand remains constant. [2]

The sand is at a constant temperature because it has reached a thermal equilibrium. The heater supplies thermal energy to the sand at a certain rate. Simultaneously, the sand is losing (thermal) energy to the cooler surroundings (the air, the pot, etc.). The temperature remains constant when the rate of energy supplied by the heater is equal to the rate of energy lost to the surroundings.

Recap

An object is in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings when it absorbs energy at the same rate as it emits energy. If an object is at a constant temperature but is being actively heated, it means its rate of energy gain from the heater is perfectly balanced by its rate of energy loss to the environment.

A boy looks at the image of a clock in a plane mirror. Fig. 5.1 shows the mirror, the clock and the position of one of the boy's eyes.

Recap

The law of reflection states that the angle of incidence (i) is equal to the angle of reflection (r). Both angles are measured relative to the normal, which is an imaginary line drawn at 90° to the mirror surface at the point where the ray hits.

(a) (ii) On Fig. 5.1, mark with an X the position of the image of the clock. [1]

The image X should be marked at a position directly behind the mirror, at the same perpendicular distance from the mirror as the clock is in front of it.

Recap

Images formed in a plane mirror have specific characteristics: they are virtual, upright, laterally inverted (left and right are swapped), and the same size as the object. The image is also located as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.

(a) (iii) State whether the image formed by the mirror is virtual or real. Explain your answer. [1]

The image is virtual. This is because the rays of light do not actually pass through the image's location; they only appear to come from that point. A virtual image cannot be projected onto a screen.

Recap

A real image (like one from a projector) is formed where light rays actually converge. A virtual image (like one in a plane mirror) is formed where light rays only *appear* to diverge from. Your eye traces these diverging rays back to a point behind the mirror.

(a) (iv) Fig. 5.2 shows the image of the clock seen by the boy.

The boy now looks directly at the clock. On Fig. 5.3, draw what the boy sees.

Recap

Lateral inversion is the "left-right" swapping that occurs in a plane mirror. The reflection of your right hand looks like a left hand. For a clock, this inverts the face horizontally.

(b) (i) The clock is illuminated by a source of monochromatic green light. State the meaning of monochromatic. [1]

Monochromatic means light of a single frequency (and therefore a single wavelength in a vacuum).

Recap

Monochromatic light (e.g., from a laser) consists of one colour or frequency. White light (polychromatic) is a mixture of many different frequencies (colours), which can be separated by dispersion through a prism.

(b) (ii) The green light has a wavelength of 5.6 × 10⁻⁷ m. Calculate the frequency of this green light. [3]

frequency = ..................................................... [3]

The wave equation is v = f × λ
All electromagnetic waves (like light) travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum (and approximately the same in air).
Frequency (f) = speed (v) / wavelength (λ)
f = (3.0 × 10⁸ m/s) / (5.6 × 10⁻⁷ m)
f = 5.357... × 10¹⁴ Hz

Recap

The wave equation, v = fλ, links wave speed (v), frequency (f), and wavelength (λ). The speed of light (and all electromagnetic waves) in a vacuum is a constant: 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

Question 6 Paper: 0625/42/F/M/22 Topics: Magnetism, EM Induction

(a) Fig. 6.1 shows two bar magnets. On Fig. 6.1, sketch the pattern and the direction of the magnetic field lines between the bar magnets. [2]

The sketch should show magnetic field lines as smooth curves emerging from the North (N) pole and entering the South (S) pole. The lines must have arrows on them pointing from N to S. The lines should be perpendicular to the magnet surfaces where they meet and should not cross each other. There should be at least one line straight across the middle and two curved lines (one above, one below).

Recap

Magnetic field lines are used to represent a magnetic field. They show the direction of the force that a North pole would experience. By convention, field lines always point away from a North pole and towards a South pole. The closer the lines, the stronger the field.

(b) Fig. 6.2 shows the same bar magnets with a coil of wire between them.

(i) Name the parts labelled A in Fig. 6.2. [1]

The parts labelled A are slip rings.

Recap

In an A.C. generator (alternator), slip rings and carbon brushes are used to connect the rotating coil to the external circuit. In a D.C. dynamo, a split-ring commutator is used instead to reverse the current every half-turn.

(ii) The coil of wire is rotated in the direction shown in Fig. 6.2. On Fig. 6.2, draw an arrow to show the direction of the current in the coil. Explain your answer. [2]

Using Fleming's Right-Hand Rule (the Generator Rule): The Field is from N to S (left to right). The Motion of the top wire is *out of the page* (and the bottom wire is *into the page*). Therefore, the Current in the top wire flows towards the back (and in the bottom wire, towards the front). The arrow drawn on the coil should show a clockwise direction of current flow.
Explanation: The field, motion of the wire, and induced current are all mutually perpendicular, as described by the Right-Hand Rule.

Recap

Fleming's Right-Hand Rule is used to find the direction of the induced current in a generator. Your Thumb points in the direction of Motion (Thrust), your Forefinger points in the direction of the Field (N to S), and your Centre finger points in the direction of the induced Current.

(iii) Explain how rotating the coil in Fig. 6.2 continuously causes the galvanometer needle to show an alternating current. [4]

As the coil rotates, the wires cut through the magnetic field lines between the magnets. This process, known as electromagnetic induction, induces an e.m.f. (voltage) across the ends of the coil. Because the coil is part of a complete circuit (with the galvanometer), this e.m.f. drives a current. As the coil rotates, one side (e.g., the top wire) moves up, and the other side moves down, inducing a current in one direction. Half a turn later (every 180 degrees), the wire that was moving up is now moving down, and vice versa. This reverses the direction of the induced e.m.f. and current. This continuous reversal of current is an alternating current (A.C.).

Recap

Electromagnetic induction occurs when a conductor cuts magnetic field lines, or when the magnetic field passing through a coil changes. This induces an e.m.f. (voltage). If there is a complete circuit, an induced current will flow. This is the principle behind generators.

Recap

When cells are connected in series (positive to negative), their individual e.m.f.s (voltages) add up to give the total e.m.f. of the battery. Vtotal = V₁ + V₂ + ...

Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor is proportional to the potential difference across it, provided the temperature is constant. The relationship is V = IR, where V is voltage, I is current, and R is resistance.

Question 8 Paper: 0625/42/F/M/22 Topics: Electromagnetism, Transformers

A radio is connected to the mains supply using a step-down transformer.

(a) Draw a labelled diagram of the structure of a basic step-down transformer. [3]

The diagram should include:
1. A soft iron core, typically drawn as a square or rectangular loop.
2. A primary coil wrapped around one side of the core.
3. A secondary coil wrapped around the other side.
4. For a step-down transformer, the secondary coil must be labelled and drawn with fewer turns (loops) than the primary coil.

Recap

A transformer consists of two coils, primary and secondary, wound on a soft iron core. A step-up transformer has *more* turns on the secondary (increases voltage). A step-down transformer has *fewer* turns on the secondary (decreases voltage).

(b) Explain the operation of a basic transformer. [3]

1. An alternating voltage (A.C.) in the primary coil drives an alternating current through it.
2. This alternating current produces a changing magnetic field in the soft iron core.
3. The iron core concentrates this changing magnetic field, which then passes through (links with) the secondary coil.
4. The changing magnetic field induces an alternating e.m.f. (voltage) across the ends of the secondary coil.

Recap

Transformers work on the principle of electromagnetic induction. They require a changing magnetic field, which is why they only work with A.C. (alternating current), not D.C. (direct current).

value of turns ratio = ...................................... [2]

Using the transformer equation:
(Voltage in secondary) / (Voltage in primary) = (Turns on secondary) / (Turns on primary)
Vs / Vp = Ns / Np
Turns ratio (Ns / Np) = 6.0 V / 230 V
Turns ratio (Ns / Np) = 0.02608...
Turns ratio (Ns / Np) = 0.026 (to 2 significant figures)

Recap

The transformer ratio equation links the voltages and the number of turns on the coils: Vs / Vp = Ns / Np. For an ideal (100% efficient) transformer, the power is conserved: VpIp = VsIs.

Question 10 Paper: 0625/42/F/M/22 Topics: Nuclear Physics, Radioactivity

The isotope americium-241 is represented by ${}^{241}_{95}\text{Am}$. This isotope decays by α-emission to an isotope of neptunium (Np).

(a) Complete the nuclide equation for this decay. [3]

${}^{241}_{95}\text{Am} \rightarrow$ .....................................................

An α-particle (alpha) is a helium nucleus, ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ or ${}^{4}_{2}\alpha$.
In alpha decay, the nucleon number (top) decreases by 4, and the proton number (bottom) decreases by 2.
Nucleon number: 241 - 4 = 237
Proton number: 95 - 2 = 93
Completed equation: ${}^{241}_{95}\text{Am} \rightarrow {}^{237}_{93}\text{Np} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha$

Recap

In nuclear decay equations, both nucleon number (mass number, A) and proton number (atomic number, Z) must be conserved (balanced) on both sides of the arrow.
• Alpha decay: A decreases by 4, Z decreases by 2.
• Beta decay: A is unchanged, Z increases by 1.

(b) Fig. 10.1 shows a simple diagram of a smoke detector. The smoke detector contains a small sample of americium-241. This isotope ionises the air between the metal plates in the detector.

(i) Describe how the americium-241 ionises air. [3]

The americium-241 source emits alpha (α) particles. These alpha particles are highly ionising. As they travel through the air between the plates, they collide with (or pass close to) neutral air molecules. These collisions transfer energy to the air molecules, knocking electrons out of their orbits. This leaves behind positively charged ions and free electrons, creating an ionised path.

Recap

Ionisation is the process of removing (or adding) electrons from a neutral atom to create an ion. Alpha particles are the most ionising form of radiation because they are large, slow, and have a +2 charge. Beta particles are moderately ionising. Gamma rays are the least ionising.

(ii) Suggest and explain two reasons why smoke detectors use an isotope that emits α-particles rather than an isotope that emits γ-radiation. [2]

1. ..............................................................................................................................................

2. ..............................................................................................................................................

1. Alpha particles are highly ionising. This means they are very effective at ionising the air in the detector, which is necessary for the detector to work. Gamma rays are weakly ionising and would not ionise the air sufficiently.
2. Alpha particles have a very short range (a few cm in air) and are not very penetrating (stopped by paper or smoke). This means they will be stopped by smoke particles, which triggers the alarm. It also makes them safer outside the detector, as they cannot penetrate the plastic casing (or skin).

Recap

Radiation properties:
• Alpha (α): Highly ionising, low penetration (stopped by paper/skin).
• Beta (β): Moderately ionising, medium penetration (stopped by thin aluminium).
• Gamma (γ): Weakly ionising, high penetration (stopped by thick lead/concrete).
