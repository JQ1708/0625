Topic(s): Forces, Springs, Hooke's Law, Energy Transfers

Fig. 1.1 shows a load suspended from a spring.

The value of the spring constant k of the spring is 0.20N/cm. The spring reaches its limit of proportionality when the load is 15N.

(a) Calculate the extension of the spring when the load is 3.0N.

1. Convert the spring constant to N/m:
k = 0.20 N/cm = 0.20 N / 0.01 m = 20 N/m.

2. Use Hooke's Law, F = kx, where F is the force (load), k is the spring constant, and x is the extension.
Rearrange to find x: x = F / k.

3. Calculate the extension:
x = 3.0 N / 20 N/m = 0.15 m = 15 cm.

Recap

Hooke's Law states that the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the applied force, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded (F = kx). Remember to ensure consistent units (e.g., convert cm to m or N/cm to N/m).

(b) Explain what is meant by the term limit of proportionality of the spring.

The limit of proportionality is the point beyond which the extension of the spring is no longer directly proportional to the applied load (force). Up to this point, Hooke's Law is obeyed. Beyond this point, the spring may still return to its original length (within its elastic limit), but the relationship between force and extension is no longer linear.

Recap

The elastic limit is the maximum stress a material can withstand without undergoing permanent deformation. The limit of proportionality is usually very close to, but sometimes slightly before, the elastic limit. Beyond the elastic limit, the material will not return to its original shape once the force is removed.

(c) On Fig. 1.2, sketch an extension-load graph for a spring. Label the limit of proportionality with the letter L on your graph.

The graph should show:

  • An initial straight line passing through the origin with a positive gradient.
  • A point labelled 'L' on this straight line, representing the limit of proportionality.
  • After point L, the graph should curve to the right, indicating that the extension increases more rapidly for a given increase in load (the gradient increases).

Recap

The gradient of an extension-load graph represents 1/k (the inverse of the spring constant). A straight line indicates Hooke's Law is obeyed. When the graph curves, the spring is no longer obeying Hooke's Law, and its stiffness is effectively decreasing.

(d) The load is pulled down a small distance below its equilibrium position to position A, as shown in Fig. 1.3. The load then moves up and down between position A and position B in Fig. 1.3.

Describe the energy transfers which occur as the load moves:

from position A to the equilibrium position

As the load moves from position A (maximum extension, lowest point) to the equilibrium position:

  • Elastic potential energy (stored in the stretched spring) is converted into kinetic energy of the load.
  • Gravitational potential energy also increases as the load moves upwards.

from the equilibrium position to position B.

As the load moves from the equilibrium position (maximum kinetic energy) to position B (maximum compression/minimum extension, highest point):

  • Kinetic energy of the load is converted into gravitational potential energy (as it moves higher) and elastic potential energy (as the spring compresses/reduces extension).

Recap

This describes the energy transformations in a simple harmonic motion system. Energy continuously oscillates between kinetic, elastic potential, and gravitational potential forms. In a real system, some energy would also be lost as heat due to air resistance and internal friction within the spring.

Topic(s): Pressure, Density

(a) Fig. 2.1 shows a bookshelf with two groups of books A and B on it. There are six books in each group of books. All the books are identical. The mass of each book is 0.52 kg.

(i) Explain why the pressure exerted on the shelf by the books in group B is less than the pressure exerted on the shelf by the books in group A.

Pressure is defined as Force / Area. Both groups of books have the same total mass, and therefore exert the same total downward force (weight) on the shelf. However, the books in group B are arranged such that they are lying flat, covering a larger area of the shelf compared to group A, where the books are standing upright on their narrow edges. Since Pressure is inversely proportional to Area (for a constant force), the larger contact area of group B results in less pressure being exerted on the shelf.

Recap

This illustrates the concept of pressure. For a constant force, increasing the contact area decreases the pressure, and decreasing the contact area increases the pressure. This is why snowshoes help you walk on snow (larger area, less pressure) and why a knife is sharp (small area, high pressure).

(ii) Calculate the pressure exerted on the shelf by the books in group A.

1. Calculate the total mass of books in group A:
Total mass = 6 books × 0.52 kg/book = 3.12 kg.

2. Calculate the total weight (force) exerted by the books:
Force (Weight) = mass × g = 3.12 kg × 10 N/kg = 31.2 N.

3. Determine the contact area for group A. From Fig. 2.1, each book stands on an edge of 1.3 cm × 21 cm. There are 6 books.
Area of one book's edge = 1.3 cm × 21 cm = 27.3 cm² = 0.00273 m².
Total contact area = 6 × 0.00273 m² = 0.01638 m².

4. Calculate the pressure:
Pressure = Force / Area = 31.2 N / 0.01638 m² = 1904.7 Pa (approx. 1900 Pa).

Recap

Ensure all units are consistent (e.g., convert cm to m for area calculations). The acceleration due to gravity (g) is usually taken as 10 N/kg or 10 m/s² in IGCSE Physics unless otherwise specified.

(b) A diver dives to a depth below the surface of the sea where the total pressure is 3.0 × 105 Pa. The atmospheric pressure is 1.0 × 105 Pa. The density of the sea water is 1030kg/m³.

Calculate the depth of the diver below the surface of the sea.

1. Calculate the pressure due to the sea water only:
Pressure of sea water = Total pressure - Atmospheric pressure
= (3.0 × 105 Pa) - (1.0 × 105 Pa) = 2.0 × 105 Pa.

2. Use the formula for pressure in a liquid: P = hρg, where P is pressure, h is depth, ρ is density, and g is acceleration due to gravity (10 N/kg).
Rearrange to find h: h = P / (ρg).

3. Calculate the depth:
h = (2.0 × 105 Pa) / (1030 kg/m³ × 10 N/kg)
h = (2.0 × 105) / 10300 = 19.4 m (approx. 19 m).

Recap

Remember that total pressure at a depth in a liquid includes atmospheric pressure. The formula P = hρg is crucial for calculating pressure or depth in fluids. Always subtract atmospheric pressure to find the gauge pressure (pressure due to the liquid column alone).

Topic(s): Dynamics, Kinematics, Forces

A car travels at constant speed v on a horizontal, straight road. The driver sees an obstacle on the road ahead.

(a) The distance travelled in the time between the driver seeing the obstruction and applying the brakes is the thinking distance.

Explain why the thinking distance is directly proportional to v.

The thinking time (reaction time) of the driver is approximately constant. During this constant time, the car travels at a constant speed, v. Since distance = speed × time, and time is constant, the thinking distance is directly proportional to the speed v.

Recap

Thinking distance is the distance travelled during the driver's reaction time. Factors affecting thinking distance include driver fatigue, distractions, and alcohol/drugs, all of which can increase thinking time.

(b) When the brakes are applied, the car decelerates uniformly to rest. The frictional force applied by the brakes is constant. The distance travelled between first applying the brakes and the car stopping is the braking distance.

Explain why the braking distance is proportional to v2.

1. The work done by the brakes to stop the car is equal to the initial kinetic energy of the car.
Work Done = Force × distance (W = Fd)
Kinetic Energy = ½mv²

2. So, Fd = ½mv².

3. Since the braking force (F) and the mass (m) of the car are constant, we can rearrange the equation to show:
d = (m / 2F) × v².

4. As (m / 2F) is a constant, the braking distance (d) is directly proportional to the square of the initial speed (v²).

Recap

Braking distance is the distance travelled while the brakes are applied. It is significantly affected by road conditions (wet/dry), tyre condition, and the efficiency of the brakes. The quadratic relationship with speed means that doubling the speed quadruples the braking distance.

(c) The car is travelling at 22 m/s.

(i) The thinking distance is 15m.

Calculate the time taken to travel the thinking distance.

Using the formula: Time = Distance / Speed
Time = 15 m / 22 m/s = 0.68 s (to 2 s.f.).

(ii) The car has a mass of 1400kg. The time taken for the car to stop after the brakes are applied is 2.1 s.

Calculate the force required to stop the car in this time.

1. Calculate the deceleration (a) of the car:
Using v = u + at, where v=0 (final speed), u=22 m/s (initial speed), t=2.1 s.
0 = 22 + a × 2.1
a = -22 / 2.1 = -10.48 m/s² (deceleration is 10.48 m/s²).

2. Calculate the braking force (F) using Newton's Second Law: F = ma.
F = 1400 kg × 10.48 m/s² = 14672 N (approx. 15000 N).

Recap

This problem combines kinematics (equations of motion) with Newton's Second Law. Remember to use the magnitude of deceleration when calculating force, as the force is in the opposite direction to motion.

Topic(s): Thermal Physics, Latent Heat, Evaporation

(a) (i) Define specific latent heat of fusion.

The specific latent heat of fusion is the amount of thermal energy required to change 1 kg of a substance from a solid state to a liquid state at its melting point, without any change in temperature.

Recap

Latent heat refers to the energy absorbed or released during a phase transition (e.g., melting, freezing, boiling, condensation) at constant temperature. "Specific" means per unit mass (usually 1 kg).

(ii) A cup of water contains 250cm³ of water at a temperature of 0°C. An identical cup contains 250 cm³ of a mixture of ice and water at a temperature of 0°C.

The temperature of the surrounding air is 20°C.

State and explain which cup contains the liquid with the lower temperature after 10 minutes.

Statement: The cup containing the mixture of ice and water will have the lower temperature (it will still be 0°C) after 10 minutes.

Explanation: The cup with only water at 0°C will absorb heat from the surroundings and its temperature will begin to rise. The cup with the ice and water mixture will also absorb heat, but this energy will first be used to melt the ice (latent heat of fusion) rather than increasing the temperature of the water. The temperature of the mixture will remain at 0°C until all the ice has melted. Therefore, after 10 minutes, its temperature will be lower than the cup that started with only water.

Recap

During a phase change (like melting ice), the temperature of the substance remains constant even though heat energy is being added. This energy is used to change the state of the substance, not its kinetic energy (temperature).

(b) (i) On a hot day, sweat forms on a person's skin and then evaporates.

Explain, in terms of molecules, how the evaporation of sweat cools the person.

During evaporation, the most energetic (fastest-moving) molecules of sweat gain enough kinetic energy to escape from the liquid surface and become water vapour. These energetic molecules take a significant amount of thermal energy (latent heat of vaporisation) with them as they leave. This reduces the average kinetic energy of the remaining sweat molecules and the skin, leading to a cooling effect on the person's skin.

Recap

Evaporation is a cooling process because it preferentially removes higher-energy molecules from the liquid, lowering the average kinetic energy (and thus temperature) of the remaining liquid and its surroundings.

(ii) Explain why this process is more effective when a wind is blowing.

When wind blows, it carries away the water vapour molecules that have just evaporated from the skin. This prevents the air immediately above the skin from becoming saturated with water vapour. By continuously removing the humid air, the wind maintains a steep concentration gradient, allowing more sweat molecules to evaporate more quickly, thus increasing the rate of cooling.

Recap

Factors affecting the rate of evaporation include temperature, surface area, humidity, and air movement. Wind increases the rate of evaporation by removing saturated air, allowing more liquid molecules to escape into the atmosphere.

Topic(s): Waves, Sound, Diffraction

Fig. 5.1 shows a wave on the sea approaching a harbour.

(a) On Fig. 5.1, draw three wave crests in the harbour.

The diagram should show three curved wave crests inside the harbour. These crests should be concentric arcs, originating from the gap in the harbour walls, and spreading out into the harbour. The wavelength of these diffracted waves should be the same as the incident waves.

Recap

Diffraction is the spreading of waves as they pass through an opening or around an obstacle. The amount of diffraction depends on the wavelength of the wave and the size of the opening. When waves pass through a gap, they spread out, and if the gap is small compared to the wavelength, the waves spread out almost as if the gap were a point source.

(ii) Another harbour has a much wider gap between its walls.

Describe and explain how the pattern of wave crests in this harbour is different from the pattern you have drawn in (i).

Description: With a much wider gap, the wave crests inside the harbour will be less spread out. They will appear more like straight lines, similar to the incident waves, with only slight curvature at the edges.

Explanation: The amount of diffraction is less significant when the wavelength of the wave is much smaller than the width of the gap. As the gap becomes wider relative to the wavelength, the waves pass through with less spreading, and the diffraction effect is less noticeable.

Recap

The extent of diffraction is inversely related to the ratio of the gap size to the wavelength. Large gaps relative to wavelength result in minimal diffraction, while small gaps relative to wavelength result in significant diffraction.

(b) A sound wave of frequency 850Hz travels through sea water. The speed of sound in sea water is 1500m/s.

Calculate the wavelength of this sound wave in sea water.

3. Calculate the wavelength:
λ = 1500 m/s / 850 Hz = 1.76 m (approx. 1.8 m).

Recap

The wave speed equation (v = fλ) is fundamental for all types of waves. Ensure units are consistent (m/s for speed, Hz for frequency, m for wavelength).

Topic(s): Electricity, Electromotive Force, Circuits, Resistance

(a) Define electromotive force (e.m.f.).

Electromotive force (e.m.f.) is the energy supplied by a source (e.g., battery, generator) per unit charge to drive the charge around a complete circuit. It is measured in Volts (V), where 1 V = 1 J/C.

Recap

e.m.f. is the total energy provided by the source, while potential difference (p.d.) is the energy converted from electrical to other forms per unit charge across a component. In a closed circuit, the sum of p.d.s across components equals the e.m.f. of the source.

(b) Fig. 7.1 shows a circuit.

The two lamps shown are identical. Each lamp has a potential difference (p.d.) of 3.0 V across it and a current of 2.0 A in it. PQ is a length of uniform metal wire. The resistance of PQ is R.

(i) Calculate the value of R.

1. The two lamps are in parallel. The p.d. across each lamp is 3.0 V. Therefore, the p.d. across the parallel combination is 3.0 V.

2. The current through each lamp is 2.0 A. So, the total current flowing into the parallel combination is 2.0 A + 2.0 A = 4.0 A.

3. The total voltage supplied by the battery is 12 V. The p.d. across the parallel lamps is 3.0 V. Therefore, the p.d. across the wire PQ (which is in series with the parallel lamps) is:
VPQ = Total V - Vlamps = 12 V - 3.0 V = 9.0 V.

4. The current flowing through wire PQ is the total current from the battery, which is 4.0 A.

5. Calculate the resistance R of wire PQ using Ohm's Law (R = V/I):
R = 9.0 V / 4.0 A = 2.25 Ω (approx. 2.3 Ω or 2.2 Ω).

Recap

In a series circuit, current is the same everywhere, and voltages add up. In a parallel circuit, voltage is the same across each branch, and currents add up. Ohm's Law (V=IR) is fundamental for calculating resistance, voltage, or current.

(ii) Another piece of wire is made of the same metal as PQ. The length of the new piece of wire is twice the length of PQ. The diameter of the new piece of wire is twice the diameter of PQ.

Calculate the resistance of the new piece of wire.

1. The resistance of a wire is given by R = ρL/A, where ρ is resistivity, L is length, and A is cross-sectional area. Area A = π(d/2)² = πd²/4.

2. For the new wire:
Length (L') = 2L
Diameter (d') = 2d, so Area (A') = π(2d/2)² = πd² = 4A.

3. Calculate the new resistance (R'):
R' = ρ(2L) / (4A) = (2/4) × (ρL/A) = 0.5 × R.

4. Using R = 2.25 Ω (from part i):
R' = 0.5 × 2.25 Ω = 1.125 Ω (approx. 1.1 Ω).

Recap

The resistance of a wire is directly proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area. Doubling the length doubles the resistance. Doubling the diameter (which means quadrupling the area) quarters the resistance.

Topic(s): Electronics, Logic Gates, Signals

(a) State the difference between an analogue signal and a digital signal. You may draw a diagram to help explain your answer.

An analogue signal is a continuous signal that can take any value within a given range. It varies smoothly over time, representing information as a continuous wave. Examples include sound waves, temperature, and light intensity.

A digital signal is a discrete signal that can only take a limited number of distinct values, typically two (e.g., ON/OFF, HIGH/LOW, 1/0). It represents information as a series of pulses. Examples include computer data and compact disc audio.

Recap

The key difference lies in their nature: analogue signals are continuous, while digital signals are discrete. Digital signals are generally more robust against noise and easier to process and store.

(b) Draw the symbol for a NOR gate.

The symbol for a NOR gate is an OR gate symbol followed by a small circle (inversion bubble) at the output. It typically has two inputs and one output.

Recap

A NOR gate (NOT OR) produces an output of 1 (HIGH) only if all its inputs are 0 (LOW). Otherwise, its output is 0 (LOW).

(a) An X-ray machine requires a supply of 110kV. The mains electricity supply is 230V. A transformer is used to supply the correct voltage to the X-ray machine. There are 50 turns on the primary coil of the transformer.

Calculate the number of turns on the secondary coil.

1. Use the transformer equation: Vp/Vs = Np/Ns, where V is voltage and N is the number of turns, and subscripts p and s refer to primary and secondary coils, respectively.

2. Rearrange to find Ns: Ns = Np × (Vs/Vp).

3. Convert voltages to consistent units (e.g., Volts):
Vp = 230 V
Vs = 110 kV = 110 000 V.

4. Calculate Ns:
Ns = 50 × (110 000 V / 230 V) = 50 × 478.26 = 23913 (approx. 24 000 turns).

Recap

A transformer works on the principle of electromagnetic induction to change AC voltages. For a step-up transformer (like this one, increasing voltage), the secondary coil has more turns than the primary coil.

(b) Draw a labelled diagram of a step-down transformer. On the labels, state a suitable material for each of the components.

The diagram should show:

  • A soft-iron core (material: soft iron, laminated to reduce eddy currents).
  • A primary coil (material: insulated copper wire) wound around one side of the core.
  • A secondary coil (material: insulated copper wire) wound around the other side of the core, with fewer turns than the primary coil (for a step-down transformer).

Recap

A step-down transformer reduces voltage. It has more turns on the primary coil than on the secondary coil. The soft-iron core concentrates the magnetic field, ensuring efficient energy transfer.

(c) Explain how a transformer operates.

1. An alternating voltage applied to the primary coil causes an alternating current to flow through it.

2. This alternating current produces a continuously changing magnetic field in the soft-iron core.

3. The soft-iron core concentrates and channels this changing magnetic field through the secondary coil.

4. As the changing magnetic field passes through the secondary coil, it induces an alternating electromotive force (e.m.f.) across the secondary coil (Faraday's Law of electromagnetic induction).

Recap

Transformers rely on electromagnetic induction. A changing magnetic field is essential to induce an e.m.f. in the secondary coil. This is why transformers only work with alternating current (AC), not direct current (DC).

Topic(s): Atomic Physics, Radioactivity, Magnetic Fields, Safety

Fig. 10.1 shows a beam of radiation in a vacuum. The beam contains α-particles, β-particles and γ-rays.

The beam enters a region where there is a strong, uniform magnetic field. The direction of the magnetic field is out of the page.

On Fig. 10.1, mark and label the paths through the magnetic field of:

(i) α-particles (label this path α)

Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule:

  • Magnetic Field (Forefinger) is out of the page.
  • Current (seCond finger) for α-particles (positive charge) is to the right.
  • Force (ThumB) is downwards.
Therefore, the α-particles will curve downwards.

(ii) β-particles (label this path β)

Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule:

  • Magnetic Field (Forefinger) is out of the page.
  • Current (seCond finger) for β-particles (negative charge) is to the left (opposite to motion).
  • Force (ThumB) is upwards.
Therefore, the β-particles will curve upwards. They will also curve more sharply than α-particles due to their smaller mass and higher charge-to-mass ratio.

(iii) γ-rays (label this path γ).

γ-rays are electromagnetic radiation and have no charge. Therefore, they are not affected by magnetic fields and will pass straight through the field without deflection.

Recap

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule is used to determine the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor (or moving charge) in a magnetic field. Alpha particles are positively charged, beta particles are negatively charged, and gamma rays are uncharged.

(b) State two safety precautions which hospital staff take when working with γ-ray sources.

1. Minimise exposure time: Hospital staff should spend as little time as possible near the gamma source.

2. Maximise distance: Use long-handled tongs or robotic arms to keep as far away from the source as possible.

3. Use shielding: Store and handle gamma sources behind thick lead or concrete barriers.

4. Monitor exposure: Wear film badges or dosimeters to track accumulated radiation dose.

Recap

The three main principles of radiation protection are Time, Distance, and Shielding. Minimising time, maximising distance, and using appropriate shielding are crucial for reducing radiation exposure.

(c) The radioactive isotope iodine-131 is used as a tracer in medical diagnosis. A nucleus of iodine-131 contains 53 protons and 78 neutrons. The symbol for iodine is I.

(i) Use nuclide notation to show this isotope of iodine.

Nuclide notation is written as AZX, where A is the mass number (protons + neutrons), Z is the atomic number (protons), and X is the element symbol.

Mass number (A) = Protons + Neutrons = 53 + 78 = 131.

Atomic number (Z) = Protons = 53.

Element symbol = I.

So, the nuclide notation is 13153I.

(ii) Explain why this emission and this half-life make iodine-131 a suitable material for a tracer in medical diagnosis.

1. Gamma emission: Iodine-131 emits gamma radiation. Gamma rays are highly penetrating, so they can easily pass out of the body and be detected by external detectors. They are also weakly ionising, causing less damage to body tissues compared to alpha or beta radiation.

2. Suitable half-life: A half-life of 8 hours is long enough for the tracer to be transported to the target organ and for diagnostic tests to be completed. However, it is short enough that the radioactivity quickly decays within the body, minimising the patient's long-term radiation exposure.

Recap

Ideal medical tracers should:

  • Emit gamma radiation (for penetration and low ionisation).
  • Have a short to medium half-life (long enough for diagnosis, short enough to minimise exposure).
  • Be chemically active in the body to target specific organs.
