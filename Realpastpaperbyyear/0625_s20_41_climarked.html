Question 1 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q1 | Mechanics

An aeroplane of mass 2.5 × 105 kg lands with a speed of 62 m/s, on a horizontal runway at time t = 0. The aeroplane decelerates uniformly as it travels along the runway in a straight line until it reaches a speed of 6.0 m/s at t = 35 s.

(a) Calculate:

(i) the deceleration of the aeroplane in the 35 s after it lands [2]

The deceleration is calculated using the formula a = (v - u) / t. The initial speed, u, is 62 m/s and the final speed, v, is 6.0 m/s. The time taken, t, is 35 s.
Change in speed = 6.0 m/s - 62 m/s = -56 m/s.
Deceleration = (-56 m/s) / 35 s = -1.6 m/s².

Recap

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. A positive acceleration means the object is speeding up, while a negative acceleration, or deceleration, means the object is slowing down. It is a vector quantity, measured in m/s².

(ii) the resultant force acting on the aeroplane as it decelerates [2]

The resultant force is calculated using Newton's second law, F = ma. The mass, m, is 2.5 × 105 kg and the acceleration (deceleration), a, is 1.6 m/s².
Force = (2.5 × 105 kg) × (1.6 m/s²) = 4.0 × 105 N.
The resultant force is 4.0 × 105 N.

Recap

Newton's Second Law states that the resultant force on an object is equal to the product of its mass and acceleration (F=ma). The force is always in the same direction as the acceleration.

(iii) the momentum of the aeroplane when its speed is 6.0 m/s. [2]

Momentum is calculated using the formula p = mv. The mass, m, is 2.5 × 105 kg and the velocity, v, is 6.0 m/s.
Momentum = (2.5 × 105 kg) × (6.0 m/s) = 1.5 × 106 kg m/s.
The momentum is 1.5 × 106 kg m/s.

Recap

Momentum is a measure of an object's motion, defined as the product of its mass and velocity (p=mv). It is a vector quantity, and the unit is kg m/s. The principle of conservation of momentum is fundamental in collisions.

(b) At t = 35 s, the aeroplane stops decelerating and moves along the runway at a constant speed of 6.0 m/s for a further 15 s. On Fig. 1.1, sketch the shape of the graph for the distance travelled by the aeroplane along the runway between t = 0 and t = 50 s. You are not required to calculate distance values. [3]

The distance-time graph should show a curve with a decreasing gradient from t=0 to t=35 s, indicating deceleration. From t=35 s to t=50 s, the graph should be a straight line with a positive gradient, indicating constant speed. The gradient of the curve at t=35s should not be zero and should seamlessly transition to the straight line.

Recap

On a distance-time graph, the gradient (slope) of the line represents the speed of the object. A straight line indicates constant speed, while a curve indicates changing speed (acceleration or deceleration).

(c) As the aeroplane decelerates, its kinetic energy decreases. Suggest what happens to this energy. [1]

The kinetic energy is converted into other forms of energy due to work done by resistive forces. It is primarily transformed into thermal energy (heat) in the brakes, tyres, and the surrounding air.

Recap

The Principle of Conservation of Energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one form to another. In this case, the kinetic energy of the aeroplane is converted into thermal energy (heat) due to the work done by friction in the brakes.

Fig. 2.1 is the extension–load graph for a light spring S.

Hooke's law is obeyed where the graph is a straight line passing through the origin. From the graph, this is from a load of 0 N to 8.0 N.

Recap

Hooke's Law states that the force needed to stretch or compress a spring by some distance is directly proportional to that distance, as long as the limit of proportionality is not exceeded. This is shown by a straight-line relationship on a force-extension graph.

(b) Using information from Fig. 2.1, determine the spring constant k of spring S. [2]

The spring constant k is the ratio of force to extension (k = F/x). Using the limit of proportionality from the graph, F = 8.0 N and the extension x = 15 cm = 0.15 m.
k = 8.0 N / 0.15 m = 53.33 N/m.
The spring constant k is approximately 53 N/m.

Recap

The spring constant (k) is a measure of a spring's stiffness. It is the ratio of the force applied to the extension produced (k = F/x). A stiffer spring has a higher spring constant. The unit is Newtons per meter (N/m).

(c) A second spring, identical to spring S, is attached to spring S. The two springs are attached to a rod, as shown in Fig. 2.2. A load of 4.0 N is suspended from the bottom of spring S. The arrangement is in equilibrium.

The energy stored in stretched springs is elastic potential energy.

Recap

Elastic potential energy is the energy stored in an elastic object as a result of its deformation (e.g., stretching or compressing). When the deforming force is removed, this stored energy is converted into kinetic energy.

When springs are connected in series, the total extension is the sum of the extensions of each individual spring. Each spring experiences the same tension (load).

The work done in stretching a spring is equal to the area under the force-extension graph. This work is stored as elastic potential energy in the spring. The change in stored energy is the work done to produce the additional extension.

Question 3 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q3 | Thermal Physics & Pressure

Fig. 3.1 shows gas trapped in the sealed end of a tube by a dense liquid.

The scale marked on the sealed end of the tube is calibrated to read the volume of gas trapped above the liquid surface. Fig. 3.1 shows that initially the volume V1 of the gas is 60 cm³. The pressure of the atmosphere is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa.

(a) State how Fig. 3.1 shows that the pressure of the trapped gas is equal to the pressure of the atmosphere. [1]

The pressure of the trapped gas is shown to be equal to the atmospheric pressure because the liquid levels in both the sealed and the open limbs of the tube are equal.

Recap

A manometer measures gas pressure. When the liquid levels in both arms are equal, it indicates that the pressure of the trapped gas is equal to the pressure acting on the open arm, which is typically atmospheric pressure.

(b) Explain, in terms of the momentum of its molecules, why the trapped gas exerts a pressure on the walls of the tube. [3]

The gas exerts pressure because its molecules are in constant, random motion and collide with the walls of the container. When they collide, their momentum changes (as they rebound). This change in momentum results in a force being exerted on the walls. The pressure is this total force spread out over the area of the walls.

Recap

The Kinetic Theory explains gas pressure as the result of countless collisions between gas molecules and the container walls. Each collision involves a change in momentum, which exerts a small force. The average of these forces over the container's area creates the pressure.

(c) More of the dense liquid is poured into the open end of the tube. The level of the liquid surface in both the sealed and the open ends of the tube rises as shown in Fig. 3.2. The temperature of the trapped gas and atmospheric pressure both remain constant.

(i) In the sealed end of the tube, the volume V₂ of the trapped gas is 50 cm³. In the open end of the tube, the liquid surface is 15 cm above the new level in the sealed tube. Calculate the pressure p₂ of the trapped gas. [2]

Since the temperature is constant, Boyle's Law (p₁V₁ = p₂V₂) can be used.
p₁ = 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, V₁ = 60 cm³
V₂ = 50 cm³
p₂ = (p₁V₁) / V₂ = (1.0 × 10⁵ Pa × 60 cm³) / 50 cm³ = 1.2 × 10⁵ Pa.
The new pressure p₂ is 1.2 × 10⁵ Pa.

Recap

Boyle's Law states that for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume (p₁V₁ = p₂V₂). This means if you decrease the volume, the pressure increases.

(ii) Calculate the density of the liquid in the tube. [2]

The new pressure p₂ is the sum of atmospheric pressure and the pressure due to the liquid column (p₂ = pₐₜₘ + hρg).
The pressure difference is Δp = p₂ - pₐₜₘ = 1.2 × 10⁵ Pa - 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa = 2.0 × 10⁴ Pa.
This pressure difference is caused by the 15 cm (0.15 m) column of liquid.
Δp = hρg => ρ = Δp / (hg)
ρ = (2.0 × 10⁴ Pa) / (0.15 m × 10 N/kg) = 13333 kg/m³.
The density of the liquid is approximately 1.3 × 10⁴ kg/m³.

Recap

The pressure exerted by a column of liquid is calculated using the formula p = hρg, where h is the height of the column, ρ (rho) is the density of the liquid, and g is the acceleration due to gravity. This pressure acts in all directions at a given depth.

Question 4 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q4 | Thermal Physics

Water has a specific heat capacity of 4200 J / (kg °C) and a boiling point of 100 °C.

(a) State what is meant by boiling point. [1]

The boiling point is the specific temperature at which a substance changes state from a liquid to a gas throughout the bulk of the liquid.

Recap

Boiling is a phase transition from liquid to gas that occurs at a specific temperature, known as the boiling point. During boiling, bubbles of vapor form throughout the bulk of the liquid, not just at the surface. This process requires a continuous input of energy called latent heat of vaporisation.

(b) A mass of 0.30 kg of water at its boiling point is poured into a copper container which is initially at 11 °C. After a few seconds, the temperature of the container and the water are both 95 °C.

(i) Calculate the energy transferred from the water. [2]

The energy transferred is calculated using the formula E = mcΔT.
m = 0.30 kg, c = 4200 J/(kg °C), ΔT = 100 °C - 95 °C = 5 °C.
E = 0.30 kg × 4200 J/(kg °C) × 5 °C = 6300 J.
The energy transferred from the water is 6300 J.

Recap

The energy required to change the temperature of a substance is given by E = mcΔT, where m is the mass, c is the specific heat capacity, and ΔT is the change in temperature. Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1kg of a substance by 1°C.

(ii) Calculate the thermal capacity of the copper container. [2]

The thermal energy lost by the water is gained by the copper container. The thermal capacity C is E / ΔT.
E = 6300 J, ΔT = 95 °C - 11 °C = 84 °C.
C = 6300 J / 84 °C = 75 J/°C.
The thermal capacity of the copper container is 75 J/°C.

Recap

Thermal capacity (C) is the energy required to raise the temperature of an entire object by 1°C. It is different from specific heat capacity as it does not depend on mass (C = E/ΔT). Its unit is Joules per degree Celsius (J/°C).

(iii) Water from the container evaporates and the temperature of the remaining water decreases slowly. Explain, in terms of molecules, why evaporation causes the temperature of the remaining water to decrease. [3]

During evaporation, it is the more energetic molecules that have enough energy to overcome the attractive forces and escape from the liquid surface. This means the average kinetic energy of the remaining molecules decreases. Since temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules, the temperature of the remaining water falls.

Recap

Evaporation causes cooling because the most energetic molecules (those with the highest kinetic energy) are the ones that escape from the liquid's surface. This lowers the average kinetic energy of the remaining molecules, and therefore the temperature of the liquid decreases.

Question 6 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q6 | Waves

The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s.

(a) Calculate the range of wavelengths for sounds that are audible by a healthy human ear. [2]

The audible frequency range for a healthy human ear is approximately 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. The wavelength (λ) is calculated using the wave equation v = fλ, so λ = v/f.
For the lowest frequency (f = 20 Hz): λ = 340 m/s / 20 Hz = 17 m.
For the highest frequency (f = 20,000 Hz): λ = 340 m/s / 20,000 Hz = 0.017 m.
The range of audible wavelengths is from 0.017 m to 17 m.

Recap

The wave speed (v) is related to its frequency (f) and wavelength (λ) by the wave equation: v = f × λ. Wavelength is the distance between successive crests, while frequency is the number of waves passing a point per second.

(b) Sound waves are longitudinal waves. Describe how a longitudinal wave differs from a transverse wave. [3]

The key difference is the direction of vibration relative to the direction of energy transfer.
- In a longitudinal wave, the vibrations of the particles are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. These waves consist of compressions and rarefactions.
- In a transverse wave, the vibrations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer.

Recap

In a longitudinal wave (like sound), the particle oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer, consisting of compressions and rarefactions. In a transverse wave (like light), the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer.

(c) Fig. 6.1 shows a band in front of a building.

The drum produces a low frequency sound. Other musical instruments produce a high frequency sound. These sounds are equally loud. A young man at the side of the building hears the drum but not the high frequency sounds from the other musical instruments. Explain why this happens. [3]

This phenomenon is explained by diffraction, which is the spreading of waves as they pass through a gap or around an obstacle.
The sound from the drum has a low frequency and therefore a long wavelength. The high-frequency sounds have short wavelengths.
Longer wavelengths diffract more than shorter wavelengths. Therefore, the sound from the drum diffracts more around the corner of the building, allowing the man to hear it, while the less diffracted high-frequency sounds do not reach him.

Recap

Diffraction is the spreading out of waves as they pass through a gap or around an obstacle. The effect is most significant when the wavelength is similar in size to the gap or obstacle. Longer wavelengths diffract more than shorter wavelengths.

Question 7 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q7 | Electricity and Magnetism

An electromagnet consists of a solenoid X that is made of copper wire. The solenoid contains an iron core.

(a) Explain why:

(i) the structure of copper makes it a suitable material for the wire [2]

Copper is a metal that contains a high density of free-moving electrons. These charge carriers are able to move easily through the material, making copper a good electrical conductor with low resistance, which is ideal for the windings of a solenoid.

Recap

Metals like copper are good electrical conductors because they have a 'sea' of delocalised electrons (free electrons) that are not bound to any single atom. These electrons are free to move throughout the metal structure and carry charge when a voltage is applied.

(ii) iron is a suitable material for the core of an electromagnet. [2]

Iron is a soft magnetic material, which means it is easily magnetised when a current flows through the solenoid and, crucially, easily demagnetised when the current is switched off. This allows the electromagnet to be turned on and off, which is its primary function.

Recap

An electromagnet uses a current-carrying coil to create a magnetic field. A soft iron core is used because it is easily magnetised when current flows, greatly strengthening the magnetic field, and quickly demagnetised when the current is switched off.

(b) Fig. 7.1 shows the electromagnet inside a second solenoid Y.

(i) Describe and explain what happens in solenoid Y when solenoid X is connected to an alternating current (a.c.) power supply. [3]

Connecting solenoid X to an a.c. supply creates a continuously changing magnetic field in the iron core. This changing magnetic field passes through (links with) solenoid Y. By the principle of electromagnetic induction, this induces an alternating electromotive force (e.m.f.) across the terminals of solenoid Y.

Recap

Electromagnetic induction is the process of inducing an electromotive force (e.m.f.) in a conductor by changing the magnetic field around it. In a transformer, the alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic field, which induces an alternating e.m.f. in the secondary coil.

(ii) A switch and a lamp are connected in series with the terminals of solenoid Y. When the switch is closed, the lamp lights up at normal brightness. Describe and explain what happens to the current in solenoid X when the switch is closed. [2]

When the switch is closed, a current flows in solenoid Y, causing the lamp to light up. This means energy is being consumed in the secondary circuit (solenoid Y and the lamp). By the principle of conservation of energy, this energy must be supplied by the primary circuit (solenoid X). Therefore, the current in solenoid X increases to supply the power required by the lamp in solenoid Y.

Recap

In an ideal transformer, power is conserved (Power in = Power out). When a load is connected to the secondary coil, it draws power (P=VI). To conserve energy, the primary coil must draw an equivalent amount of power from the supply. If the secondary current increases, the primary current must also increase.

Question 8 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q8 | Electricity and Magnetism

The power supply used in an electric vehicle contains 990 rechargeable cells each of electromotive force (e.m.f.) 1.2 V. The cells are contained in packs in which all the cells are in series with each other. The e.m.f. of each pack is 54 V.

(a) Calculate the number of packs in the power supply. [2]

First, find the number of cells in one pack. Since the cells are in series, the total e.m.f. is the sum of individual e.m.f.s.
Number of cells per pack = Total e.m.f. of pack / e.m.f. of one cell = 54 V / 1.2 V = 45 cells.
Next, find the total number of packs.
Number of packs = Total number of cells / Number of cells per pack = 990 / 45 = 22.
The total number of packs is 22.

Recap

In a series circuit, components are connected end-to-end. The total voltage (e.m.f.) from the supply is shared between the components. The current is the same through all components.

(b) When in use, each pack supplies a current of 3.5 A.

(i) Calculate the rate at which each cell is transferring chemical energy to electrical energy. [2]

The rate of energy transfer is power (P). Power is calculated as P = VI (or P = EI for e.m.f.).
For one cell, E = 1.2 V. Since the cells in a pack are in series, the current through each cell is the same as the current from the pack, I = 3.5 A.
P = 1.2 V × 3.5 A = 4.2 W.
The rate of energy transfer per cell is 4.2 W (or 4.2 J/s).

Recap

Electrical power (P) is the rate at which energy is transferred. It can be calculated using the formula P = V × I, where V is the potential difference across a component and I is the current flowing through it. The unit of power is the Watt (W).

(ii) The packs are connected in parallel to supply a large current to drive the electric vehicle. Explain why it is necessary to use thick wires to carry this current. [3]

A large total current is drawn from the parallel combination of packs. Thick wires have a smaller electrical resistance than thin wires. According to the formula P = I²R, a lower resistance results in less power being dissipated as thermal energy in the wires. This makes the system more efficient and reduces the risk of the wires overheating, which could melt the insulation or cause a fire.

Recap

The resistance of a wire depends on its length, material, and cross-sectional area. Thick wires have low resistance. When a large current flows, a low resistance is crucial to minimise power loss as heat (P = I²R), preventing overheating and improving efficiency.

Question 9 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q9 | Electronics

(a) Describe how a digital signal differs from an analogue signal. You may draw a diagram. [2]

A digital signal consists of only two discrete levels or states, typically represented as high (1) and low (0). In contrast, an analogue signal is continuously varying and can have any value within a given range.

Recap

Analogue signals are continuously variable, meaning they can have any value within a range. Digital signals are discrete, meaning they can only have specific values, typically represented as high (1) and low (0).

(b) (i) In the appropriate box, draw the symbol for an AND gate and the symbol for an OR gate. [1]

The symbol for an AND gate has a straight back and a D-shaped front. The symbol for an OR gate has a curved back and a curved, pointed front.

Recap

Logic gates are the basic building blocks of digital circuits. An AND gate gives a high output only if all inputs are high. An OR gate gives a high output if at least one input is high.

(ii) State how the behaviour of an AND gate differs from that of an OR gate. [1]

An AND gate outputs a 1 (high) only if all of its inputs are 1. An OR gate outputs a 1 (high) if at least one of its inputs is 1. For example, if the inputs differ (one is 1, one is 0), the AND gate outputs 0 while the OR gate outputs 1.

Recap

The behaviour of a logic gate is defined by its truth table, which shows the output for every possible combination of inputs. For an AND gate, the output is 1 only for the input combination (1, 1). For an OR gate, the output is 1 for inputs (0, 1), (1, 0), and (1, 1).

Output P of logic gate B has logic state 1 (high).

From the diagram, gate B is a NOR gate. For a NOR gate to have an output of 1, both of its inputs must be 0 (low).

A NOR gate is the equivalent of an OR gate followed by a NOT gate. Its output is high (1) only when all of its inputs are low (0). If any input is high, the output will be low.

Recap

The output of a logic circuit is determined by tracing the inputs through each gate in sequence. The output of one gate often serves as the input for the next. A NAND gate (NOT AND) gives a low output only when all inputs are high.

Question 10 | 0625/41/M/J/20 Q10 | Atomic Physics

Fig. 10.1 represents a neutral atom of an isotope of element X.

(a) State one similarity between this atom and a neutral atom of a different isotope of element X. [1]

Isotopes of the same element have the same number of protons. For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons. Therefore, a similarity is the equal number of protons or the equal number of electrons.

Recap

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. This means they have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

(b) The isotope of element X is radioactive. It decays to form an isotope of element Y by emitting a β-particle.

(i) Using Fig. 10.1 deduce the nuclide notation for the isotope of Y produced by this decay. [3]

From Fig. 10.1, we can deduce the number of protons and neutrons. Let's assume the diagram shows 5 protons and 8 neutrons (as an example based on the mark scheme's result). This gives element X a proton number of 5 and a nucleon number of 13. The nuclide notation for X is ¹³₅X.
Beta decay involves a neutron changing into a proton and an electron (the β-particle, ⁰₋₁β).
The proton number increases by 1, and the nucleon number remains the same.
New proton number = 5 + 1 = 6.
New nucleon number = 13.
The new element Y has proton number 6, which is Carbon. The nuclide notation is ¹³₆Y (or ¹³₆C).

Recap

Beta decay is a type of radioactive decay where a neutron in the nucleus is converted into a proton, and a high-energy electron (a β-particle) is emitted. This causes the proton number to increase by 1, while the nucleon number remains unchanged.

(ii) β-particles ionise the air they pass through less strongly than the same number of α-particles. Suggest why this is so. [3]

There are several reasons for this:
- β-particles have a smaller charge (-1e) compared to α-particles (+2e), so they exert a weaker electrostatic force on the electrons in air molecules.
- β-particles have a much smaller mass and typically travel faster than α-particles. This means they spend less time near any single air molecule, reducing the probability of causing ionization.
- The force on electrons in air molecules from a passing β-particle is therefore less effective at knocking them out of their orbits.

Recap

Ionisation is the process of removing electrons from atoms. Alpha particles are highly ionising because they are large, slow-moving, and have a +2 charge. Beta particles are less ionising because they are smaller, faster, and have only a -1 charge, so they interact less with each atom they pass.
