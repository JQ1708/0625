Question 1

(a) Fig. 1.1 shows a piece of glass of thickness 2.0 cm and area 0.15 m2.
The density of the glass is 2.6 × 103 kg / m3.

Calculate the weight of the piece of glass.

First, calculate the volume of the glass:

V = Area × Thickness = 0.15 m2 × 0.02 m = 0.003 m3

Next, calculate the mass of the glass using the density:

m = Density × Volume = 2.6 × 103 kg/m3 × 0.003 m3 = 7.8 kg

Finally, calculate the weight:

W = Mass × Gravitational Field Strength (g) = 7.8 kg × 10 N/kg = 78 N

Density is a measure of how much mass is contained in a given volume. The formula for density is: density = mass ÷ volume.

An object will sink if its density is greater than the density of the liquid it is in. If it has a lower density, it will float.

(b) The piece of glass shown in Fig. 1.1 is used as the vertical viewing window of an aquarium.
The atmospheric pressure outside the aquarium is 1.0 × 105 Pa. The average pressure on the inside of the aquarium window is 1.3 × 105 Pa.
Calculate the resultant force acting on the window due to these pressures and state the direction in which it acts.

First, calculate the pressure difference:

ΔP = Pinside - Poutside = 1.3 × 105 Pa - 1.0 × 105 Pa = 3.0 × 104 Pa

Next, calculate the resultant force:

F = Pressure Difference × Area = 3.0 × 104 Pa × 0.15 m2 = 4500 N = 4.5 × 103 N

The direction of the force is outwards, because the pressure inside is greater than the pressure outside.

The pressure on a surface is the force acting perpendicularly per unit area. The formula is Pressure = Force ÷ Area. The unit of pressure is the Pascal (Pa), which is equivalent to N/m2.

(c) Fig. 1.2 shows a vacuum pump connected to the top of a vertical tube with its lower end immersed in a tank of liquid. The pump reduces the pressure above the column to zero and the pressure at point X is 9.6 × 104 Pa.

Calculate the density of the liquid.

The pressure at point X is due to the column of liquid above it. We can use the formula for pressure in a liquid:

P = ρ × g × h

Where:
P = Pressure = 9.6 × 104 Pa
g = Gravitational Field Strength = 10 N/kg
h = Height of the liquid column = 12 m

Rearranging the formula to find the density (ρ):

ρ = P / (g × h) = 9.6 × 104 / (10 × 12) = 800 kg/m3

The pressure below the surface of a liquid is caused by the weight of the liquid column above it. It can be calculated using the formula: Pressure = height of the column × density of the liquid × gravitational field strength (P = hρg).

The moment of a force is the turning effect of the force about a pivot or point.

The moment of a force is a measure of its turning effect. The greater the force, the greater the moment, and the further the force acts from the pivot, the greater the moment.

Question 3

(a) A power station burns waste materials from farm crops to generate electricity. State and explain whether this process is renewable.

This process is renewable.

This is because the farm crops can be regrown in a relatively short period of time, so the source of the fuel will not run out.

A renewable energy resource is one that will not run out, or can be replenished in a short period of time. Examples include solar, wind, and wave power. A non-renewable energy resource is one that will eventually run out, such as fossil fuels (coal, oil, and gas) and nuclear fuel.

(b) The power station uses some of its waste thermal energy to heat water for houses in a nearby town. State one problem of using waste energy in this way if the power station is far from the town. Suggest a way of reducing this problem.

Problem: A significant amount of thermal energy will be lost to the surroundings as the hot water travels through the pipes from the power station to the town.

Solution: To reduce this heat loss, the pipes should be insulated. This can be done by lagging the pipes with a material that is a poor conductor of thermal energy.

Insulation is used to reduce the rate of heat transfer. Materials used for insulation, such as fibreglass, work by trapping air. Air is a poor conductor of heat, so this reduces heat loss by conduction. The trapped air also prevents the formation of convection currents.

(c) State two environmental consequences of burning coal to generate electricity.

1. The burning of coal releases sulfur dioxide, which dissolves in water in the atmosphere to form acid rain. Acid rain can damage buildings, harm forests, and make lakes and rivers too acidic for aquatic life.

2. The combustion of coal also releases large amounts of carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas. The increase in greenhouse gases in the atmosphere contributes to global warming and climate change.

Burning fossil fuels like coal, oil, and natural gas has significant environmental impacts. These include air pollution from the release of harmful gases, the formation of acid rain, and the contribution to the greenhouse effect and global warming.

Question 4

(a) In terms of the momentum of molecules, explain how a gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container.

The molecules of a gas are in continuous, random motion. When a molecule collides with a wall of the container, it rebounds, causing a change in its momentum. According to Newton's second law, a change in momentum results in a force being exerted on the molecule by the wall. By Newton's third law, the molecule exerts an equal and opposite force on the wall. The pressure exerted by the gas is the total force exerted by all the molecules colliding with the walls per unit area.

Gas pressure is caused by the collisions of gas particles with the walls of the container. When a particle collides with the wall, its momentum changes. This change in momentum creates a force on the wall. The pressure is the total force per unit area.

(b) (i) A fixed mass of gas of volume V1 is at a pressure p1. It is compressed to a volume V2. Complete the equation for the final pressure p2 of the gas when the gas is compressed at constant temperature.

p2 =

p2 = (p1V1) / V2

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume. This is known as Boyle's Law. This means that if you double the pressure, you halve the volume. The relationship can be summarized by the equation: p1V1 = p2V2.

(b) (ii) State and explain how the final pressure compares with p2 when the temperature of the gas increases during compression.

The final pressure will be greater than p2.

This is because an increase in temperature means the gas molecules have a higher average kinetic energy and are moving faster. This leads to more frequent and more forceful collisions with the container walls, resulting in a higher pressure.

The pressure of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (in Kelvin), provided the volume is kept constant. This is known as the Pressure Law (or Gay-Lussac's Law). Increasing the temperature increases the kinetic energy of the gas particles, causing them to move faster and collide with the container walls more often and with more force.

Question 5

(a) State the name of the reflection of a sound wave or ultrasound wave.

An echo.

An echo is the reflection of a sound wave. When a sound wave hits a hard, flat surface, it bounces back. This reflected sound is what we hear as an echo. Echoes can be used to determine the speed of sound and for applications like sonar and ultrasound imaging.

(b) Fig. 5.1 shows an ultrasound wave being used to scan an internal organ of a human body.

The ultrasound wave has a frequency of 2.0 MHz and passes through human tissue at a speed of 1500 m / s. Calculate the wavelength of the ultrasound wave in human tissue.

We can use the wave equation to solve this problem:

v = f × λ

Where:
v = speed = 1500 m/s
f = frequency = 2.0 MHz = 2.0 × 106 Hz

Rearranging the formula to find the wavelength (λ):

λ = v / f = 1500 / (2.0 × 106) = 0.00075 m = 7.5 × 10-4 m

The wave speed (v) is related to its frequency (f) and wavelength (λ) by the wave equation: speed = frequency × wavelength (v = fλ). This equation applies to all types of waves.

  3. A ray through the principal focus, which refracts parallel to the principal axis.

Question 7

(a) Fig. 7.1 shows a horizontal conducting wire XY between two opposite magnetic poles. Wire XY forms a circuit with an ammeter. Explain why the reading on the ammeter is zero when the wire XY is not moving.

When the wire XY is not moving, it is not cutting magnetic field lines (or magnetic flux). For electromagnetic induction to occur and induce a current, there must be relative motion between the conductor and the magnetic field. Since there is no relative motion, no electromotive force (e.m.f.) is induced, and therefore no current flows, resulting in a zero reading on the ammeter.

Electromagnetic induction is the process of inducing an electromotive force (and thus an electric current) in a conductor by changing the magnetic field around it. This can be achieved by moving a conductor through a magnetic field, or by changing the magnetic field strength around a stationary conductor. The key is that the conductor must "cut" the magnetic field lines.

(b) The wire XY is moved and there is a deflection on the ammeter that indicates there is a current in the wire from X to Y. On Table 7.1, tick one box to indicate the direction of the movement of the wire XY and explain your answer.

The magnetic field lines go from North (N) to South (S), which is from left to right in the diagram. The induced current is from X to Y (into the page).

Using Fleming's Right-Hand Rule (for generators):

  • Thumb: Direction of Motion
  • Forefinger: Direction of Field (N to S)
  • Middle finger: Direction of Current

If the forefinger points right (field) and the middle finger points into the page (current), then the thumb points to the top of the page.

Fleming's Right-Hand Rule is used to determine the direction of the induced current when a conductor moves in a magnetic field. The thumb represents the direction of motion of the conductor, the forefinger represents the direction of the magnetic field (North to South), and the middle finger represents the direction of the induced current.

(c) State what is observed on the ammeter when the wire XY is moved (i) in the opposite direction to part (b) (ii) in the same direction as part (b) but at a greater speed.

(i) When the wire XY is moved in the opposite direction, the direction of the induced current will be reversed. This will cause the ammeter to show a deflection in the opposite direction.

(ii) When the wire XY is moved at a greater speed, the rate at which the magnetic field lines are cut increases. This will induce a larger electromotive force (e.m.f.) and thus a greater current. The ammeter will show a larger deflection.

The magnitude of the induced current depends on the rate at which magnetic field lines are cut. Moving the conductor faster or using a stronger magnetic field will increase the induced current. The direction of the induced current depends on the direction of motion of the conductor relative to the magnetic field, as described by Fleming's Right-Hand Rule.

Question 8

(a) Define electromotive force (e.m.f.).

Electromotive force (e.m.f.) is the energy supplied by a source (e.g., a battery or generator) in driving a unit charge (1 Coulomb) around a complete circuit. It is measured in Volts (V), which is equivalent to Joules per Coulomb (J/C).

Electromotive force (e.m.f.) is the work done by the energy source per unit charge in moving charge around a complete circuit. It is the potential difference across the terminals of the source when no current is flowing. It is distinct from potential difference (p.d.), which is the work done per unit charge between two points in a circuit.

(b) (i) Fig. 8.1 shows a source E of e.m.f. 60 V in a circuit. The heater H has a resistance of 22.5 Ω and the potential difference (p.d.) across it is 45 V. Calculate the power of the heater.

First, calculate the current (I) flowing through the heater using Ohm's Law (V = IR), rearranged to I = V/R:

I = 45 V / 22.5 Ω = 2 A

Next, calculate the power (P) of the heater using the formula P = VI:

P = 45 V × 2 A = 90 W

Alternatively, using P = V2/R:

P = (45 V)2 / 22.5 Ω = 2025 / 22.5 = 90 W

Electrical power (P) is the rate at which electrical energy is converted into other forms of energy (e.g., heat, light). It can be calculated using the formulas: P = VI (Power = Voltage × Current), P = I2R (Power = Current2 × Resistance), or P = V2/R (Power = Voltage2 / Resistance).

(b) (ii) Calculate the p.d. across resistor X.

In a series circuit, the total potential difference supplied by the source (e.m.f.) is divided among the components. Therefore, the sum of the potential differences across each component is equal to the e.m.f. of the source.

p.d. across X = e.m.f. - p.d. across H

p.d. across X = 60 V - 45 V = 15 V

In a series circuit, components are connected end-to-end, forming a single path for current. The potential difference (voltage) across the entire circuit is the sum of the potential differences across each individual component. The current is the same at all points in a series circuit.

(b) (iii) Calculate the current in the 10 Ω resistor.

Since the 10 Ω resistor is in series with resistor X, the current flowing through both components is the same. We can use Ohm's Law (I = V/R) to calculate the current through the 10 Ω resistor, using the potential difference across it (which is the same as across X, calculated in part (ii)).

I = V / R = 15 V / 10 Ω = 1.5 A

Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points. The constant of proportionality is the resistance. The formula is V = IR, where V is voltage, I is current, and R is resistance. In a series circuit, the current is the same through all components.

Input A Input B Output

Logic gates are elementary building blocks of digital circuits. They take one or more binary inputs (0 or 1) and produce a single binary output. An OR gate is a type of logic gate that produces a high output (1) if any of its inputs are high (1). If all inputs are low (0), then the output is low (0).

(b) Draw the symbol for a NOR gate.

The symbol for a NOR gate is the same as an OR gate, but with a small circle on the output, which represents inversion.

A NOR gate is a combination of an OR gate followed by a NOT gate. Its output is true (1) only if all its inputs are false (0). Otherwise, the output is false (0).

Question 10

(a) State what is meant by the half-life of a radioactive isotope.

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay, or for the activity of the sample to halve.

The half-life is a measure of the stability of a radioactive isotope. It is a constant value for a given isotope and is not affected by external factors such as temperature, pressure, or chemical environment. After one half-life, half of the original radioactive atoms remain; after two half-lives, one-quarter remain, and so on.

(b) A sample of a radioactive isotope has an initial activity of 1200 Bq. After 24 hours, the activity is 150 Bq. Calculate the half-life of the isotope.

We start with an initial activity of 1200 Bq and end with 150 Bq after 24 hours. We need to determine how many half-lives have passed.

  • After 1 half-life: 1200 Bq / 2 = 600 Bq
  • After 2 half-lives: 600 Bq / 2 = 300 Bq
  • After 3 half-lives: 300 Bq / 2 = 150 Bq

So, 3 half-lives have passed in 24 hours.

Therefore, the half-life of the isotope is:

Half-life = Total time / Number of half-lives = 24 hours / 3 = 8 hours

The half-life of a radioactive isotope can be determined by observing the decrease in its activity over time. Each time the activity halves, one half-life has passed. By counting the number of times the activity halves and knowing the total time elapsed, the half-life can be calculated.

(c) State two precautions that should be taken when handling radioactive isotopes.

When handling radioactive isotopes, it is crucial to minimize exposure to radiation. Here are two important precautions:

  1. Use shielding: Place appropriate shielding materials (e.g., lead, concrete) between yourself and the radioactive source to absorb radiation. The type and thickness of shielding depend on the type and energy of the radiation.
  2. Minimize time of exposure: Reduce the amount of time spent near the radioactive source. The total radiation dose received is directly proportional to the time of exposure.
  3. Maximize distance: Increase your distance from the radioactive source. Radiation intensity decreases significantly with distance (inverse square law).
  4. Use remote handling tools: Use tongs or robotic arms to handle radioactive materials, keeping them as far away from your body as possible.
  5. Wear protective clothing: While not always effective against penetrating radiation, protective clothing can prevent contamination from radioactive dust or liquids.

The fundamental principles of radiation protection are "Time, Distance, Shielding." Time refers to minimizing the duration of exposure. Distance refers to maximizing the separation from the source. Shielding refers to using materials to absorb radiation. These principles help to reduce the risk of harm from ionizing radiation.
