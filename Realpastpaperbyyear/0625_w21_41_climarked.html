0625/41/O/N/21 Q1

(a) State how a vector quantity differs from a scalar quantity.

A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction, whereas a scalar quantity only has magnitude.

Recap

Physical quantities can be classified as either scalar or vector. A scalar quantity is completely specified by its magnitude (a number and a unit). Examples include distance, speed, mass, and energy. A vector quantity requires both magnitude and direction to be fully described. Examples include displacement, velocity, force, and momentum.

(b) Circle the vector quantities in the list.

acceleration energy mass momentum temperature time speed velocity

The vector quantities are: acceleration, momentum, and velocity.

Recap

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity (a vector). Momentum is the product of mass (a scalar) and velocity (a vector). Velocity is the rate of change of displacement (a vector). Energy, mass, temperature, time, and speed are all scalar quantities.

(c) A microphone in a recording studio has a mass of 0.55 kg and a weight W.

(i) Calculate W.

Weight is calculated using the formula W = mg, where m is mass and g is the acceleration of free fall.
W = 0.55 kg × 10 N/kg = 5.5 N.

Recap

Weight is the force of gravity acting on an object. It is a vector quantity, directed towards the center of the Earth. Mass is the amount of matter in an object and is a scalar quantity. The acceleration of free fall (g) is taken as 10 m/s² or 10 N/kg on Earth.

(ii) The microphone is suspended from the ceiling by a cord attached to a small ring. Fig. 1.1 shows the microphone pulled to one side and kept stationary by a horizontal thread.

The tension T in the horizontal thread is 8.1 N.

Determine graphically the magnitude and the direction, relative to the vertical, of the resultant of W and T. Use a scale of 1.0 cm to 1.0 N or greater.

To find the resultant force graphically, we draw the vectors head-to-tail. Using a scale of 1 cm = 1 N:
1. Draw the weight vector W vertically downwards, with a length of 5.5 cm.
2. From the head of the weight vector, draw the tension vector T horizontally, with a length of 8.1 cm.
3. The resultant vector is the arrow drawn from the tail of W to the head of T.
By measuring the length of the resultant vector, the magnitude is approximately 9.8 N.
By measuring the angle between the resultant and the vertical, the direction is approximately 56° relative to the vertical.

Recap

The resultant force is the single force that has the same effect as all the individual forces acting on an object combined. When forces are in equilibrium, the resultant force is zero. In this case, the resultant of W and T is balanced by the tension in the cord.

(iii) State and explain how the magnitude and direction of the resultant in (c)(ii) compares with the force on the ring due to the tension in the cord.

The force on the ring due to the tension in the cord is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the resultant of W and T. This is because the ring is in equilibrium, meaning the net force on it is zero. The tension in the cord must therefore balance the combined effect of the weight and the horizontal tension.

Recap

Newton's First Law states that an object will remain at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by a resultant force. For an object to be in equilibrium, the vector sum of all forces acting on it must be zero. This means all the forces are balanced.

0625/41/O/N/21 Q2

(a) Define thermal capacity.

Thermal capacity is the amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of an object by 1 °C.

Recap

Thermal capacity (or heat capacity) is a measure of how much heat energy is needed to change a substance's temperature. It is an extensive property, meaning it depends on the mass of the object. The unit for thermal capacity is Joules per degree Celsius (J/°C).

(b) Several ice cubes are at a temperature of 0 °C. The ice cubes are dropped into the water and the internal energy of the water decreases.

(i) Give a simple molecular account of this decrease in internal energy.

The internal energy of the water depends on the kinetic energy of its molecules. As the water cools, its molecules slow down, which means their average kinetic energy decreases, leading to a decrease in the water's internal energy.

Recap

The internal energy of a substance is the sum of the random distribution of kinetic and potential energies of its atoms or molecules. For a liquid, the kinetic energy is related to the motion of the molecules, and the potential energy is related to the bonds between them. A decrease in temperature primarily signifies a decrease in the average kinetic energy of the molecules.

(ii) The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg °C). Calculate the decrease in the internal energy of the water as its temperature decreases from 17 °C to 0 °C.

The decrease in internal energy is calculated using the formula ΔE = mcΔT.
ΔE = 0.24 kg × 4200 J/(kg °C) × (17 °C - 0 °C)
ΔE = 17136 J, which is approximately 1.7 × 10⁴ J.

Recap

Specific heat capacity (c) is the amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. The formula for thermal energy change is ΔE = mcΔT, where m is the mass and ΔT is the change in temperature.

(c) As the temperature of the water decreases, some of the ice melts.

(i) Explain why this ice melts.

The ice melts because it absorbs thermal energy from the warmer water. This absorbed energy is the latent heat of fusion, which is used to overcome intermolecular forces and break the bonds holding the water molecules in a fixed solid structure, causing a change of state from solid to liquid.

Recap

Latent heat is the energy absorbed or released by a substance during a change of state that occurs without changing its temperature. The specific latent heat of fusion is the energy needed to melt 1 kg of a substance at its melting point.

(ii) Describe how to determine the specific latent heat of fusion of ice using this experiment. State any other measurements that the student needs to make.

To determine the specific latent heat of fusion (Lf), the student must measure the mass of the ice that has melted (m). This can be done by measuring the increase in the mass of the water in the beaker. The energy (E) absorbed by the ice is the energy lost by the water, calculated in (b)(ii). The specific latent heat is then calculated using the formula Lf = E / m. It is also important to ensure the ice is dry before adding it and to insulate the beaker to minimize heat exchange with the surroundings.

Recap

An experiment to measure the specific latent heat of fusion of ice typically involves a calorimeter, a known mass of water, a thermometer, and a means of measuring the mass of melted ice. The principle is that the heat lost by the cooling water is equal to the heat gained by the melting ice. The formula is Lf = (m_water * c_water * ΔT_water) / m_ice.

0625/41/O/N/21 Q3

(a) Explain, in terms of the momentum of the molecules, why there is a pressure at the inner surface of the balloon.

The air molecules inside the balloon are in constant, random motion. These molecules collide with the inner surface of the balloon. When a molecule collides with the wall, its momentum changes (it reverses direction). This change in momentum results in a force being exerted on the wall. The pressure is the total force per unit area from these continuous collisions.

Recap

The kinetic theory of gases explains the macroscopic properties of gases by considering the motion of their constituent molecules. Gas pressure is a result of the countless collisions of gas molecules with the walls of their container. Each collision exerts a small force, and the cumulative effect of these forces creates the pressure.

(b) The volume of the air in the balloon is 630 cm³ and the pressure of the air in the balloon is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. The balloon is tied to a heavy stone and dropped into a lake. The balloon is pulled down quickly and the temperature of the air inside does not change.

(i) Calculate the volume of the air when the pressure of the air is 1.4 × 10⁵ Pa.

Since the temperature is constant, we can use Boyle's Law: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂.
(1.0 × 10⁵ Pa) × (630 cm³) = (1.4 × 10⁵ Pa) × V₂
V₂ = (1.0 × 10⁵ × 630) / (1.4 × 10⁵)
V₂ = 450 cm³.

Recap

Boyle's Law states that for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume (p ∝ 1/V). The formula is p₁V₁ = p₂V₂.

(ii) The balloon and stone stop moving when the stone hits the bottom of the lake. The temperature of the air now begins to decrease. Explain why the volume of the air in the balloon decreases as the temperature decreases.

As the temperature of the air inside the balloon decreases, the molecules move more slowly and have less kinetic energy. This leads to less frequent and less forceful collisions with the balloon's inner wall, causing the internal pressure to decrease. The higher external water pressure then compresses the balloon, reducing its volume until the internal and external pressures are balanced again.

Recap

The Pressure Law (or Gay-Lussac's Law) states that for a fixed mass of gas at constant volume, the pressure is directly proportional to the absolute temperature (p ∝ T). Similarly, Charles's Law states that for a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, the volume is directly proportional to the absolute temperature (V ∝ T). In this scenario, both pressure and volume are changing in response to the temperature drop.

The train continues at a speed of 20 m/s for 10 s. At t = 25 s, the driver applies the brakes and the resistive force on the train causes it to decelerate uniformly to rest in a further 24 s.

(a) Complete Fig. 4.1 by drawing:

(i) a line to represent the motion of the train between t = 15 s and t = 25 s.

(ii) a curve to represent the motion of the train between t = 0 and t = 15 s.

(ii) Between t = 0 and t = 15 s, the train is accelerating. On a distance-time graph, acceleration is represented by a curve with an increasing gradient. The curve should start from the origin and become steeper as it approaches t = 15 s.

Recap

On a distance-time graph:
- A horizontal line means the object is stationary.
- A straight line with a constant positive gradient means constant speed.
- A curve with an increasing gradient means acceleration.
- A curve with a decreasing gradient means deceleration.

(b) Calculate the kinetic energy of the train between t = 15 s and t = 25 s.

The kinetic energy is calculated using the formula Ek = ½mv².
Ek = ½ × (1.8 × 10⁵ kg) × (20 m/s)²
Ek = 0.5 × 1.8 × 10⁵ × 400
Ek = 3.6 × 10⁷ J.

Recap

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses due to its motion. It depends on the mass of the object and the square of its speed. The unit of energy is the Joule (J).

(c) While the train decelerates to rest, it does work against the resistive force and its kinetic energy decreases.

(i) Define work done.

Work done is the product of the force and the distance moved in the direction of the force.

Recap

The formula for work done is W = F × d. Work is a transfer of energy. When work is done against a resistive force like friction, kinetic energy is converted into thermal energy, causing the temperature of the objects to increase.

(ii) Using Fig. 4.1, determine the distance moved by the train while it decelerates.

The train decelerates from t = 25 s for 24 s, so it stops at t = 49 s. The distance moved during deceleration is the area under the speed-time graph, or can be read from the completed distance-time graph. The distance moved is approximately 240 m.

Recap

For an object decelerating uniformly to rest, the distance travelled can be calculated using the equation of motion v² = u² + 2as, or by finding the area of the triangle under a speed-time graph: distance = ½ × base × height = ½ × time × initial speed.

(iii) Calculate the resultant force acting on the train while it decelerates.

First, calculate the deceleration: a = Δv / t = (0 - 20 m/s) / 24 s = -0.833 m/s².
Then, use Newton's Second Law, F = ma.
F = (1.8 × 10⁵ kg) × (-0.833 m/s²)
F = -1.5 × 10⁵ N. The negative sign indicates the force is in the opposite direction to the motion.

Recap

Newton's Second Law of Motion states that the resultant force acting on an object is directly proportional to the rate of change of its momentum. For an object with constant mass, this simplifies to F = ma, where F is the resultant force, m is the mass, and a is the acceleration.

0625/41/O/N/21 Q5

(a) Explain, in terms of the behaviour of light rays, what is meant by principal focus for a thin converging lens.

The principal focus is the point on the principal axis where rays of light that are initially parallel to the principal axis meet (or converge) after passing through the lens.

Recap

A converging lens (or convex lens) is thicker in the middle than at the edges. It refracts parallel light rays so that they come together at a single point, the principal focus (or focal point). A diverging (concave) lens causes parallel rays to spread out as if they came from a virtual principal focus.

(b) State what is meant by focal length.

The focal length is the distance between the principal focus and the optical centre of the lens.

Recap

The optical centre is the point at the exact center of the lens. Light rays passing through the optical centre are not deviated. The focal length is a key property of a lens that determines its focusing power.

(c) A lens is used to produce a focused image of an object on a translucent screen. Fig. 5.1 shows the object O and its image I.

(i) Consider the straight ray that passes from the tip of O to the tip of I and find the position of the lens. Mark the position of the lens by drawing a vertical line labelled L from the top of the grid to the bottom.

A ray of light that passes through the optical centre of the lens is undeviated. By drawing a straight line from the tip of the object O to the tip of the image I, the point where this line intersects the principal axis is the location of the optical centre. The lens (L) is a vertical line passing through this point, perpendicular to the principal axis. Based on the grid, this line is 4.0 cm to the right of the object O.

Recap

Ray diagrams are used to locate the image formed by a lens. Three principal rays are typically used:
1. A ray parallel to the principal axis, which refracts through the principal focus.
2. A ray passing through the optical centre, which continues undeviated.
3. A ray passing through the principal focus, which refracts to become parallel to the principal axis.

(ii) On Fig. 5.1, draw a ray that passes through one of the principal focuses and determine the focal length of the lens.

Draw a ray from the tip of the object O, parallel to the principal axis, until it hits the lens line L. From this point on the lens, draw a line that passes through the tip of the image I. The point where this refracted ray crosses the principal axis is the principal focus (F). The distance from the optical centre to this point is the focal length. The focal length is 3.0 cm.

Recap

The lens formula, 1/f = 1/u + 1/v, relates the focal length (f), the object distance (u), and the image distance (v). In this case, u = 4.0 cm and v = 12.0 cm.
1/f = 1/4.0 + 1/12.0 = 3/12 + 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3.
Therefore, f = 3.0 cm, confirming the result from the ray diagram.

(iii) Object O is a printed document that includes a large letter R on the side facing the lens. The top edge of the document corresponds to the tip of O. Fig. 5.2 shows the printed document.

On Fig. 5.3, mark a tick in one of the boxes to indicate how the image on the translucent screen appears to someone who is looking at the screen from point P. Explain why the image has this appearance.

The image formed by a single converging lens of a real object is inverted both vertically and horizontally. Therefore, the letter R will appear upside down and back-to-front. The correct box to tick is the fourth one. The image is reversed and inverted.

Recap

A real image formed by a single convex lens is always inverted. This means it is upside down (vertically inverted) and laterally inverted (left-right reversed). This is why the image in a simple camera or a projector is inverted.

0625/41/O/N/21 Q6

X-rays are electromagnetic waves. Fig. 6.1 shows the position of X-rays in the electromagnetic spectrum arranged according to increasing wavelength.

(a) Three components of the spectrum are unnamed but labelled J, K and L.

(i) State the names of these three components.

J is ultraviolet (UV) radiation.
K is infrared (IR) radiation.
L is radio waves.

Recap

The electromagnetic spectrum, in order of increasing wavelength, is: Gamma rays, X-rays, Ultraviolet, Visible light, Infrared, Microwaves, Radio waves. A common mnemonic is "Raging Martians Invaded Venus Using X-ray Guns".

(ii) State which of these three components has the lowest frequency.

L (radio waves) has the lowest frequency. This is because it has the longest wavelength, and frequency is inversely proportional to wavelength (c = fλ).

Recap

The relationship between the speed (c), frequency (f), and wavelength (λ) of an electromagnetic wave is given by the wave equation: c = fλ. Since the speed of light (c) is constant in a vacuum, frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional. High frequency corresponds to short wavelength and high energy.

(b) Calculate the frequency of X-rays that have a wavelength of 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m in a vacuum.

Using the wave equation c = fλ, where c (the speed of light) is 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.
f = c / λ
f = (3.0 × 10⁸ m/s) / (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m)
f = 2.5 × 10¹⁸ Hz.

Recap

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum, the speed of light, which is approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. The unit of frequency is Hertz (Hz), which is one cycle per second.

(c) (i) Describe one medical use of X-rays.

One medical use is in X-ray shadow photography. X-rays are passed through a patient's body onto a detector. They are absorbed by dense materials like bone but pass through soft tissues. This creates a shadow image, or radiograph, which can be used to diagnose fractures or other conditions.

Recap

Other medical uses of X-rays include CT scans (Computed Tomography) for creating detailed 3D images of the body, and in radiotherapy to treat cancer by killing malignant cells. The high energy of X-rays makes them effective at destroying cells.

(ii) State one reason why it is necessary to take safety precautions when X-rays are used.

X-rays are ionising radiation, which means they can damage living cells or cause cell mutation. This can lead to conditions such as cancer or cause burns.

Recap

Ionising radiation has enough energy to remove electrons from atoms or molecules, creating ions. This can disrupt biological processes and damage DNA. Safety precautions include limiting exposure time, increasing distance from the source, and using shielding (e.g., lead aprons).

0625/41/O/N/21 Q7

(a) Describe, in terms of particles, how the rod becomes negatively charged when rubbed with a woollen cloth.

When the plastic rod is rubbed with the cloth, friction provides energy. This causes negatively charged particles called electrons to move from the cloth to the rod. The rod gains an excess of electrons, making it negatively charged, while the cloth is left with a net positive charge.

Recap

Charging by friction (triboelectric effect) occurs when two different materials are rubbed together. One material has a stronger affinity for electrons than the other, so electrons are transferred from one to the other. Protons are in the nucleus and are not transferred in this process. Only electrons move.

(b) A light, conducting ball is at rest on a metal table. When the rod is brought close to the ball, as shown in Fig. 7.1, the ball jumps up towards the rod.

(i) Explain why the ball jumps up.

The negatively charged rod induces a charge on the conducting ball. The electrons in the ball are repelled by the rod and move away, leaving the top of the ball with a net positive charge. There is a force of attraction between the opposite charges (the negative rod and the positive top of the ball) which is strong enough to overcome the weight of the ball, causing it to jump up.

Recap

Electrostatic induction is the process of redistributing the charge within an object by bringing a charged object close to it. In conductors, free electrons can move easily. The induced charge on the side of the conductor closer to the charged object is always opposite in sign.

(ii) The ball touches the rod and falls back down to the table. Explain why this happens.

When the ball touches the rod, electrons are transferred from the rod to the ball by conduction, so the ball also becomes negatively charged. Since both the rod and the ball now have the same type of charge, they repel each other. This repulsive force, combined with the ball's weight, causes it to fall back down.

Recap

Charging by conduction (or contact) involves the transfer of charge from one object to another when they touch. The object being charged acquires the same sign of charge as the charging object. The principle "like charges repel, unlike charges attract" governs the interaction.

0625/41/O/N/21 Q8

A circuit contains two fixed resistors and a light-dependent resistor (LDR). Fig. 8.1 shows that the power supply is a 9.0 V battery.

The current in the 450 Ω resistor is 0.012 A.

(a) State what is meant by electric current.

Electric current is the rate of flow of charge (or electrons).

Recap

Current (I) is defined by the equation I = Q/t, where Q is the amount of charge passing a point in a circuit in time t. The unit of current is the Ampere (A), which is one Coulomb of charge per second.

(b) The current in the LDR is I₁ and the current in the 800 Ω resistor is I₂. Complete the equation that relates the current in the 450 Ω resistor to I₁ and I₂.

current in the 450 Ω resistor = I₁ + I₂

Recap

Kirchhoff's First Law (the junction rule) states that the total current entering a junction must equal the total current leaving it. This is a statement of the conservation of charge. In this circuit, the current from the 450 Ω resistor splits at the junction to flow through the LDR (I₁) and the 800 Ω resistor (I₂).

(c) Calculate the power dissipated in the 800 Ω resistor.

1. Find the voltage across the 450 Ω resistor: V = IR = 0.012 A × 450 Ω = 5.4 V.
2. The resistors are in parallel, so the voltage across the 800 Ω resistor is the battery voltage minus the voltage across the 450 Ω resistor: V_parallel = 9.0 V - 5.4 V = 3.6 V.
3. Calculate the power dissipated in the 800 Ω resistor using P = V²/R.
P = (3.6 V)² / 800 Ω = 12.96 / 800 = 0.0162 W or 1.6 × 10⁻² W.

Recap

Electrical power (P) can be calculated using three main formulas:
1. P = VI (Power = Voltage × Current)
2. P = I²R (derived by substituting V = IR)
3. P = V²/R (derived by substituting I = V/R)
Choosing the right formula depends on which quantities are known.

(d) The brightness of the light that is incident on the LDR increases. Explain what happens to the potential difference (p.d.) across the 450 Ω resistor.

When the light brightness increases, the resistance of the LDR decreases. This causes the total resistance of the parallel combination to decrease. As a result, the total resistance of the entire circuit decreases. According to Ohm's law (I = V/R_total), a lower total resistance leads to a higher total current flowing from the battery. This is the current that flows through the 450 Ω resistor. Since the potential difference across the 450 Ω resistor is given by V = IR, and the current (I) through it has increased, the p.d. across the 450 Ω resistor increases.

Recap

A Light-Dependent Resistor (LDR) is a component whose resistance changes with light intensity. In bright light, its resistance is low; in the dark, its resistance is high. LDRs are often used in potential divider circuits to create light-sensitive switches, for example, in automatic street lighting.

0625/41/O/N/21 Q9

(a) Describe the composition and structure of a neutral atom of uranium-235.

A neutral atom of uranium-235 consists of a very small, dense nucleus at its center. The nucleus contains protons and neutrons. Specifically, uranium has 92 protons, and uranium-235 has 235 - 92 = 143 neutrons. Surrounding the nucleus are 92 electrons orbiting in shells. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

Recap

In nuclide notation (AZX), Z is the proton number (atomic number), A is the nucleon number (mass number), and X is the chemical symbol. The number of neutrons is A - Z. The proton number defines the element.

(b) Another isotope of uranium is uranium-238. Describe how an atom of uranium-238 differs from an atom of uranium-235.

An atom of uranium-238 has the same number of protons and electrons as uranium-235, but it has a different number of neutrons. Uranium-238 has 238 - 92 = 146 neutrons, which is three more neutrons than uranium-235.

Recap

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. This means they have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Because they have the same electron configuration, isotopes have identical chemical properties but may have different physical properties, such as stability (radioactivity).

(c) In the reactor in a nuclear power station, a nucleus of uranium-235 absorbs a slow-moving neutron and then undergoes nuclear fission. Two neutrons, a nucleus of xenon-140 (¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe) and a nucleus of an element represented by E are produced. Complete the equation for this fission reaction.

n + ²³⁵₉₂U → ¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe + E + 2n

To balance the nuclear equation, the total nucleon number and proton number must be conserved.
Nucleon number (top): 1 + 235 = 140 + A + 2(1) => 236 = 142 + A => A = 94.
Proton number (bottom): 0 + 92 = 54 + Z + 2(0) => 92 = 54 + Z => Z = 38.
So, element E is ⁹⁴₃₈Sr (Strontium-94).

Recap

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus into two smaller nuclei, along with the release of neutrons and a large amount of energy. In a nuclear reactor, this process is controlled to generate electricity. The conservation of nucleon number and proton number are fundamental principles in all nuclear reactions.

(d) Xenon-140 (¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe) is radioactive. It decays by β-emission to isotope Q. Determine:

(i) the proton number of Q.

(ii) the nucleon number of Q.

Beta (β) decay involves a neutron in the nucleus turning into a proton and an electron. The electron is ejected as a beta particle (⁰₋₁e).
The nucleon number (A) remains the same, and the proton number (Z) increases by 1.
¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe → ᴬ₂Q + ⁰₋₁e
(i) The proton number of Q is 54 + 1 = 55.
(ii) The nucleon number of Q is 140.

Recap

There are three main types of radioactive decay:
- Alpha (α) decay: A helium nucleus (⁴₂He) is emitted. A decreases by 4, Z decreases by 2.
- Beta (β) decay: An electron (⁰₋₁e) is emitted. A is unchanged, Z increases by 1.
- Gamma (γ) decay: High-energy electromagnetic radiation is emitted. A and Z are unchanged.
