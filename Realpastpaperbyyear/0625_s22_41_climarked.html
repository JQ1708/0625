Question 1

Topics: Motion, Speed-Time Graphs | Paper: 0625/41/M/J/22 Q1

A car of mass m is travelling along a straight, horizontal road at a constant speed v.

At time t = 0, the driver of the car sees an obstruction in the road ahead of the car and applies the brakes.

The car does not begin to decelerate at t = 0.

(a) Explain what is meant by deceleration. [2]

Deceleration is negative acceleration, which means the velocity of an object is decreasing. It is defined as the rate of change of velocity (or the change in velocity per unit time).

Recap

Recap: Acceleration is the increase in velocity per second, while deceleration is the decrease in velocity per second. Both are vector quantities. The formula for acceleration is:

acceleration (m/s²) = change in velocity (m/s)time (s)

a = v - ut

A negative value for 'a' indicates deceleration.

(b) Suggest one reason why the car does not begin to decelerate at t = 0. [1]

There is a delay between the driver seeing the obstruction and applying the brakes. This is due to the driver's reaction time.

Recap

Recap: The total stopping distance of a vehicle is a combination of "thinking distance" and "braking distance". The time taken for the driver to react is the reaction time. During this time, the car continues to move at its initial speed.

(c) Fig. 1.1 is the distance-time graph for the car from t = 0.

(i) State the property of a distance-time graph that corresponds to speed. [1]

The gradient (or slope) of the distance-time graph represents speed.

Recap

Recap: On a distance-time graph, the gradient (steepness) of the line indicates the speed. A steeper gradient means a higher speed, and a horizontal line (gradient of zero) means the object has stopped.

The gradient of the graph = y stepx step = speed

(ii) Using Fig. 1.1, determine the initial speed v of the car. [2]

To find the initial speed, we calculate the gradient of the graph during the reaction time (the initial straight-line part).
Using a point on the line, e.g., at t = 0.5 s, distance = 11 m.
Speed = Gradient = change in distancechange in time = 11 m0.5 s = 22 m/s.
(Any value between 20.5 and 23.5 m/s is acceptable).

Recap

Recap: To calculate the speed from a distance-time graph, you find the gradient. Choose two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) on the straight line and use the formula:

Speed = y₂ - y₁x₂ - x₁

For a curve, you must draw a tangent to the curve at that point and find its gradient.

(d) When the car is decelerating, there is a constant resistive force F on the car due to the brakes.

The deceleration of the car is greater than Fm and is not constant.

Explain why:

(i) the deceleration of the car is greater than Fm [1]

The total resistive force on the car is the sum of the braking force (F) and other resistive forces, such as air resistance (drag). Since the total resistive force is greater than F, the deceleration is greater than Fm.

Recap

Recap: Drag is a frictional force that occurs due to particles hitting an object as it moves through a liquid or a gas. When an object moves through air, this is called air resistance. The faster the object moves, the greater the drag force.

(ii) the deceleration is not constant. [2]

The deceleration depends on the total resistive force. This force is Ftotal = Fbrakes + Fair. As the car slows down (decelerates), the air resistance acting on it decreases. Since the braking force F is constant, the total resistive force decreases, and therefore the deceleration is not constant (it decreases as the car gets slower).

Recap

Recap: As an object falls, its acceleration decreases with time as air resistance increases. Eventually, it may reach a steady velocity (zero gradient) called terminal velocity, where the air resistance balances the force of gravity. The same principle applies here: as speed decreases, the air resistance component of the resistive force also decreases.

Topics: Energy, Pressure | Paper: 0625/41/M/J/22 Q2

Fig. 2.1 shows water stored in a reservoir behind a hydroelectric dam.

(a) State the form of the energy stored in the water in the reservoir that is used to generate electricity. [1]

Gravitational potential energy (or potential energy).

Recap

Recap: Gravitational Potential Energy is the energy gained as an object is moved away from the Earth (i.e., lifted to a height). The water in the reservoir has this energy because of its height above the turbine.

(b) The turbine is 150 m below the level of the water in the reservoir.

Atmospheric pressure is 1.0 x 105 Pa. The density of water is 1000 kg/m³.

(i) Calculate the total pressure in the water at the turbine. [3]

1. Calculate the pressure due to the water column (Δp):
Δp = ρ x g x Δh
Δp = 1000 kg/m³ x 10 N/kg x 150 m
Δp = 1,500,000 Pa (or 1.5 x 106 Pa)

2. Calculate the total pressure by adding atmospheric pressure:
ptotal = Δp + patm
ptotal = (1.5 x 106 Pa) + (1.0 x 105 Pa)
ptotal = 1,500,000 Pa + 100,000 Pa = 1,600,000 Pa (or 1.6 x 106 Pa)

Recap

Recap: The pressure below the surface of a liquid increases with depth and density.

Pressure (N/m²) = density (kg/m³) x gravitational field strength (N/kg) x height (m)

Δp = ρgΔh

This calculated pressure is *in addition* to any pressure acting on the surface, such as atmospheric pressure.

(ii) The turbine has a cross-sectional area of 3.5 m².

Calculate the force exerted on the turbine by the water. [2]

1. Use the pressure formula p = FA, rearranged to F = p x A.
2. Use the total pressure from part (b)(i).
F = (1.6 x 106 Pa) x 3.5 m²
F = 5,600,000 N (or 5.6 x 106 N)

Recap

Recap: Pressure is the force exerted per unit area.

pressure (N/m²) = force (N)area (m²)

p = FA

This can be rearranged to find the force if the pressure and area are known: F = p x A.

(c) The water flows to the turbine through a pipe of constant cross-sectional area.

Explain why the kinetic energy of the water in the pipe remains constant as it flows through the pipe. [2]

The speed of the water remains constant. This is because water is a liquid, which is incompressible (its density doesn't change). Since the pipe has a constant cross-sectional area and the water enters and leaves at a constant rate, the flow speed must also be constant. As kinetic energy depends on mass and speed (KE = 12mv2), constant speed means constant kinetic energy.

Recap

Recap: In a liquid, the particles are close together and cannot be pushed much closer. This means liquids are incompressible. For a fluid flowing through a pipe, if it is incompressible and the pipe's area is constant, the speed of the flow must also be constant to maintain a steady rate.

Question 3

Topics: Thermal Physics, Evaporation, Conduction | Paper: 0625/41/M/J/22 Q3

During a picnic on a warm, dry day, a metal can of lemonade is wrapped in a damp cloth.

Evaporation cools the water in the cloth.

(a) Explain, in terms of molecules, how evaporation cools the water in the cloth. [3]

The water molecules have a range of kinetic energies. The more energetic molecules (those with higher kinetic energy) have enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction and escape from the surface of the liquid.

This leaves behind the molecules with lower kinetic energy. As a result, the average kinetic energy of the remaining molecules decreases.

Since temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of the molecules, the water in the cloth cools down.

Recap

Recap: Evaporation is the transformation of a liquid into a gas at a temperature below its boiling point. The faster particles (more energetic) escape from the surface. This decreases the average kinetic energy of the remaining particles, so the temperature of the liquid decreases. This process causes cooling.

(b) As the water in the cloth cools, so does the lemonade.

Explain how electrons transfer thermal energy through the metal of the can. [3]

Metals have a lattice of positive ions and a 'sea' of free electrons. At the hot end (lemonade side, initially), the ions vibrate more and pass on vibrations. More importantly, the free electrons gain kinetic energy and move (diffuse) through the metal.

These fast-moving electrons collide with other electrons and distant ions, transferring kinetic energy rapidly from the hot side to the cold side (the damp cloth).

Recap

Recap: Metals are good thermal conductors because they have free electrons. These electrons gain kinetic energy at the hot end and can diffuse through the metal, colliding with other electrons and metal ions to transfer energy quickly. This is much more efficient than conduction through vibrations alone, which is why metals are the best conductors.

A thermocouple is a device that is used as a thermometer.

(a) Fig. 4.1 shows a beaker that contains molten sulfur at an initial temperature greater than 400°C.

(i) On Fig. 4.1, sketch and label a diagram of a thermocouple that is used to determine the temperature of the sulfur as it cools to room temperature. [4]

The diagram should show:

  1. Two wires made of different metals (e.g., copper and constantan).
  2. One junction (the 'hot' junction) placed inside the molten sulfur.
  3. The other junction (the 'cold' junction) placed in a reference bath, such as an ice-water mixture (at 0°C) or at room temperature.
  4. A voltmeter connected to the two wires to measure the e.m.f. (voltage).

Recap

Recap: While a thermocouple isn't detailed in the text, other thermometers are. A common example is the liquid-in-glass thermometer. It works on the principle that liquids expand more than solids when heated. The alcohol or mercury in the bulb expands up a narrow tube. The sensitivity is increased by making the tube very narrow.

(ii) Describe briefly how the temperature of the sulfur in the beaker is deduced. [2]

First, you measure the e.m.f. (voltage) produced by the thermocouple using the voltmeter. Then, you use this e.m.f. value to find the corresponding temperature, typically by using a pre-determined calibration graph or a data table that relates e.m.f. to temperature.

Recap

Recap: Temperature is a measure of the 'hotness' of an object. It is related to the average kinetic energy of the particles within that object. An increase in temperature corresponds to an increase in the internal energy of the body.

(b) State one advantage of using a thermocouple to measure temperature rather than using a liquid-in-glass thermometer. [1]

Any one of the following:

  • Can measure high temperatures (like molten sulfur) that would break a glass thermometer.
  • Has a rapid response to temperature changes.
  • Is more robust / less fragile than glass.
  • Can measure the temperature at a very small point.
  • Produces an electrical output that can be read by a computer.

Recap

Recap: Different thermometers have different properties. A liquid-in-glass thermometer's sensitivity can be increased by making the capillary tube (bore) narrower, so a small expansion of the liquid produces a large change in the length of the thread. This is an advantage for small temperature ranges but limits its overall use compared to a thermocouple's wide range.

Topics: Thermal Physics, Electricity, Electrical Safety | Paper: 0625/41/M/J/22 Q5

Fig. 5.1 shows a kitchen tap that supplies instant boiling water.

Cold water passes over an electric immersion heater inside the tap.

The boiling point of water is 100°C.

(a) State what is meant by boiling point. [2]

The boiling point is the specific, constant temperature at which a substance (a liquid) changes state to become a gas (at a given pressure).

Recap

Recap: Boiling is a change of state that occurs at a constant temperature (the boiling point). During boiling, the thermal energy supplied does not increase the temperature; instead, it is used to break the bonds between the particles, allowing them to escape as a gas. This is different from evaporation, which occurs only at the surface and at a range of temperatures.

(b) The immersion heater is powered by the mains at a voltage of 230 V. When the tap is opened, the heater switches on and the current in the heater is 13 A.

(i) Calculate the thermal energy produced by the heater in 60 s. [2]

1. First, find the power: P = V x I
P = 230 V x 13 A = 2990 W (or 2990 J/s)

2. Then, find the energy: E = P x t
E = 2990 J/s x 60 s = 179,400 J
(Alternatively, using E = VIt: E = 230 x 13 x 60 = 179,400 J)

Answer: 180,000 J (or 1.8 x 105 J) (to 2 significant figures)

Recap

Recap: Power is the rate of doing work or the energy transferred per second. The unit of power is the Watt (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s.

Power (W) = change in energy (J)time (s)

P = ΔEt

This can be rearranged to find the energy transferred: ΔE = P x t.

(ii) The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg°C). The cold water that enters the tap is at 22°C.

Calculate the rate at which water at its boiling point emerges from the tap. [4]

1. Find the power of the heater (from part i):
P = 2990 W (or 2990 J/s)

2. Find the energy needed to heat 1 kg of water to boiling (ΔEkg):
Temperature change Δθ = 100°C - 22°C = 78°C
ΔEkg = m x c x Δθ
ΔEkg = 1 kg x 4200 J/(kg°C) x 78°C = 327,600 J/kg

3. Find the rate (mass per second):
Rate (kg/s) = Power (J/s)Energy per kg (J/kg)
Rate = 2990 J/s327,600 J/kg = 0.00913 kg/s (or 9.1 x 10-3 kg/s)

Recap

Recap: The specific heat capacity (c) of a material is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of the material by 1°C. The formula for the energy transferred is:

Energy Transferred (J) = mass (kg) x specific heat capacity (J/kg°C) x temperature change (°C)

ΔE = mcΔθ

(c) The metal tap is earthed and there is a fuse in the cable that connects the heater to the mains.

1. Explain how the earth wire protects the user.

2. Explain how the fuse protects the circuit. [3]

1. (Earth wire): If a fault causes the live wire to touch the (metal) tap casing, the casing becomes live. The earth wire provides a very low-resistance path for the current to flow directly to the earth. This prevents the current from flowing through the user if they touch the tap.

2. (Fuse): This large current (a "short circuit") flowing to earth causes the wire in the fuse to heat up and melt (or "blow"). This breaks the circuit, cutting off the electrical supply and making the appliance safe.

Recap

Recap: Electrical safety features are designed to manage energy transfer. When an appliance is working, we want its efficiency to be high (most of the input energy becomes useful output energy).

efficiency = useful energy outputenergy input x 100%

A fault, like a short circuit, causes the input energy to be converted into wasted heat very quickly, which is dangerous. Fuses and earth wires are designed to stop this dangerous energy transfer.

Topics: Thermal Physics, Radiation, Convection | Paper: 0625/41/M/J/22 Q6

Fig. 6.1 shows a road next to the sea.

(a) On a sunny day, the Sun warms the road.

Describe how energy from the Sun reaches the Earth and warms the road. [3]

Energy is transferred from the Sun to the Earth by radiation (specifically infrared and light radiation, which are electromagnetic waves).

This radiation can travel through the vacuum of space.

When this radiation reaches the road, it is absorbed by the surface, causing the road's internal energy to increase and its temperature to rise.

Recap

Recap: All objects emit infrared (thermal) radiation. This is a type of electromagnetic wave and, like all EM waves, it can travel across a vacuum. The Earth absorbs this high-energy radiation from the Sun, which causes it to warm up.

(b) The temperature of the road is greater than the temperature of the sea.

The surface of the road is black.

Suggest one reason why the temperature of the road is greater than that of the sea. [1]

The black surface of the road is a good absorber of (infrared) radiation, while the sea is a poorer absorber (it reflects more radiation).

(Also acceptable: Water has a much higher specific heat capacity than the road material.)

Recap

Recap: The colour of a surface affects how it absorbs and emits radiation. A matt black surface is the best absorber and the best emitter of infrared radiation. A shiny silver surface is the worst absorber (it is a good reflector) and the worst emitter.

(c) The air above the road is heated by the warm road.

(i) Describe how this affects the molecules of the air. [2]

The air molecules gain kinetic energy from the hot road, causing them to speed up (move faster). As they move faster, they also move further apart from each other (the air expands).

Recap

Recap: When gases are heated, the particles gain kinetic energy and move around faster. They take up more space, meaning the gas expands. This expansion is much greater than in solids or liquids.

(ii) A cyclist travelling along the road notices that a cool breeze is blowing from the sea to the land.

Explain how convection produces this breeze. You may include a diagram if it helps your answer. [3]

1. The air above the warm road gains kinetic energy, expands, and becomes less dense.

2. This warm, less dense air rises.

3. The air above the sea is cooler and more dense. This cooler, denser air moves in from the sea to replace the rising warm air. This movement of air is the sea breeze.

Recap

Recap: Convection is the transfer of heat in fluids (liquids and gases). When a fluid is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and move further apart, causing the fluid to expand and its density to decrease. The warm, less dense fluid rises, and cooler, more dense fluid sinks to take its place, creating a convection current.

Topics: Light, Lenses, Ray Diagrams | Paper: 0625/41/M/J/22 Q7

Fig. 7.1 is a full-scale diagram of a small nail N in front of a thin converging lens. The line L represents the lens.

The focal length of the lens is 3.0 cm.

(a) Rays of light, parallel to XY, are travelling towards the lens.

Describe what happens to the light after it passes through the lens. [3]

The converging lens refracts the parallel rays of light so that they all meet at a single point. This point is called the principal focus (or focal point). This point is located on the principal axis (XY) at a distance of 3.0 cm from the lens.

Recap

Recap: When parallel rays of light pass through a convex (converging) lens, they are focused to a point called the principal focus. The principal axis is the imaginary line passing through the centre of the lens. The distance from the centre of the lens to the principal focus is the focal length.

(b) On Fig. 7.1, mark and label with an F each of the two principal focuses of the lens. [1]

A point labelled 'F' should be marked on the line XY at 3.0 cm (3 large squares) to the left of the lens (L), AND another point 'F' at 3.0 cm to the right of the lens.

Recap

Recap: A lens has a principal focus on both sides, as light can pass through it in either direction. The focal length is the distance from the lens's centre to this point. For a diverging (concave) lens, the principal focus is the point from which parallel rays *appear* to diverge.

(c) The small nail N, of height 1.2 cm, is positioned 2.0 cm to the left of the lens.

(i) By drawing on Fig. 7.1, find the position of the image I of N and add image I to the diagram. [3]

To find the image, draw two (or three) rays from the tip of the nail (N):

  1. A ray travelling parallel to the principal axis (XY) which refracts through the lens and passes through the principal focus (F) on the right.
  2. A ray passing through the centre of the lens, which continues undeflected.

These two rays diverge (spread out) after passing through the lens. Trace them backwards (as dotted lines) to the point where they appear to intersect. This point of intersection is the tip of the image (I).

The image I should be drawn at 6.0 cm to the left of the lens, and it will be upright and magnified.

Recap

Recap: This specific setup (object placed *inside* the focal length) is how a magnifying glass works. The three standard rays to draw for a converging lens are:

  1. Ray parallel to axis → through F
  2. Ray through centre → undeviated
  3. Ray through F → parallel to axis

In this case, the first two are easiest. When the rays diverge, you must trace them back to find a virtual image.

(ii) State and explain whether I is a real or a virtual image. [1]

The image is virtual.

This is because the real rays of light do not actually pass through the image position; they only appear to come from that point. A virtual image cannot be formed on a screen.

Recap

Recap: An image is virtual if it cannot be focused on a screen. It is formed where rays *appear* to diverge from. An image is real if it *can* be focused on a screen (like with a projector or in a camera). Real images are formed where light rays actually converge.

(iii) State the name given to a lens when it is used in this way. [1]

A magnifying glass (or simple magnifier).

Recap

Recap: When an object is placed between the centre of a converging lens and its principal focus (F), the image formed is virtual, magnified, and upright. This arrangement is used as a magnifying glass.

Topics: Motion, Energy, Electromagnetism | Paper: 0625/41/M/J/22 Q8

Fig. 8.1 shows two vertical, cylindrical tubes and a cylindrical magnet all held in a vacuum.

One tube is made of plastic and the other tube is made of copper. The two cylindrical tubes have identical dimensions.

The magnetic field of the small, cylindrical magnet is extremely strong.

Initially, the magnet is at rest at the top of the plastic tube.

The magnet is released and it falls through the plastic tube without experiencing a resistive force. The magnet takes 0.67 s to fall to the lower end of the plastic tube.

(a) The mass of the magnet is 0.012 kg.

Calculate the kinetic energy of the magnet when it reaches the lower end of the plastic tube. [4]

1. Find the final velocity (v) of the magnet (accelerating from rest at g = 10 m/s²):
v = u + at
v = 0 + (10 m/s²) x 0.67 s
v = 6.7 m/s

2. Calculate the kinetic energy (KE):
KE = 12 m v2
KE = 0.5 x 0.012 kg x (6.7 m/s)2
KE = 0.5 x 0.012 x 44.89
KE = 0.27 J (or 0.269 J)

Recap

Recap: The equation for an object's kinetic energy (energy of movement) is:

Kinetic energy (J) = 12 x mass (kg) x velocity² (m/s)²

Ek = 12 m v2

The equation for acceleration is a = Δvt, which can be rearranged to find the change in velocity: Δv = a x t.

(b) The magnet is then held at the top of the copper tube and released. As it falls through the copper tube, an electric current is generated in the copper.

(i) Explain why there is a current in the copper. [2]

As the magnet falls, its magnetic field moves. This moving magnetic field lines cut across the copper tube (which is a conductor).

This process is called electromagnetic induction, and it induces an e.m.f. (voltage) in the copper. Because copper is a conductor in a closed loop, this e.m.f. drives a current (an eddy current).

Recap

Recap: Electromagnetic induction is the process of generating an e.m.f. (and current) in a conductor by changing the magnetic field around it. A generator is a device that uses this principle, transferring kinetic energy into electrical energy by rotating a coil in a magnetic field (or vice versa).

(ii) The current in the copper produces a magnetic field of its own in the tube.

The magnet falls much more slowly in the copper tube than in the plastic tube. Explain why the magnet falls more slowly in the copper tube. [2]

The induced current in the copper creates its own magnetic field. By Lenz's Law, this new magnetic field opposes the change that created it.

This means the induced magnetic field exerts an upwards (opposing) force on the falling magnet, which opposes its motion. This 'magnetic braking' force reduces the net downward force, causing the magnet to fall more slowly.

Recap

Recap: This is an example of the principle of conservation of energy. The kinetic energy of the magnet is being converted into electrical energy in the copper tube (which then dissipates as heat). Because energy is being transferred *away* from the magnet's motion, its kinetic energy (and thus speed) must be less than it would be in freefall.

Two of the isotopes of hydrogen are hydrogen-2 (21H) and hydrogen-3 (31H).

A nucleus of hydrogen-3 has a different number of neutrons. It has one more neutron than hydrogen-2. (H-3 has 2 neutrons, H-2 has 1 neutron).
