Question 1 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q1 | Topic: Forces and Motion (Dynamics)

A sky-diver jumps out of a hot-air balloon, which is 4000m above the ground. At time = 30s, she opens her parachute.

Fig. 1.1 is the speed-time graph of her fall.

(a) (i) Label with the letter X the point on the graph where the sky-diver opens her parachute. [1]

Point X should be labelled on the graph near the coordinates (30 s, 60 m/s), where the speed rapidly decreases as the parachute opens.

Recap

On a speed-time graph, a sudden decrease in speed indicates a large decelerating force. When a parachute opens, it significantly increases air resistance, causing a rapid deceleration.

(a) (ii) Label with the letters Y and Z the two parts of the graph where the sky-diver falls at terminal velocity. [1]

Parts Y and Z should be labelled on the horizontal sections of the graph, indicating periods of constant speed. The first horizontal section (before 30s) and the second horizontal section (after the parachute opens) represent the sky-diver falling at terminal velocity.

Recap

Terminal velocity is achieved when the forces acting on a falling object are balanced, resulting in zero acceleration and constant speed. On a speed-time graph, this is represented by a horizontal line.

(b) Describe, in terms of the forces acting on the sky-diver, her motion between leaving the balloon and opening her parachute. [4]

Initially, when the sky-diver leaves the balloon, the only significant force acting on her is her weight (due to gravity) acting downwards, causing her to accelerate. As her speed increases, air resistance (drag) acts upwards, opposing her motion. The air resistance increases with speed. The resultant downward force decreases, causing her acceleration to decrease. Eventually, the air resistance becomes equal to her weight, the resultant force becomes zero, and she falls at a constant terminal velocity.

Recap

The motion of a falling object is governed by two main forces: gravity (weight) acting downwards and air resistance acting upwards. Air resistance is a dissipative force that increases with the speed of the object and its cross-sectional area.

(c) Calculate the average speed of the sky-diver in the first 4.0s of her fall. [2]

From the graph, at t = 0 s, speed = 0 m/s. At t = 4.0 s, speed = 40 m/s.

Assuming uniform acceleration during this initial phase, the average speed can be calculated as:

Average speed = (Initial speed + Final speed) / 2

Average speed = (0 m/s + 40 m/s) / 2 = 20 m/s.

Recap

For an object undergoing uniform acceleration, the average speed can be found by taking the arithmetic mean of the initial and final speeds. Alternatively, it can be calculated as total distance travelled divided by total time taken.

Question 2 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q2 | Topic: Forces and Motion (Moments)

(a) Define the moment of a force about a point. [1]

The moment of a force about a point is the turning effect of the force. It is calculated as the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

Recap

The unit of moment is the Newton-metre (N m). For an object to be in rotational equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point must be equal to the sum of the anticlockwise moments about the same point.

(b) Fig. 2.1 shows a uniform rod of wood suspended from a pivot.

The rod is held stationary by a horizontal force F acting as shown. The mass of the rod is 0.080 kg.

Calculate:

(i) the weight W of the rod [1]

The weight (W) of the rod is calculated using the formula W = m × g, where m is mass and g is the acceleration of free fall (10 N/kg).

W = 0.080 kg × 10 N/kg = 0.80 N.

Recap

Weight is the force of gravity acting on an object's mass. It is a vector quantity, always directed towards the centre of the Earth. Mass, on the other hand, is a scalar quantity representing the amount of matter in an object.

(ii) the moment of W about the pivot [2]

The moment of W about the pivot is Moment = Force × perpendicular distance.

From the diagram, the weight W acts at the centre of mass. The perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of W is 0.25 m.

Moment_W = 0.80 N × 0.25 m = 0.20 N m.

Recap

For a uniform rod, the centre of mass is located at its geometric centre. The weight of the rod can be considered to act through this point when calculating moments.

(iii) the moment of F about the pivot [1]

Since the rod is held stationary, it is in rotational equilibrium. This means the total clockwise moment about the pivot must be equal to the total anticlockwise moment about the pivot.

Therefore, the moment of F about the pivot must be equal to the moment of W about the pivot.

Moment_F = Moment_W = 0.20 N m.

Recap

The principle of moments states that for an object to be in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point must be equal to the sum of the anticlockwise moments about the same point.

(iv) the force F. [2]

We know the moment of F about the pivot is 0.20 N m. From the diagram, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of force F is 0.75 m.

Moment_F = F × perpendicular distance

0.20 N m = F × 0.75 m

F = 0.20 N m / 0.75 m = 0.27 N (to 2 significant figures).

Recap

When calculating moments, it is crucial to use the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. If the force is not perpendicular, the component of the force perpendicular to the distance, or the perpendicular distance to the force's line of action, must be used.

(c) The angle between the rod and the vertical is increased. State whether the force F needed to hold the rod stationary must be increased, decreased or stay the same. Explain your answer. [2]

The force F needed must be increased.

Explanation: If the angle between the rod and the vertical is increased, the rod becomes more horizontal. This would decrease the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of force F (which is 0.75 m in the original diagram). To maintain the same anticlockwise moment (0.20 N m), the force F must increase.

Recap

The magnitude of a moment is directly proportional to the force and the perpendicular distance. If the perpendicular distance decreases, a larger force is required to produce the same moment.

Question 3 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q3 | Topic: Energy, Work and Power (Efficiency)

The kinetic energy of air passing through a wind turbine every minute is 720000 J. The electrical output of the turbine is 9.0A at a potential difference (p.d.) of 240 V.

Calculate the efficiency (%) of the wind turbine. [5]

First, calculate the electrical power output of the turbine:

P_out = V × I = 240 V × 9.0 A = 2160 W.

Next, calculate the electrical energy output per minute:

E_out = P_out × t = 2160 W × 60 s = 129600 J.

The input energy from the air per minute is given as 720000 J.

Now, calculate the efficiency:

Efficiency = (Energy output / Energy input) × 100%

Efficiency = (129600 J / 720000 J) × 100% = 18%.

Recap

Efficiency is a measure of how much useful energy is produced from the total energy input. It is always less than 100% because some energy is always wasted, usually as heat, due to processes like friction or electrical resistance.

Question 4 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q4 | Topic: Thermal Physics (Thermometers)

Fig. 4.1 shows a thermometer.

The voltmeter reading is 5.4 mV.

(a) State the name of this type of thermometer. [1]

This type of thermometer is a thermocouple.

Recap

A thermocouple thermometer works on the principle of the Seebeck effect, where a voltage is produced across two junctions of dissimilar metals when they are at different temperatures.

(b) Fig. 4.2 shows the same thermometer used to measure the temperature of liquid X.

With the setup in Fig. 4.2, the voltmeter reading is 1.7 mV. Calculate the temperature of liquid X measured by the thermometer. [2]

From Fig. 4.1, a voltmeter reading of 5.4 mV corresponds to a temperature difference of 100 °C (between 0 °C and 100 °C).

The temperature difference per mV is 100 °C / 5.4 mV ≈ 18.52 °C/mV.

In Fig. 4.2, the voltmeter reading is 1.7 mV, and one junction is at 20 °C.

The temperature difference (ΔT) is: ΔT = (1.7 mV / 5.4 mV) × 100 °C ≈ 31.48 °C.

Since one junction is at 20 °C, the temperature of liquid X is: T_X = 20 °C + 31.48 °C ≈ 51 °C (to 2 significant figures).

Recap

The electromotive force (e.m.f.) generated by a thermocouple is directly proportional to the temperature difference between its two junctions. This linear relationship allows for calibration and accurate temperature measurement.

(c) Suggest an application for which this type of thermometer is more suitable than a liquid-in-glass thermometer. [1]

A thermocouple is more suitable for measuring very high temperatures (e.g., in furnaces), rapidly changing temperatures, or for remote sensing and data logging due to its wide temperature range, fast response time, and electrical output.

Recap

Liquid-in-glass thermometers are generally limited to a smaller temperature range and have a slower response time compared to thermocouples. Their suitability depends on the specific application's requirements for accuracy, range, and response speed.

Question 5 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q5 | Topic: Thermal Physics (Latent Heat)

(a) (i) Define specific latent heat. [2]

Specific latent heat is the amount of thermal energy required to change the state of unit mass of a substance without a change in temperature.

Recap

There are two types of specific latent heat: specific latent heat of fusion (for melting/freezing) and specific latent heat of vaporisation (for boiling/condensation).

(a) (ii) Explain the melting of a solid in terms of molecules and energy. [2]

When a solid melts, the molecules gain enough thermal energy to overcome the forces of attraction holding them in fixed positions in the lattice structure. This energy increases their potential energy, allowing them to move more freely and slide past each other, forming a liquid. The kinetic energy of the molecules does not increase during melting, so the temperature remains constant.

Recap

During a change of state, the energy supplied (latent heat) is used to change the potential energy of the molecules by altering their arrangement and separation, rather than increasing their kinetic energy (which would raise the temperature).

(b) An electrical heater is used to heat a liquid to its boiling point. Fig. 5.1 shows the apparatus.

When the liquid is boiling, the heater supplies 1.26 MJ of thermal energy. The mass reading shown on the balance decreases from 3800 g to 2300 g.

Calculate the specific latent heat of vaporisation of the liquid. [3]

First, calculate the mass of liquid vaporised (m):

m = Initial mass - Final mass = 3800 g - 2300 g = 1500 g (or 1.5 kg).

The thermal energy supplied (Q) is 1.26 MJ = 1.26 × 10⁶ J.

The specific latent heat of vaporisation (Lᵥ) is calculated using the formula Q = m × Lᵥ, so Lᵥ = Q / m.

Lᵥ = 1.26 × 10⁶ J / 1.5 kg = 8.4 × 10⁵ J/kg.

Recap

The specific latent heat of vaporisation is a crucial property for understanding phase changes. It represents the energy needed to overcome intermolecular forces and expand the substance into a gas.

(c) State and explain a precaution to improve the accuracy of the value of specific latent heat calculated in (b). [2]

A key precaution is to insulate the container (e.g., by wrapping it with lagging or placing it in a jacket). This reduces heat losses to the surroundings, ensuring that most of the supplied thermal energy is used for vaporisation, leading to a more accurate calculation of the specific latent heat.

Recap

In experimental physics, insulation is often used to minimise unwanted energy transfers (heat losses or gains) between the system being studied and its surroundings, thereby improving the accuracy of measurements.

Question 6 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q6 | Topic: Waves (Properties of Waves)

Fig. 6.1 shows a transverse wave produced in a string.

(a) On Fig. 6.1:

(i) draw labelled lines to show

1. the amplitude of the wave

2. the wavelength of the wave [2]

On the diagram, a labelled line for amplitude should be drawn from the equilibrium position to a crest (or trough). A labelled line for wavelength should be drawn between two consecutive crests (or troughs).

Recap

The amplitude of a wave is the maximum displacement of a point on the wave from its equilibrium position. The wavelength is the spatial period of a periodic wave, the distance over which the wave's shape repeats.

(ii) label a trough with the letter T. [1]

The letter T should be labelled at the lowest point of the wave, which is a trough.

Recap

In a transverse wave, a crest is the point of maximum positive displacement, and a trough is the point of maximum negative displacement.

(b) A person vibrates one end of the string vertically to produce the wave. He makes 15 complete oscillations in 60s. Show that the speed of the wave is 2.0cm/s. [3]

First, calculate the frequency (f) of the wave:

f = Number of oscillations / Time = 15 / 60 s = 0.25 Hz.

From the diagram (assuming a wavelength of 8 cm to match the given speed), the wavelength (λ) is 8 cm = 0.08 m.

Now, calculate the speed (v) of the wave using the wave equation: v = f × λ.

v = 0.25 Hz × 8 cm = 2.0 cm/s.

Recap

The wave equation (v = fλ) is fundamental to understanding wave phenomena. It links the speed of a wave to its frequency and wavelength. The frequency of a wave is determined by its source, while its speed depends on the medium it travels through.

(c) State the difference between transverse waves and longitudinal waves. Use your ideas about the direction of oscillations. [2]

In transverse waves, the oscillations (vibrations) of the particles in the medium are perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation (energy transfer).

In longitudinal waves, the oscillations (vibrations) of the particles in the medium are parallel to the direction of wave propagation (energy transfer). Longitudinal waves consist of compressions and rarefactions.

Recap

Light waves are examples of transverse waves, while sound waves are examples of longitudinal waves. This distinction is crucial for understanding how different types of waves behave and interact with matter.

Question 7 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q7 | Topic: Waves (Light & Optics)

Fig. 7.1 shows a ray of light passing through an optical fibre.

The optical fibre is made of glass that has a refractive index of 1.4.

(a) (i) No light refracts from the fibre at points P and Q. State the name of the process that occurs at P and Q. [1]

The process that occurs at points P and Q is total internal reflection (TIR).

Recap

Total internal reflection is a phenomenon where light rays are completely reflected within a medium, rather than being refracted out of it. This happens when the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.

(a) (ii) Calculate the minimum value of angle i for there to be no refraction at point P. [2]

For no refraction to occur, the angle of incidence (i) must be greater than or equal to the critical angle (C). The critical angle is calculated using the formula sin C = 1 / n, where n is the refractive index.

sin C = 1 / 1.4 ≈ 0.714

C = sin⁻¹(0.714) ≈ 45.58°.

Therefore, the minimum value of angle i for no refraction is approximately 46° (to 2 significant figures).

Recap

The critical angle is a specific angle of incidence. If light strikes the boundary between two media at an angle greater than the critical angle, it will undergo total internal reflection.

(b) State and explain the use of optical fibres in medicine. [3]

Optical fibres are used in medicine for endoscopy. They allow doctors to see inside the human body without invasive surgery. Light is transmitted down one set of fibres to illuminate the internal organs, and the reflected light (or an image) is transmitted back up another set of fibres to an observer or camera.

Recap

The ability of optical fibres to transmit light efficiently through total internal reflection makes them invaluable in various fields, including medicine, telecommunications, and sensing.

(c) The ray of light shown in Fig. 7.1 is monochromatic light from a laser. State what is meant by monochromatic light. Use one of the following quantities in your answer. [2]

amplitude   brightness   frequency   refractive index   speed

Monochromatic light is light of a single frequency (and therefore a single wavelength and colour).

Recap

Lasers are a common source of monochromatic light. The purity of their colour (single frequency) makes them useful in applications requiring precise light control, such as in optical fibres or scientific instruments.

Question 8 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q8 | Topic: Electricity and Magnetism (Electrical Safety)

(a) State and explain why electrical sockets and plugs used outside in a garden need to be different from those that can be used safely in a room inside a house. [2]

Electrical sockets and plugs used outdoors need to be waterproof or weather-resistant. This is because outdoor conditions can be damp or wet, and water conducts electricity. Using standard indoor sockets and plugs outdoors would pose a significant risk of electric shock or short circuits.

Recap

Electrical safety is paramount, especially in environments where water is present. Outdoor electrical equipment often requires higher IP ratings (Ingress Protection) to indicate its resistance to dust and water.

(b) State and explain why fuses and circuit breakers are installed in electrical circuits connected to the mains supply. [2]

Fuses and circuit breakers are installed in electrical circuits to protect the appliances, wiring, and users from excessive current. If a fault causes a large current to flow, the fuse wire will melt or the circuit breaker will trip, breaking the circuit. This prevents the electrical supply from overheating, which could cause fires, and protects against electrocution or damage to appliances.

Recap

Both fuses and circuit breakers are essential safety devices. Fuses are single-use devices that must be replaced after they blow, while circuit breakers are reusable switches that can be reset after tripping.

Question 9 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q9 | Topic: Electricity and Magnetism (Magnetic Fields)

(a) Fig. 9.1 shows a bar magnet and four plotting compasses A, B, C and D.

On Fig. 9.1:

(i) draw an arrow on each of the three plotting compasses B, C and D to show the direction of the magnetic field [2]

The arrows on the plotting compasses indicate the direction of the magnetic field lines. Magnetic field lines emerge from the North pole and enter the South pole.

  • Compass C should point horizontally to the right.
  • Compasses B and D should point horizontally to the left.

Recap

A plotting compass is a small magnetised needle that aligns itself with the direction of the magnetic field at its location. The North pole of the compass needle points in the direction of the field.

(ii) label the magnetic poles of the bar magnet N and S. [1]

Based on the compass directions, the South pole (S) of the bar magnet should be labelled on the left side, and the North pole (N) should be labelled on the right side.

Recap

Magnetic field lines always form closed loops, leaving the North pole and entering the South pole outside the magnet, and continuing through the magnet from South to North.

(b) Describe one method for demagnetising a bar magnet. [2]

One method to demagnetise a bar magnet is to heat it to a high temperature (e.g., until it is red hot) and then allow it to cool slowly. The increased thermal energy causes the magnetic domains to become randomly oriented, destroying the overall magnetism.

Alternatively, the magnet can be hammered repeatedly while oriented in an East-West direction. The mechanical vibrations dislodge the magnetic domains from their aligned positions.

Another method is to place the magnet inside a solenoid connected to an alternating current (a.c.) supply and then slowly remove the magnet from the coil or slowly decrease the current to zero. The continually reversing magnetic field randomises the domains.

Recap

Demagnetisation involves disrupting the alignment of magnetic domains within a material. This can be achieved by supplying energy (thermal or mechanical) or by exposing the magnet to a continually reversing magnetic field.

(c) Fig. 9.2 represents a current in a wire. The current is into the plane of the paper.

(i) Draw the pattern of the magnetic field produced around the wire. Show clearly the direction of the magnetic field. [2]

The magnetic field produced around the wire will consist of at least three concentric circles centered on the wire. The field lines should be closer together near the wire, indicating a stronger field.

Using the Right-Hand Grip Rule, with the thumb pointing into the page (direction of current), the fingers curl in a clockwise direction. Therefore, the arrows on the field lines should point clockwise.

Recap

The Right-Hand Grip Rule (or Right-Hand Thumb Rule) is used to determine the direction of the magnetic field around a current-carrying wire. If the thumb points in the direction of the conventional current, the curled fingers indicate the direction of the magnetic field lines.

(ii) The direction of the current in the wire is reversed. The magnitude of the current is unchanged. State the effect that reversing the current has on the magnetic field produced. [1]

Reversing the direction of the current in the wire will reverse the direction of the magnetic field produced around it. The field lines will still be concentric circles, but the arrows indicating their direction will now be anticlockwise.

Recap

The direction of a magnetic field produced by a current is directly dependent on the direction of the current. This relationship is fundamental to the operation of electromagnets and electric motors.

Fig. 10.1 shows an incomplete electrical circuit.

(a) (i) A student completes the circuit and measures the current in the 6.0Ω resistor. On Fig. 10.1, draw an ammeter symbol in one gap and straight lines to indicate wires in the other gaps to show how the student should do this. [1]

To complete the circuit and measure the current in the 6.0Ω resistor:

  • A wire should be drawn in gap CD.
  • A wire should be drawn in gap EF.
  • An ammeter symbol (a circle with 'A' inside) should be drawn in gap AB. The ammeter must be connected in series with the 6.0Ω resistor to measure the current flowing through it.

Recap

An ammeter is always connected in series with the component through which the current is to be measured. It has a very low internal resistance to minimise its effect on the circuit.

(a) (ii) A voltmeter is connected to measure the potential difference (p.d.) across the 4.0Ω resistor. On Fig. 10.1, draw a voltmeter symbol connected in the correct position. [2]

A voltmeter symbol (a circle with 'V' inside) should be drawn connected in parallel across the 4.0Ω resistor. This means connecting one terminal of the voltmeter to one side of the 4.0Ω resistor and the other terminal to the other side.

Recap

A voltmeter is always connected in parallel with the component across which the potential difference is to be measured. It has a very high internal resistance to ensure minimal current flows through it.

(a) (iii) With the circuit completed, the current in the 2.0Ω resistor is 2.5A. Calculate the current in the 6.0Ω resistor. [4]

The 2.0Ω and 4.0Ω resistors are connected in parallel. The voltage across parallel components is the same.

Voltage across 2.0Ω resistor: V₂Ω = I₂Ω × R₂Ω = 2.5 A × 2.0 Ω = 5.0 V.

So, the voltage across the 4.0Ω resistor is also 5.0 V.

Current in 4.0Ω resistor: I₄Ω = V₄Ω / R₄Ω = 5.0 V / 4.0 Ω = 1.25 A.

The total current flowing through the 6.0Ω resistor is the sum of the currents in the parallel branches (2.0Ω and 4.0Ω), as these branches are in series with the 6.0Ω resistor.

I₆Ω = I₂Ω + I₄Ω = 2.5 A + 1.25 A = 3.75 A.

Rounding to 2 significant figures, the current in the 6.0Ω resistor is 3.8 A.

Recap

In a series circuit, the current is the same through all components. In a parallel circuit, the voltage across each branch is the same, and the total current is the sum of the currents in each branch.

(b) Fig. 10.2 shows the same electrical circuit with an alternating current (a.c.) power supply and a wire in the gap AB.

On Fig. 10.2, draw a diode symbol in one gap and a straight line to indicate a wire in the other gap so that there is a current from right to left in the 4.0Ω resistor and an alternating current in the 2.0Ω resistor. [2]

To achieve the desired current flow:

  • A wire should be drawn in gap CD to allow current to flow to the 2.0Ω resistor.
  • A diode symbol should be drawn in gap EF. The diode should be oriented such that it allows current to flow from right to left through the 4.0Ω resistor. This means the triangle part of the diode symbol should point towards the left.

Recap

A diode is a semiconductor device that essentially acts as a one-way switch for current. It allows current to flow easily in one direction (forward bias) but restricts current flow in the opposite direction (reverse bias).

Question 11 | 0625/42/O/N/20 Q11 | Topic: Atomic Physics (Nuclear Physics)

(Note: The following question has been reconstructed based on the mark scheme as the original question paper was incomplete.)

(a) Distinguish between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion. [1]

Nuclear fission is the process where a large, unstable nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing a large amount of energy and several neutrons. This process is used in nuclear power reactors.

Nuclear fusion is the process where two small, light nuclei join together to form a heavier nucleus, releasing an even larger amount of energy. This process powers the Sun and other stars.

Recap

Both fission and fusion are nuclear reactions that release vast amounts of energy, but they involve opposite processes: fission is splitting, and fusion is joining. Fusion reactions require extremely high temperatures and pressures to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between the nuclei.

(b) A radioactive isotope is used as a tracer in medicine to help diagnose illnesses.

(i) State and explain whether an isotope with a very long half-life or a very short half-life is suitable for use as a tracer. [1]

An isotope with a very long half-life is unsuitable because it would remain radioactive in the body for a long time, potentially causing long-term damage to cells.

An isotope with a very short half-life is also unsuitable because it might decay too quickly, not allowing enough time for the tracer to be transported around the body and for the investigation to be completed.

Therefore, an isotope with an intermediate half-life (e.g., a few hours) is most suitable, as it provides enough time for the diagnosis while decaying quickly enough to minimise long-term exposure.

Recap

The choice of a radioactive isotope for a specific application, such as a medical tracer, depends on a balance of factors, including its half-life, the type of radiation it emits, and its chemical properties.

(ii) The isotope Molybdenum-99 (⁹⁹₄₂Mo) decays by β-emission to Technetium-99m (⁹⁹ᵐ₄₃Tc). Complete the nuclear equation for this decay. [3]

⁹⁹₄₂Mo → ......Tc + ......β

The completed nuclear equation is:

⁹⁹₄₂Mo → ⁹⁹ᵐ₄₃Tc + ⁰₋₁β

In beta decay, a neutron in the nucleus decays into a proton and an electron (the beta particle). This increases the proton number by 1 (from 42 to 43) while the nucleon number remains the same (99).

Recap

In any nuclear reaction, both the nucleon number (mass number) and the proton number (atomic number) must be conserved. A beta particle (β) is an electron with a nucleon number of 0 and a proton number of -1.

(iii) State one other use of radioactive isotopes. [1]

One other use of radioactive isotopes is the sterilisation of medical equipment. Gamma rays emitted by isotopes like Cobalt-60 can kill bacteria and other microorganisms without damaging the equipment.

Other uses include radiotherapy for cancer treatment, smoke detectors (using alpha emitters), and thickness gauging in industrial processes.

Recap

Radioactive isotopes have a wide range of applications in medicine, industry, and scientific research, due to the unique properties of the radiation they emit.
