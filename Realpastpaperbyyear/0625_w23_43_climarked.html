(a) Oil of density 0.80 g/cm3 is poured gently onto the surface of water of density 1.0 g/cm3. The oil and the water do not mix.

Describe and explain the final position of the oil relative to the water.

description

explanation

[2]

Description: The oil stays on the surface and floats on the water.

Explanation: This is because the oil has a lower density (0.80 g/cm3) than water (1.0 g/cm3).

Recap

Recap

Density is a measure of the mass per unit volume of a material (given by the equation ρ = m/V).

An object or liquid will float on top of another liquid if it has a lower density. If it has a higher density, it will sink. This is why a rock (high density) sinks in water, but a block of wood (low density) floats.

(b) An irregularly shaped solid object has a density of 2.7 g/cm3.

(i) Describe a method to measure the volume of the irregularly shaped solid object. [2]

1. Pour a measured initial volume of a liquid (like water) into a measuring cylinder and record this volume.

2. Carefully immerse the irregularly shaped solid object completely into the water. Ensure no water splashes out.

3. Measure the new (final) volume shown on the measuring cylinder.

4. The volume of the object is calculated by subtracting the initial volume from the final volume.

Recap

Recap

This is known as the displacement method. The volume of the fluid displaced by the fully submerged object is equal to the volume of the object itself.

For accuracy, you should read the volume from the bottom of the meniscus (the curve of the water) and ensure your eye is level with it.

(ii) The volume of the object is 83 cm3.

Calculate the mass of the object. [3]

Using the formula: density = mass / volume (or ρ = m/V).

Rearranging the formula to find mass: mass = density × volume.

mass = 2.7 g/cm3 × 83 cm3

mass = 224.1 g

(The mark scheme answer is 220 g, likely rounding to 2 significant figures, but 224.1 g is the precise calculation).

Recap

Recap

The formula linking density, mass, and volume is ρ = m/V. It is essential to ensure all units are consistent before calculating.

In this case, density is in g/cm3 and volume is in cm3, so the resulting mass is in grams (g). If the density was in kg/m3, the volume would need to be in m3 to get a mass in kg.

Topic: Forces (Hooke's Law, Circular Motion)
0625/43/O/N/23 Q2

(a) Fig. 2.1 is a graph that shows how the extension of a spring varies with the load suspended from it.

(i) Determine the spring constant of this spring. [3]

The spring constant (k) is found using Hooke's Law, F = kx, where F is the force (load) and x is the extension.

Rearranging the formula: k = F / x.

From the straight-line portion of the graph, we can see that a force (F) of 14 N causes an extension (x) of 4.0 cm.

k = 14 N / 4.0 cm

k = 3.5 N/cm.

(This could also be converted to 350 N/m).

Recap

Recap

Hooke's Law states that the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the applied force (load), as long as the elastic limit is not exceeded. This is why the graph is a straight line through the origin.

The spring constant (k) is the force per unit extension (e.g., in N/m or N/cm) and represents the stiffness of the spring. A stiffer spring has a larger value of k.

(ii) On Fig. 2.1, mark the limit of proportionality and label this point L. [1]

The limit of proportionality (L) should be marked at the exact point where the graph stops being a straight line and begins to curve.

Recap

Recap

The limit of proportionality is the point beyond which Hooke's Law (F ∝ x) is no longer obeyed. Beyond this point, the spring may stretch more easily (or with greater difficulty) for each additional newton of force.

This is very close to the elastic limit, which is the point beyond which the spring will be permanently deformed and will not return to its original length when the load is removed.

(b) Fig. 2.2 shows a car travelling at constant speed around corner A on a road.

(i) On Fig. 2.2, mark with an arrow the direction of the resultant force acting on the car as it travels around corner A. [2]

The arrow should be drawn starting from the car and pointing radially inward, i.e., horizontally to the left, towards the centre of the circular path of corner A.

Recap

Recap

An object moving in a circle, even at a constant speed, is accelerating because its velocity (a vector quantity) is constantly changing direction.

This acceleration is caused by a resultant force called the centripetal force, which always acts towards the centre of the circle. This force is provided by friction between the tyres and the road.

(ii) Corner B has a smaller radius than corner A. The car travels at the same speed around corner B as around corner A.

State how the resultant force changes due to the car travelling around a corner of smaller radius. [1]

The resultant force increases.

Recap

Recap

The size of the centripetal force required to keep an object in a circular path increases if:

  • the mass of the object increases
  • the speed of the object increases
  • the radius of the circle decreases

Since the car is turning a tighter corner (smaller radius) at the same speed, a larger centripetal force is required.

Topic: Forces (Momentum, Collisions)
0625/43/O/N/23 Q3

Fig. 3.1 shows a boy throwing a ball at an object in a fairground.

The ball has a mass of 190 g and travels horizontally with a constant speed of 6.9 m/s.

(a) Calculate the momentum of the ball. [2]

First, convert the mass from grams (g) to kilograms (kg):
190 g = 190 / 1000 = 0.19 kg.

Using the formula: momentum = mass × velocity (p = mv).

p = 0.19 kg × 6.9 m/s

p = 1.311 kg m/s (or 1.3 kg m/s)

Recap

Recap

Momentum (p) is a property of a moving object, defined as its mass multiplied by its velocity. It is a vector quantity, meaning it has both size and direction. The standard unit for momentum is kg m/s.

(b) After hitting the object, the ball bounces back along the same straight path with a speed of 1.5 m/s.

The object has a mass of 1.8 kg.

Calculate the speed of the object after it is hit by the ball. [3]

We use the Principle of Conservation of Momentum:
Total momentum before collision = Total momentum after collision

Before collision:
Momentum of ball = 1.311 kg m/s (from part a)
Momentum of object = 1.8 kg × 0 m/s = 0 kg m/s
Total momentum before = 1.311 + 0 = 1.311 kg m/s

After collision:
Momentum of ball = m × v = 0.19 kg × (-1.5 m/s) = -0.285 kg m/s
(The sign is negative because it bounces *back* in the opposite direction).

Momentum of object = M × V = 1.8 kg × V (where V is the unknown speed).

Set before = after:
1.311 = -0.285 + (1.8 × V)
1.311 + 0.285 = 1.8 × V
1.596 = 1.8 × V
V = 1.596 / 1.8
V = 0.887 m/s (or 0.89 m/s)

Recap

Recap

The Principle of Conservation of Momentum states that in the absence of external forces (like friction), the total momentum of a system remains constant during a collision.

Because momentum is a vector, you must account for direction. It is a standard convention to make the initial direction of motion positive, so any object moving in the opposite direction after the collision (rebounding) has a negative velocity.

(c) The kinetic energy of the ball is 4.5 J before the collision and 0.2 J after the collision.

Calculate the change in total kinetic energy of the ball and object during the collision. [3]

We use the formula for Kinetic Energy (KE): Ek = 1⁄2mv2.

Total KE before collision:
KE of ball = 4.5 J (given)
KE of object = 1⁄2 × (1.8 kg) × (0 m/s)2 = 0 J
Total KE before = 4.5 + 0 = 4.5 J

Total KE after collision:
KE of ball = 0.2 J (given)
KE of object = 1⁄2MV2 = 1⁄2 × (1.8 kg) × (0.887 m/s)2
KE of object = 0.9 × 0.7868 = 0.708 J
Total KE after = 0.2 J + 0.708 J = 0.908 J

Change in total KE:
Change = Total KE before - Total KE after
Change = 4.5 J - 0.908 J = 3.592 J

(The mark scheme gives 3.8 J or 3.6 J, the difference is due to rounding in part (b) or the given KE values. Using V=0.89 m/s gives KE=0.713 J, total=0.913 J, change=3.587 J. Using V=0.88 m/s gives KE=0.697 J, total=0.897 J, change=3.603 J. We will use 3.6 J as a rounded value).

Recap

Recap

Kinetic Energy (Ek) is the energy an object has due to its motion. Unlike momentum, Ek is a scalar quantity (it has no direction) and is not always conserved in a collision.

Collisions where kinetic energy is *not* conserved are called inelastic collisions. The "lost" energy is transformed into other stores, such as internal (thermal) energy and sound energy.

Topic: Thermal Physics (Gas Laws)
0625/43/O/N/23 Q4

(a) The lowest possible temperature is zero kelvin (0K).

(i) State the name of this lowest possible temperature. [1]

Absolute zero.

Recap

Recap

Absolute zero is the lowest theoretical temperature, at which particles of matter have their minimum internal energy. It is the starting point of the Kelvin (K) temperature scale, and is equal to –273 °C.

(ii) Nitrogen boils at 77 K.

Calculate the boiling point of nitrogen on the Celsius scale. [2]

To convert from Kelvin (K) to Celsius (°C), use the formula:
Temperature in °C = Temperature in K - 273

Temperature in °C = 77 - 273

Temperature = -196 °C

Recap

Recap

The Kelvin scale is the absolute temperature scale, where 0 K is absolute zero. The size of one degree Celsius is the same as one kelvin.

  • To convert °C to K: K = °C + 273
  • To convert K to °C: °C = K - 273

(b) The temperature of a fixed mass of gas at constant volume changes from 300 K to 400 K.

State and explain, in terms of particles, the effect on the pressure of the gas. [4]

Statement: The pressure of the gas increased.

Explanation: 1. When the temperature increases, the gas particles gain kinetic energy and move faster.
2. Because they move faster, the particles collide with the walls of the container more frequently.
3. Also, each collision involves a larger change in momentum, so the particles collide with the walls with a larger force.
4. Since pressure is the total force per unit area, the increased frequency and force of collisions cause the pressure to increase.

Recap

Recap

This relationship is described by the Pressure Law. For a fixed mass of gas at constant volume, the pressure is directly proportional to the absolute temperature (in Kelvin). This is because gas pressure is caused by the bombardment of particles on the container walls.

(c) A sample of gas is at a pressure of 120 kPa. The volume of the gas is doubled at constant temperature.

Calculate the new pressure of the gas. [2]

At constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional (p ∝ 1/V), which is Boyle's Law.

This can be written as P1V1 = P2V2.

Let V1 = V and P1 = 120 kPa.
Then V2 = 2V and P2 = ?.

(120 kPa) × V = P2 × (2V)

Divide both sides by V:

120 = P2 × 2

P2 = 120 / 2

P2 = 60 kPa

(Alternatively, since the volume is doubled, the pressure must be halved: 120 / 2 = 60 kPa).

Recap

Recap

Boyle's Law states that for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, the pressure is inversely proportional to its volume (pV = constant).

This is because in a larger volume, the particles are further apart and collide with the container walls less frequently, resulting in lower pressure.

(b) Fig. 6.2 shows an electric circuit.

(i) Calculate the reading on the voltmeter. [2]

The voltmeter is connected in parallel with the two power sources.

The 12.0 V and 3.0 V sources are connected in series-opposing (the positive terminal of one is connected to the positive terminal of the other via the switch).

The resultant voltage (e.m.f.) is the difference between them:

Resultant V = 12.0 V - 3.0 V = 9.0 V.

The voltmeter reads the potential difference across this combination.

Recap

Recap

When voltage sources are in series, you check their orientation.

  • Series-aiding (positive to negative): Voltages add up (Vtotal = V1 + V2).
  • Series-opposing (positive to positive): Voltages subtract (Vtotal = V1 - V2).
A voltmeter is always connected in parallel and measures the potential difference (voltage) *across* the points it is connected to.

(ii) Calculate the current in the 4.2 Ω resistor. [2]

The diode in the top branch is in forward-biased direction (conventional current + to -), so current flows.

The voltage across the parallel branches is 9.0 V (from part b(i)).

Using Ohm's Law (V = IR), rearrange to I = V/R.

I = 9.0 V / 4.2 Ω

I = 2.14 A (or 2.1 A)

Recap

Recap

Ohm's Law (V=IR) is the fundamental relationship between voltage (V), current (I), and resistance (R). A diode is a component that only allows current to flow in one direction (the direction its arrow points).

(iii) Determine the current in the 2.1 Ω resistor. [1]

The diode in the bottom branch is reverse-biased (the arrow points against the conventional current flow from + to -).

Therefore, no current flows through the bottom branch.

Current = 0 A.

Recap

Recap

A diode acts like a one-way street for current.

  • Forward-biased: Current flows (if voltage is high enough).
  • Reverse-biased: No current (or a negligible tiny current) flows.
This circuit shows conventional current flow from the positive (longer line) of the *resultant* 9.0V source (i.e., from the 12V positive) towards the negative.

Total current = Current in top branch + Current in bottom branch.

Total current = 2.14 A + 0 A

Recap

Recap

This is Kirchhoff's First Law (Current Law): The total current entering a junction must equal the total current leaving the junction. An ammeter is always connected in series to measure the current *flowing through* that part of the circuit.

P = 19.26 W (or 19 W)

P = (2.14 A)2 × 4.2 Ω = 4.5796 × 4.2 = 19.23 W

Alternatively, using P = V2/R:

(All methods give approx. 19 W).

Recap

Recap

Electrical power (P) is the rate at which energy is transferred. It is measured in Watts (W), where 1 Watt = 1 Joule per second.

You can calculate power using any of these three formulas:

  • P = V × I
  • P = I2 × R
  • P = V2/R

(b) A piece of plastic is charged positively by friction.

State what charge transfers occur during this process. [2]

Electrons (which are negative charges) are transferred from the plastic (e.g., to the cloth used for rubbing).

Recap

Recap

Charging by friction is a process of electron transfer. Only the electrons are mobile; the positive protons are fixed in the nuclei of the atoms.

  • An object becomes positively charged when it loses electrons.
  • An object becomes negatively charged when it gains electrons.

(c) Explain how the structure of an electrical conductor differs from the structure of an electrical insulator. [2]

Electrical conductors (e.g., metals) have free or delocalised electrons that are able to move throughout the structure and carry charge.

Electrical insulators (e.g., plastic) do not have free electrons. Their electrons are all tightly bound to their atoms and cannot move to carry a current.

Recap

Recap

The presence of mobile charge carriers is what separates conductors from insulators. In metals, these carriers are delocalised electrons. In ionic solutions (electrolytes), the carriers are mobile ions.

Question 9

Topic: Nuclear Physics (Radioactivity)
0625/43/O/N/23 Q9

(a) For each application of radioactive isotopes, state and explain which type of radioactive emission is suitable and suggest an appropriate half-life for the isotope.

(i) household smoke alarm [3]

Type of radioactive emission: Alpha (α).

Explanation: Alpha particles are highly ionising. They ionise the air particles in the detector's chamber, allowing a small current to flow. When smoke particles enter, they block the alpha particles, the current drops, and the alarm sounds. Beta and gamma are not ionising enough and are too penetrating.

Half-life: A long half-life (e.g., 100-500 years) is needed so the source remains sufficiently active for the useful lifetime of the smoke detector without needing replacement.

Recap

Recap

Radioactive emissions have different properties:

  • Alpha (α): Helium nucleus. Highly ionising, least penetrating (stopped by paper).
  • Beta (β): High-speed electron. Medium ionising, medium penetrating (stopped by thin aluminium).
  • Gamma (γ): EM wave. Weakly ionising, most penetrating (stopped by thick lead/concrete).

A half-life is the time taken for half the unstable nuclei in a sample to decay.

(ii) measuring the thickness of aluminium strips produced in a factory [3]

Type of radioactive emission: Beta (β).

Explanation: Beta particles can pass through aluminium, but their absorption depends on the thickness. A detector on the other side measures the count rate. If the strip is too thick, the count rate drops; if too thin, it rises. Alpha would be stopped completely, and gamma would pass through almost unaffected.

Half-life: A long half-life (e.g., 10-20 years) is needed so the source provides a consistent count rate over long periods of industrial use.

Recap

Recap

This is a common industrial use. The choice of isotope is crucial. It must be able to penetrate the material being measured, but also be absorbed enough for variations in thickness to be detectable. Beta is perfect for paper or thin aluminium, while gamma might be used for thicker steel.

(b) Lead-208 (20882Pb) has the highest nucleon number of the stable isotopes of lead.

Explain why Lead-214 (21482Pb) is radioactive. [2]

Lead-214 is radioactive because its nucleus is unstable.

This instability is because it has too many neutrons (132 neutrons, compared to 82 protons) for the number of protons. It has an imbalanced neutron-to-proton ratio compared to stable isotopes like Lead-208. It will undergo decay (specifically beta decay) to reduce the number of neutrons and become more stable.

Recap

Recap

The stability of a nucleus depends on the ratio of neutrons to protons. For light elements, this ratio is about 1:1. For heavy elements like lead, more neutrons are needed (e.g., 126 neutrons for 82 protons in 20882Pb), but there is a limit.

Isotopes are atoms with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. Radioactive isotopes (radioisotopes) have unstable nuclei and emit radiation to become more stable.

(ii) The numerical value of H0 is 2.2 × 10-18. State the unit of H0. [1]

s-1 (per second).

(This is the SI unit. It is derived from (m/s) / m. It is more commonly, but not in SI units, expressed as km/s per Mpc).

Recap

Recap

Since v (speed) has units of m/s and d (distance) has units of m, the unit for H0 is (m/s) / m = 1/s = s-1.

(iii) Use this value of H0 to determine an estimate for the age of the Universe in seconds. [2]

The age of the Universe (T) can be estimated as the reciprocal of the Hubble constant.

Age ≈ 1/H0

Age ≈ 1 / (2.2 × 10-18 s-1)

Age ≈ 4.545... × 1017 s (or 4.5 × 1017 s)

Recap

Recap

If the universe has been expanding at a constant rate H0 since the Big Bang, then the time it has taken for a galaxy to reach its current distance d (while moving at speed v) is T = d/v.

Since H0 = v/d, it follows that 1/H0 = d/v. Therefore, T = 1/H0. This gives an estimate for the age of the Universe.

(b) State when cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR) was formed and where we detect it coming from. [2]

When: Shortly after the Big Bang (approximately 380,000 years after).

Where: From all points in space / all directions / uniformly from the entire Universe.

Recap

Recap

The Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation (CMBR) is the "afterglow" of the Big Bang. It is thermal radiation that filled the early Universe. As the Universe expanded, this radiation cooled and stretched, and it is now detected as microwaves with a temperature of about 2.7 K.

It is considered one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the Big Bang theory.
