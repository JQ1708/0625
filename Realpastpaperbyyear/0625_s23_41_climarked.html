Question 1 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Motion, Vectors, Forces

Fig. 1.1 shows a straight section of a river where the water is flowing from right to left at a speed of 0.54 m/s.

A swimmer starts at point P and swims at a constant speed of 0.72 m/s relative to the water and at right angles to the current.

(a) (i) Determine, relative to the river bank, both the magnitude and direction of the swimmer's velocity.

[4]

The swimmer's velocity and the river current's velocity are vectors acting at right angles. The resultant velocity can be found using Pythagoras' theorem for the magnitude and trigonometry for the direction.

Magnitude:
v2 = (0.72 m/s)2 + (0.54 m/s)2
v2 = 0.5184 + 0.2916 = 0.81
v = √0.81 = 0.90 m/s

Direction:
We find the angle (θ) relative to the direction the swimmer is pointing (across the river).
tan(θ) = opposite⁄adjacent = 0.54⁄0.72
θ = tan−1(0.75) = 36.87°
The question asks for the direction relative to the riverbank. This is the angle relative to the "across the river" direction, which is 90° to the bank. A more common interpretation is the angle with the bank itself, which would be 90° - 36.87° = 53° (approx). The mark scheme accepts 53° to the riverbank.

Recap

Vectors vs. Scalars (Ref: p. 11)

Scalar quantities have size (magnitude) only (e.g., speed, distance, mass, time).

Vector quantities have both size (magnitude) and direction (e.g., velocity, displacement, force, acceleration).

When two vectors act at right angles, their resultant (combined effect) is found using Pythagoras for the magnitude and trigonometry (SOH CAH TOA) for the direction.

(a) (ii) After 1.5 minutes, the swimmer reaches point Q.

Calculate the distance between P and Q.

[3]

First, convert the time into seconds:
Time = 1.5 minutes × 60 seconds/minute = 90 s.

The distance between P and Q is the straight-line path the swimmer travels, using the resultant velocity (magnitude) calculated in part (i).

Using the equation distance = speed × time (or s = v × t):
Distance = 0.90 m/s × 90 s = 81 m.

Recap

The Speed Equation (Ref: p. 10)

The relationship between average speed, distance, and time is given by:

speed (m/s) = distance (m)⁄time (s)

This can be rearranged to find distance (s = v × t) or time (t = s⁄v).

(b) When the swimmer is crossing the river, his actions produce a constant forward force on his body.

Explain why he moves at a constant speed.

[2]

The swimmer moves at a constant speed (constant velocity relative to the water) because the forces acting on him are balanced. The constant forward force from his swimming balances the backward force of friction (or drag) from the water.

When forces are balanced, there is no resultant force, which means there is no acceleration (Newton's First Law). Therefore, the swimmer moves at a constant velocity.

Recap

Newton's First Law (Ref: p. 25)

This law describes the effect of forces on motion:

"An object remains at rest or continues in a straight line with constant speed, unless a resultant force acts on it."

Frictional forces, like air resistance or water drag, always act opposite to the direction of motion.

Question 2 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Motion, Forces, Circular Motion

Fig. 2.1 shows a motorcyclist accelerating along a straight horizontal section of track.

The motorcyclist and motorcycle have a combined mass of 240 kg.

(a) On the straight horizontal section of the track, the motorcyclist accelerates from rest at 7.2 m/s2.

(i) The motorcyclist reaches the end of the straight section of track in 5.3 s.

Calculate the speed of the motorcyclist at the end of the straight section.

[2]

We can use the equation for acceleration:
a = (v - u)⁄t, where a is acceleration, v is final speed, u is initial speed, and t is time.

Rearranging for final speed (v): v = u + (a × t)
The motorcyclist starts "from rest", so u = 0 m/s.

v = 0 + (7.2 m/s2 × 5.3 s)
v = 38.16 m/s

Rounding to two significant figures (as in the data): 38 m/s.

Recap

Calculating Acceleration (Ref: p. 15)

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. The equation is:

acceleration (m/s2) = change in velocity (m/s)⁄time (s)
a = (v - u)⁄t

(a) (ii) Calculate the resultant force on the motorcyclist and motorcycle on the straight section of track.

[2]

We use Newton's Second Law: F = m × a, where F is resultant force, m is mass, and a is acceleration.

F = 240 kg × 7.2 m/s2
F = 1728 N

Rounding to two significant figures: 1700 N.

Recap

Newton's Second Law (Ref: p. 25)

This law relates force, mass, and acceleration. The equation is:

Force (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m/s2)
F = m × a

A resultant force on an object will always cause it to accelerate.

(b) At the end of the straight section, the track remains horizontal but bends to the right, as shown in Fig. 2.1.

When the motorcyclist reaches the bend, she travels around the bend in a circular path at a constant speed.

(i) Velocity is a vector quantity.

State how a vector quantity differs from a scalar quantity.

[1]

A scalar quantity has only magnitude (size), whereas a vector quantity has both magnitude and direction.

Recap

Vector and Scalar Quantities (Ref: p. 11)

  • Scalar Examples: Speed, Distance, Mass, Time, Energy, Temperature
  • Vector Examples: Velocity, Displacement, Force, Acceleration, Momentum

(b) (ii) Describe what happens to the velocity of the motorcyclist as she travels around the bend at constant speed.

[1]

Even though the speed is constant, the velocity changes. This is because velocity is a vector, and the direction of motion is continuously changing as the motorcyclist moves around the bend.

Recap

Motion in a Circle (Ref: p. 26)

For an object moving in a circle, its speed can be constant, but its velocity is always changing because its direction is always changing.

Since velocity is changing, the object is accelerating.

(b) (iii) Explain why there must be a resultant force on the motorcyclist as she travels around the bend.

[2]

As established in (b)(ii), the motorcyclist's velocity is changing (due to the change in direction). A change in velocity is an acceleration.

According to Newton's Second Law (F=ma), if there is an acceleration, there must be a resultant force causing it. This force, known as the centripetal force, acts perpendicular to the motion (towards the centre of the circle).

Recap

Centripetal Force (Ref: p. 26)

The resultant force which causes an object to move in a circle is called the centripetal force.

This force acts at a right angle (perpendicular) to the direction of motion, pulling the object towards the centre of the circle. It constantly changes the direction of the object's velocity, but not its speed.

Question 3 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Thermal Physics, Convection, Kinetic Theory

A rubber balloon is inflated with helium and sealed so that no helium escapes.

The balloon is positioned immediately below the ceiling in a room.

Heaters are switched on and the temperature of the air in the room increases.

(a) When the heaters are first switched on, the temperature of the air immediately below the ceiling increases more quickly than the temperature of the air in the rest of the room.

Explain why this happens.

[2]

This happens due to convection. The air near the heaters gets warmed, its particles move faster and further apart, making the air less dense.

Because this warm, less dense air is lighter than the surrounding cooler air, it rises to the ceiling, displacing the cooler, more dense air downwards to be heated.

Recap

Convection (Ref: p. 70)

Convection is a method of heat transfer that occurs only in fluids (liquids and gases), where particles are free to move.

  1. A part of the fluid is heated.
  2. Its particles gain kinetic energy and move further apart.
  3. This section of fluid becomes less dense.
  4. The warm, less dense fluid rises.
  5. Cooler, more dense fluid sinks to take its place, setting up a convection current.

(b) The temperature of the helium in the balloon increases and as the rubber stretches, the volume occupied by the helium increases.

(i) State what happens to the motion of the helium particles as the temperature increases.

[1]

As the temperature increases, the particles gain kinetic energy, so their average speed/velocity increases and they move faster.

Recap

Kinetic Theory of Gases (Ref: p. 59)

The temperature of a gas is a measure of the average kinetic energy of its particles. As the temperature of a gas increases, the average speed of the particles increases.

(b) (ii) As the rubber stretches and the volume of the helium increases, the pressure of the helium remains constant.

Explain, in terms of the particles of helium, how the pressure of the helium remains constant.

[3]

Gas pressure is caused by particles colliding with the walls of the container. There are two competing effects happening:

  1. Temperature Effect: Because the temperature increases, particles move faster and collide with the balloon walls harder (with more force/momentum change). This would normally increase the pressure.
  2. Volume Effect: Because the volume increases, the surface area is larger. This means the particles collide with the walls less frequently, as they have further to travel between collisions. This would normally decrease the pressure.

In this case, the effect of the larger volume (less frequent collisions) cancels out the effect of the increased temperature (harder collisions), resulting in the overall pressure remaining constant.

Recap

Gas Pressure (Ref: p. 59)

Gas particles are in constant, random motion. When they hit the sides of the container, they rebound, exerting a force on the walls. Pressure is the total force exerted per unit area (P = F⁄A).

  • Increasing temperature (at constant volume) increases pressure (harder, more frequent collisions).
  • Decreasing volume (at constant temperature) increases pressure (more frequent collisions).
Question 4 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Energy, Specific Heat Capacity, Efficiency

A student investigates the efficiency of a filament lamp. Fig. 4.1 shows the filament lamp with its glass bulb immersed in water in a beaker.

The reading on the thermometer in the water is 19.0 °C. ... The lamp is switched on. At the end of the experiment, the temperature of the water is 21.5 °C.

(a) The mass of the water in the beaker is 600 g and the specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg°C).

(i) Show that the increase in the internal energy of the water is 6300 J.

[3]

We use the specific heat capacity equation: ΔE = m × c × Δθ

First, convert mass to kilograms:
m = 600 g = 0.60 kg

Next, find the change in temperature (Δθ):
Δθ = Final Temp - Initial Temp = 21.5 °C - 19.0 °C = 2.5 °C

Now, calculate the energy change (ΔE):
ΔE = 0.60 kg × 4200 J/(kg°C) × 2.5 °C
ΔE = 6300 J

Recap

Specific Heat Capacity (Ref: p. 65)

The specific heat capacity (c) of a material is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of the material by 1 °C.

The equation is:
Energy Transferred (J) = mass (kg) × specific heat capacity (J/kg°C) × temperature change (°C)
ΔE = mcΔθ

(a) (ii) In the experiment, the lamp is switched on for 500 s. The power supplied to the filament lamp is 13 W. The useful energy from the lamp is transferred as light. The energy that increases the temperature of the water is wasted energy.

Determine the maximum possible efficiency of the filament lamp.

[4]

1. Calculate Total Energy Input:
Energy = Power × Time (E = P × t)
Total Energy Input = 13 W × 500 s = 6500 J

2. Identify Wasted and Useful Energy:
Wasted Energy (as heat) = Energy absorbed by water = 6300 J (from part (i)).
Useful Energy Output (as light) = Total Input - Wasted (heat)
Useful Energy Output = 6500 J - 6300 J = 200 J

3. Calculate Efficiency:
Efficiency = (Useful Energy Output⁄Total Energy Input)
Efficiency = 200 J⁄6500 J = 0.03076...

As a percentage: 0.03076... × 100% = 3.1% (to 2 s.f.)

Recap

Efficiency and Power (Ref: p. 45, 49)

Power is the rate of energy transfer:
Power (W) = Energy (J)⁄Time (s) or E = P × t

Efficiency is a measure of how much input energy is converted to useful output energy:
Efficiency = (Useful Energy Output⁄Total Energy Input) × 100%

(b) The efficiency of the lamp is less than the value determined in (a)(ii).

Suggest one reason for this.

[1]

The calculation assumes *all* the wasted heat (6300 J) was captured by the water. In reality, some thermal energy is transferred from the water to the surroundings (e.g., the beaker, the air, the bench) and not measured.

Alternatively: Some water evaporates from the surface, which causes cooling and removes energy that is not measured by the thermometer.

Recap

Heat Loss (Ref: p. 68, 73)

In any experiment involving heat, energy is inevitably lost to the surroundings through conduction (through the beaker walls), convection (from the water surface to the air), and radiation. This makes the measured efficiency appear higher than it truly is, or the measured heat gain lower than the true heat loss.

Question 5 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Light, Refraction, Total Internal Reflection

Fig. 5.1 shows a block ABCD made of glass that has a refractive index of 1.5. ...

A ray of monochromatic light enters the block through the curved side AB and strikes side BC at P. Some light emerges into the air and some is reflected.

(a) State what is meant by monochromatic.

[1]

Monochromatic light is light (or electromagnetic radiation) of a single frequency or single wavelength.

Recap

Monochromatic Light (Ref: p. 95)

White light is a mixture of many different frequencies (which we see as the colours of the spectrum). Monochromatic light (e.g., from a laser) is light of a single frequency. When it passes through a prism, it is refracted (bent) but not dispersed (split).

(b) Explain why the ray of light does not change direction when it enters the block through side AB.

[2]

The ray of light is travelling along a radius of the circular side AB, with the centre at P. This means the ray strikes the curved surface at a 90° angle (perpendicular) to the tangent at that point.

The ray is therefore travelling along the normal to the surface. When the angle of incidence is 0°, refraction does not cause any change in direction.

Recap

Refraction (Ref: p. 88)

Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium to another. The bending happens because the speed of light changes.

However, if a ray of light enters a new medium along the normal (i.e., at an angle of incidence of 0°), it changes speed but does not change direction.

(c) Show that the critical angle c for glass of refractive index 1.5 is 42°.

[2]

The formula relating refractive index (n) and the critical angle (c) is:
n = 1⁄sin(c)

Rearranging for c:
sin(c) = 1⁄n
sin(c) = 1⁄1.5
sin(c) = 0.6667...
c = sin−1(0.6667...)
c = 41.8°, which rounds to 42°.

Recap

Critical Angle Equation (Ref: p. 90)

The critical angle (c) is the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the less dense medium is 90°.

The equation is: n = 1⁄sin(c), where n is the refractive index of the denser medium.

(d) Fig. 5.1 shows that the angle between the ray of light and line AP is θ, where line AP is at right angles to side BC.

Angle θ increases to 45°.

(i) State and explain what happens to the light that strikes P.

[2]

What happens: Total internal reflection occurs, and all the light is reflected back into the glass block.

Explanation: The angle of incidence (θ) is 45°. This angle is greater than the critical angle (c = 42°). When light travels from a denser to a less dense medium (glass to air) at an angle greater than the critical angle, it undergoes total internal reflection.

Recap

Total Internal Reflection (TIR) (Ref: p. 90)

TIR occurs when two conditions are met:

  1. Light is travelling from a more optically dense medium to a less optically dense medium (e.g., glass to air).
  2. The angle of incidence (i) is greater than the critical angle (c).

When TIR occurs, no light is refracted; all of it is reflected.

(d) (ii) When θ = 45°, the reflected light strikes side CD.

Describe what happens when this reflected light strikes side CD.

[1]

The ray is reflected from P at 45° (since angle of incidence = angle of reflection). It travels to side CD. The normal to side CD is parallel to line AP.

The reflected ray is travelling at 45° to the normal of side CD. Since this angle of incidence (45°) is greater than the critical angle (42°), the ray undergoes total internal reflection again.

Recap

Law of Reflection (Ref: p. 87)

The law of reflection states: angle of incidence (i) = angle of reflection (r). This law applies to *all* reflection, including total internal reflection. The angles are always measured relative to the normal.

Question 6 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Waves, EM Spectrum, Communication

A mobile phone (cell phone) network uses microwaves of frequency 1.9 × 109 Hz to transmit and receive signals.

The speed of microwaves in air is 3.0 × 108 m/s.

(a) Calculate the wavelength of these microwaves in air.

[2]

We use the wave equation: v = f × λ (speed = frequency × wavelength)

Rearranging for wavelength (λ):
λ = v⁄f
λ = (3.0 × 108 m/s)⁄(1.9 × 109 Hz)
λ = 0.158 m (or 0.16 m)

Recap

The Wave Equation (Ref: p. 79)

This equation applies to all waves in the electromagnetic spectrum, as well as sound and water waves.

velocity (m/s) = frequency (Hz) × wavelength (m)
v = f × λ

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum: 3.0 × 108 m/s.

(b) State two reasons why microwaves are used for mobile phone (cell phone) signals.

[2]

  1. They can penetrate (some) walls and buildings, allowing for indoor reception.
  2. Their wavelength is small, so they only need short aerials (antennas) in the handset.
  3. (Also: They can pass through the Earth's atmosphere to communicate with satellites.)

Recap

Uses of Microwaves (Ref: p. 96-97)

Microwaves are used for:

  • Rapid cooking (they are absorbed by water molecules)
  • Communication with satellites (they pass through the atmosphere)
  • Mobile phones and WiFi (they can penetrate some walls and require short aerials)

(c) All mobile phone (cell phone) networks use digital signals to communicate with the phone.

(i) Describe, with the aid of a diagram, how a digital signal differs from an analogue signal.

[3]

An analogue signal is continuously variable; it can have any value within a given range.

A digital signal is discrete; it consists of only two values (or a few set levels), typically represented as 'on' (1) and 'off' (0).

Recap

Analogue vs. Digital (Ref: p. 97)

An analogue signal, like a sound wave, is a continuous wave that mirrors the original information.

A digital signal converts this information into a series of numbers (binary 1s and 0s), which are transmitted as 'on' or 'off' pulses. This makes the signal much more robust.

(c) (ii) State two advantages of using digital signals rather than analogue signals.

[2]

  1. Noise (interference) is easily removed from the signal. The signal can be perfectly regenerated, maintaining its quality.
  2. A faster data transmission rate is possible, allowing more information (like video or data) to be sent.
  3. The signal can be transmitted over longer distances because it can be regenerated without amplifying the noise.

Recap

Benefits of Digital Signalling (Ref: p. 97)

Analogue signals pick up noise (hiss, static) which gets amplified along with the signal, reducing quality. Digital signals can be "cleaned" at repeater stations, as the receiver only needs to distinguish between 'on' and 'off'. This allows for increased range and a higher rate of data transmission.

Question 7 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Electricity, Circuits, Potential Dividers

Fig. 7.1 shows a circuit that contains a battery, a switch, a voltmeter and three 40 Ω resistors, R₁, R₂ and R₃.

The switch is open and resistors R₁ and R₂ form a potential divider.

(a) Describe what is meant by a potential divider.

[2]

A potential divider is a circuit, typically made of two (or more) resistors connected in series, that is used to splits (or divides) the e.m.f. (voltage) of a power source.

The total voltage is shared between the components in proportion to their resistances, allowing a specific, lower voltage to be drawn from the circuit.

Recap

Voltage in Series Circuits (Ref: p. 152)

In a series circuit, the total voltage (e.m.f.) from the supply is shared between the components.

V_total = V₁ + V₂ + ...

The component with the largest resistance will have the largest share of the voltage (V=IR, and I is the same for all components).

(b) The reading on the voltmeter is 7.5 V.

(i) Calculate the electromotive force (e.m.f.) of the battery.

[1]

With the switch open, R₁ and R₂ are in series. They are both 40 Ω resistors.

The voltmeter reads the voltage across R₁, which is 7.5 V.

Since the total e.m.f. is shared in proportion to resistance, and R₁ and R₂ have *equal* resistance (40 Ω), they must also share the voltage *equally*. Therefore, the voltage across R₂ is also 7.5 V.

The total e.m.f. is the sum of the voltages:
e.m.f. = V₁ + V₂ = 7.5 V + 7.5 V = 15 V.

Recap

Potential Divider Logic (Ref: p. 152)

For two resistors in series (R₁ and R₂) connected to a supply (V_in), the output voltage (V_out) across R₁ is:

V_out = V_in × (R₁⁄(R₁ + R₂))

In this case: 7.5 V = V_in × (40⁄(40 + 40)) = V_in × (40⁄80) = V_in × 0.5
Therefore, V_in = 7.5 V⁄0.5 = 15 V.

(b) (ii) The switch is closed.

Calculate the resistance of the complete circuit.

[3]

1. Calculate Parallel Resistance: When the switch is closed, R₂ and R₃ are in parallel. Both are 40 Ω.
1⁄R_parallel = 1⁄R₂ + 1⁄R₃ = 1⁄40 + 1⁄40 = 2⁄40
R_parallel = 40⁄2 = 20 Ω

2. Calculate Total Resistance: This parallel combination (20 Ω) is in series with R₁ (40 Ω).
R_total = R₁ + R_parallel = 40 Ω + 20 Ω = 60 Ω.

Recap

Resistors in Parallel (Ref: p. 154)

For resistors in parallel, the total resistance (R_total) is found using the formula:

1⁄R_total = 1⁄R₁ + 1⁄R₂ + ...

A shortcut for two equal resistors in parallel is that the combined resistance is half the value of one (e.g., two 40 Ω resistors in parallel become 20 Ω).

(c) Calculate the reading on the voltmeter when the switch is closed.

[2]

The voltmeter measures the voltage across R₁ (40 Ω).

We know from (b) that the e.m.f. is 15 V and the total circuit resistance is 60 Ω.

1. Find Total Current (I):
I = V⁄R_total = 15 V⁄60 Ω = 0.25 A

2. Find Voltage across R₁ (V₁):
This total current (0.25 A) flows through R₁.
V₁ = I × R₁ = 0.25 A × 40 Ω = 10 V.

The voltmeter will read 10 V.

Recap

Ohm's Law (Ref: p. 136)

The relationship between voltage, current, and resistance is given by Ohm's Law:

Voltage (V) = Current (A) × Resistance (Ω)
V = I × R

This can be applied to a single component or to the entire circuit.

Question 8 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Electromagnetism, Transformers

The electricity supplied to a town is transmitted using a high-voltage cable. A transformer in the town has a soft-iron core.

(a) Explain the principle of operation of a simple iron-cored transformer.

[4]

  1. An alternating current (a.c.) flows through the primary coil.
  2. This produces a continuously changing magnetic field in the soft iron core.
  3. The iron core concentrates this changing field and links it to the secondary coil.
  4. The changing magnetic field cuts across the wires of the secondary coil, inducing an e.m.f. (voltage) across it (this is electromagnetic induction).

Recap

Transformer Principle (Ref: p. 174)

A transformer works on the principle of electromagnetic induction. It requires a changing magnetic field to work, which is why it only functions with alternating current (a.c.) and not direct current (d.c.).

The soft iron core is easily magnetised and demagnetised, making it efficient at transferring the changing magnetic field from the primary to the secondary coil.

(b) The transformer steps the supply voltage down from 220 000 V to 33 000 V.

(i) There are 450 turns on the secondary coil.

Calculate the number of turns on the primary coil.

[2]

We use the transformer turns-voltage ratio equation:
(Vp⁄Vs) = (Np⁄Ns)

Where:

  • Vp (primary voltage) = 220 000 V
  • Vs (secondary voltage) = 33 000 V
  • Np (primary turns) = ?
  • Ns (secondary turns) = 450

Rearranging for Np:
Np = Ns × (Vp⁄Vs)
Np = 450 × (220 000⁄33 000)
Np = 450 × 6.667...
Np = 3000 (turns)

Recap

The Transformer Equation (Ref: p. 176)

The ratio of the voltages across the coils is equal to the ratio of the number of turns on the coils:

Vp⁄Vs = Np⁄Ns

  • Step-up transformer: Ns > Np (voltage increases)
  • Step-down transformer: Ns < Np (voltage decreases)

(b) (ii) The electrical power transferred to the transformer by the high-voltage cable is 77 MW.

Calculate the current in the primary coil.

[3]

First, convert Megawatts (MW) to Watts (W):
Power (P) = 77 MW = 77,000,000 W (or 7.7 × 107 W)

We use the power equation: P = I × V (Power = Current × Voltage)

This applies to the primary coil: Pp = Ip × Vp
Rearranging for current (Ip):
Ip = Pp⁄Vp
Ip = 77,000,000 W⁄220,000 V
Ip = 350 A

Recap

Power in Transformers (Ref: p. 177)

For an ideal (100% efficient) transformer, the power in the primary coil equals the power in the secondary coil: P_in = P_out.

The equation for electrical power is:
P = I × V (Power = Current × Voltage)

Therefore, Ip × Vp = Is × Vs

Question 9 | 0625/41/M/J/23 | Topics: Nuclear Physics, Radioactivity, Half-life

Fig. 9.1 represents all the particles in a neutral atom of a radioactive isotope X1.

(a) Determine the number of neutrons in this atom and explain how the answer is obtained.

[2]

Number of neutrons = 7

Explanation:
In a neutral atom, the number of electrons = number of protons. There are 5 electrons in the shells, so there must be 5 protons (the white dots) in the nucleus.

The total number of particles in the nucleus (nucleon number) is 5 protons + 7 neutrons (grey dots) = 12.

Therefore, the number of neutrons = Nucleon Number - Proton Number = 12 - 5 = 7.

Recap

Atomic Structure (Ref: p. 182)

  • Proton Number (Z): The number of protons in the nucleus. In a neutral atom, this also equals the number of electrons.
  • Nucleon Number (A): The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
  • Number of Neutrons = A - Z

(b) The isotope X1 is a beta emitter that decays to the stable isotope X2.

(i) Describe how a nucleus of X2 differs from a nucleus of X1.

[2]

During beta decay, a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton and an electron (the beta particle), which is ejected.

Therefore, the nucleus of X2 will have one fewer neutron and one more proton than the nucleus of X1. Its nucleon number (total protons + neutrons) remains the same (12).

Recap

Beta (β) Decay (Ref: p. 184)

In beta decay, a neutron in an unstable nucleus changes into a proton and an electron (the beta particle).

  • The Proton Number (Z) increases by 1.
  • The Nucleon Number (A) stays the same.
  • The Neutron Number (N) decreases by 1.

(b) (ii) Suggest why isotope X2 is stable whereas X1 is not stable.

[1]

Nuclei are stable when they have a balanced ratio of protons to neutrons. X1 (5 protons, 7 neutrons) is unstable.

X2 (6 protons, 6 neutrons) has a more stable ratio of protons to neutrons (a 1:1 ratio, which is very stable for light elements). X1 had too many (or excess) neutrons for its number of protons.

Recap

Nuclear Stability (Ref: p. 183)

Unstable nuclei (isotopes) are radioactive. They become stable by emitting radiation (alpha, beta, or gamma) to adjust their internal makeup.

Stability depends on the ratio of neutrons to protons. Light elements (like Carbon-12, with 6p and 6n) are often most stable with a 1:1 ratio.

(c) The half-life of X1 is approximately 20 ms.

(i) Define the term half-life.

[2]

Half-life is the time (taken) for the number of (radioactive) nuclei in a sample to decay (or be reduced) to halve.

Alternatively, it is the time taken for the *activity* or *rate of decay* of a sample to halve.

Recap

Half-Life (Ref: p. 186)

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for half of the unstable nuclei in a sample to decay.

It is a random process, but for a large number of nuclei, the half-life is a predictable constant, ranging from fractions of a second to billions of years.

(c) (ii) Suggest one reason why isotopes with very short half-lives are especially hazardous.

[1]

A very short half-life means that a large number of particles (a large amount of radiation) are produced in a short time.

This results in a high/large decay rate (high activity), which delivers a high dose of radiation quickly, making it very dangerous.

Recap

Hazards of Radiation (Ref: p. 189)

Radiation is hazardous because it is ionising. It can knock electrons off atoms in living cells, causing cell damage, mutations (which can lead to cancer), or cell death.

A source with a short half-life is intensely radioactive for a short period, while a source with a long half-life is less active but remains a hazard for a longer time.

Pluto is a dwarf planet. Fig. 10.1 shows the direction of motion of Pluto as it follows its elliptical orbit around the Sun.

Point X is the point in the orbit closest to the Sun and point Y is the point furthest away. The orbital speed of Pluto varies as it orbits the Sun.

(a) (i) Describe how the speed of Pluto varies as it moves from X to Y and then back to X.

[1]

As Pluto moves from X (closest) to Y (furthest), its speed decreases.

As it moves from Y back to X, its speed increases.

(It moves fastest at X and slowest at Y).

Recap

Elliptical Orbits (Ref: p. 206)

For a planet or comet in an elliptical orbit:

  • It speeds up as it gets closer to the Sun (stronger gravitational pull).
  • It slows down as it gets further from the Sun (weaker gravitational pull).

(a) (ii) Explain, in terms of energy transfers, why the speed of Pluto varies in this way.

[3]

The total energy (Kinetic Energy + Gravitational Potential Energy) of Pluto is constant.

Moving from X to Y (away from Sun): Pluto is moving "uphill" against the Sun's gravity. Its Kinetic Energy (KE) is transferred into Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE). As KE decreases, its speed decreases.

Moving from Y to X (towards Sun): Pluto is "falling" towards the Sun. Its GPE is transferred back into KE. As KE increases, its speed increases.

Recap

Energy in Orbits (Ref: p. 206)

The total energy of an orbiting body is conserved.

  • Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE): Increases as the object gets further from the Sun.
  • Kinetic Energy (KE): Energy of motion (KE = 1⁄2mv2). Increases as the object moves faster.

As the planet moves away from the Sun, KE is converted to GPE (it slows down). As it moves towards the Sun, GPE is converted to KE (it speeds up).

(b) The average temperature on the surface of Pluto is 43 K.

(i) Convert this temperature to a value in degrees Celsius (°C).

[1]

The conversion formula is: °C = K - 273

Temperature in °C = 43 - 273 = −230 °C

Recap

Kelvin and Celsius (Ref: p. 59)

The Kelvin (K) scale is the absolute temperature scale, where 0 K (absolute zero) is the lowest possible temperature.

To convert between Kelvin and Celsius:

temperature / K = temperature / °C + 273

(b) (ii) Pluto has a white surface, as shown in Fig. 10.2. As Pluto rotates, the white surface alternately faces towards and away from the Sun.

Explain how this affects the temperature of Pluto as it rotates on its own axis.

[2]

A white surface is a poor absorber and a good reflector of thermal (infrared) radiation.

When the white surface faces the Sun, it reflects much of the incoming solar radiation, so the temperature increases less than it would if the surface were black. When facing away from the Sun, a white surface is also a poor emitter, so it cools down more slowly. This leads to less variation in temperature during its rotation.

Recap

Thermal Radiation (Ref: p. 71)

The colour and texture of a surface affect how it absorbs and emits infrared (thermal) radiation:

  • Matt Black: Best absorber and best emitter.
  • Shiny Silver/White: Worst absorber (best reflector) and worst emitter.
