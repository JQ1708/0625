Question 1

Topics: Motion, Force, Acceleration

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q1

An aeroplane accelerates along a horizontal runway before take-off.

The aeroplane accelerates for 35s. The speed of the aeroplane when it takes off is 72 m/s.

Fig. 1.1 shows how the speed of the aeroplane varies between time t = 0 and t = 35 s.

(ii) The combined mass of the aeroplane, its passengers and its fuel on take-off is 1.1 × 105 kg.

Calculate the average resultant force on the aeroplane between t = 0 and t = 35 s. [2]

Calculation:

Resultant Force = mass × acceleration (Newton's Second Law)

Resultant Force = (1.1 × 105 kg) × (2.057... m/s2) (using the unrounded acceleration)

Resultant Force = 226285.7... N

Answer: 230 000 N or 2.3 × 105 N (to 2 significant figures)

Recap

The relationship between resultant force, mass, and acceleration is given by the equation:

force (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m/s2)

F = m × a

(iii) The force provided by the engines of the aeroplane is constant.

Give one possible explanation for the change in acceleration of the aeroplane between t = 0 and t = 35 s. [1]

As the aeroplane's speed increases, the air resistance (or drag) acting against it also increases. This opposing force reduces the *resultant* force, and since F=ma, a smaller resultant force means a smaller acceleration.

Recap

Drag is a frictional force that occurs when an object moves through a fluid (like air). The faster the object moves, the more air particles it hits per second, and the greater the drag force. The resultant force is the engine thrust *minus* the drag force.

(iv) On Fig. 1.2, sketch a graph to show how the acceleration of the aircraft varies between t = 0 and t = 35 s. [3]

The graph should be sketched as follows:

  • The line starts at its highest value on the acceleration (y) axis at t = 0.
  • The line is a downward-sloping line (either curved or straight, though curved is more accurate) showing that acceleration decreases over time.
  • The line does not reach zero acceleration by t = 35 s, as the speed-time graph is still curving upwards.

Recap

The acceleration of the plane is highest at the start (t=0) because its speed is zero, meaning there is no air resistance. The resultant force is at its maximum (Resultant Force = Engine Thrust). As speed increases, air resistance increases, which decreases the resultant force and therefore decreases the acceleration.

Question 2

Topics: Momentum, Impulse, Kinetic Energy

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q2

Fig. 2.1 shows a tennis ball approaching a tennis racket.

The tennis ball hits the racket at a speed of 52 m/s. The average force on the ball during the time that it is in contact with the racket is 350 N. The speed of the ball after it leaves the racket is 26 m/s in the opposite direction to the initial speed of the ball. The mass of the ball is 58 g.

(a) (i) Calculate the change in momentum of the ball while it is in contact with the racket. [3]

Note: Mass = 58 g = 0.058 kg. Since momentum is a vector, we must assign directions. Let the initial direction (52 m/s) be positive.

Initial momentum (p_initial) = mass × initial velocity = 0.058 kg × (52 m/s)

Final momentum (p_final) = mass × final velocity = 0.058 kg × (-26 m/s) (negative as it's the opposite direction)

Change in momentum (Δp) = p_final - p_initial

Δp = (0.058 × -26) - (0.058 × 52)

Δp = -1.508 - 3.016

Δp = -4.524 kg m/s

Answer (Magnitude): 4.5 kg m/s (or 4.52)

Recap

Momentum is a vector quantity linked to mass and velocity by the equation:

momentum (kg m/s) = mass (kg) × velocity (m/s)

p = m × v

When calculating *change* in momentum, you must account for the direction. Since the ball reverses direction, the change is the *sum* of the magnitudes of the initial and final momentum.

(ii) State an equation which defines impulse in terms of force and time. [1]

Impulse = force × time

Impulse = F × t

Recap

Impulse is equal to the change in momentum (Δp). Therefore, we have the important relationship:

F × t = mv - mu

where mv is the final momentum and mu is the initial momentum.

(iii) Calculate the time that the racket is in contact with the ball. [2]

Calculation:

From (a)(i) and (a)(ii), we know: Change in momentum = Force × time

4.524 kg m/s = 350 N × time

time = 4.524 ÷ 350

time = 0.0129... s

Answer: 0.013 s

Recap

This equation shows that for a given change in momentum (like stopping a ball), if the time of impact is increased, the force exerted is decreased. This is the principle behind car crumple zones and soft mats in gymnastics.

(b) Calculate the difference between the values of the kinetic energy of the ball before and after the impact with the racket. [3]

Calculation:

Kinetic Energy (KE) = ½ × mass × velocity2

KE_before = ½ × (0.058 kg) × (52 m/s)2 = 78.416 J

KE_after = ½ × (0.058 kg) × (26 m/s)2 = 19.604 J

Difference = KE_before - KE_after

Difference = 78.416 J - 19.604 J = 58.812 J

Answer: 59 J

Recap

Unlike momentum, energy is a scalar quantity, so direction does not matter. The kinetic energy equation is:

Kinetic energy (J) = 1/2 × mass (kg) × velocity2 (m/s)2

E_k = ½mv2

The collision is inelastic because kinetic energy was lost (transferred to heat and sound).

Question 3

Topics: Energy Resources, Energy Transfers

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q3

Fig. 3.1 shows the cross-section of a barrage built across a tidal bay. The barrage is part of a tidal power station.

The gates are raised to be open when the tide comes in. The gates are lowered to close when it is high tide. Fig. 3.1 shows the water levels in the open sea and the tidal bay when it is low tide. The gates are raised and water flows through the turbine.

(a) Complete the sentences to describe the energy transfers which take place when the gates are opened. [3]

Use words from the list.

tidal bay, open sea, rotating, electrical power, kinetic, gates, gravitational potential, turbines, water


... energy of the ... in the ... is transferred to ... energy in the ... This energy is used in the generator to produce ...

Gravitational potential energy of the water in the (tidal) bay is transferred to kinetic energy in the turbines. This energy is used in the generator to produce electrical power.

Recap

This is an example of the principle of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another.

  • Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE): Energy stored by an object due to its height in a gravitational field (E_p = mgh).
  • Kinetic Energy (KE): Energy an object has due to its movement (E_k = ½mv2).

Worked Example (from textbook):

A ball of mass 0.5 kg is dropped from rest at a height of 5 m. Find its velocity when it hits the ground. (Assuming no friction):

Ep lost = Ek gained

mgh = ½mv2 (mass cancels out)

v2 = 2gh

v = √(2 × 10 × 5) = √100 = 10 m/s

(b) State one advantage and one disadvantage of tidal power as an energy resource. [2]

Advantage: It is a renewable resource / it is reliable and predictable / it does not produce (harmful) pollution or greenhouse gases.

Disadvantage: The (high) cost of construction is very large / it can have negative effects on (marine) life and estuary habitats / it is not available all day (only when the tide flows) / it is only suitable for a limited number of sites.

Recap

Energy resources can be classified as renewable (will not run out, e.g., solar, wind, tidal) or non-renewable (will eventually run out, e.g., fossil fuels, nuclear).

When considering energy resources, we must balance their advantages (e.g., cost, reliability, environmental impact) with their disadvantages.

(c) State the main source of energy for tidal energy. [1]

The Moon (and the Sun).

Recap

The tides are caused by the gravitational attraction (or pull) of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, the Sun, on the Earth's oceans. This makes tidal energy one of the few resources (along with geothermal and nuclear) that does not originate from the Sun.

Question 4

Topics: Thermal Physics, Thermometers

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q4

(a) Fig. 4.1 shows a liquid-in-glass thermometer labelled thermometer X.

(i) State the physical property which varies with temperature in a liquid-in-glass thermometer. [1]

The volume (of the liquid).

Recap

Liquid-in-glass thermometers work because of thermal expansion. When the liquid (like alcohol or mercury) in the bulb is heated, it expands and is forced up the narrow tube (capillary). The amount it expands is proportional to the temperature change.

(ii) Thermometer Y has a bulb that contains twice the volume of liquid compared to thermometer X.

State and explain how the sensitivity of thermometer Y compares with the sensitivity of thermometer X. [2]

Statement: Thermometer Y has more (or greater) sensitivity.

Explanation: With twice the volume of liquid, a 1°C temperature change will cause twice as much expansion. This larger change in volume will move the liquid further up the narrow tube, making the change more noticeable.

Recap

Sensitivity of a thermometer refers to how much the reading changes for a 1°C (or 1 K) change in temperature. A more sensitive thermometer shows a larger change in length for the same temperature rise. This is often achieved with a larger bulb or a narrower tube (capillary).

(iii) State and explain one change that can be made to the design of thermometer X to increase its range. [2]

Statement: Make the (capillary) tube longer.

Explanation: A longer tube provides more space for the liquid to expand into before it reaches the end, allowing it to measure higher temperatures.

(Alternative: Make the capillary tube wider. Explanation: A wider tube means the liquid level rises less for each °C, so it takes a much higher temperature to reach the end of the tube.)

Recap

The range of a thermometer is the difference between the maximum and minimum temperatures it can measure. Increasing the range is often the opposite of increasing the sensitivity. To increase the range, you could use a smaller bulb (less expansion per °C) or a wider tube (less movement per °C).

(b) A liquid-in-glass thermometer cannot measure a temperature of 1300 °C.

State a physical property which varies with temperature in a thermometer which can measure a temperature of 1300 °C. [1]

The e.m.f. (electromotive force) or voltage produced by a thermocouple.

Recap

While liquid-in-glass thermometers rely on thermal expansion, other thermometers use different properties. A thermocouple, which is used for very high temperatures, generates a voltage (e.m.f.) that depends on the temperature difference between its two junctions.

Question 5

Topics: Thermal Physics, Evaporation, Change of State

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q5

(a) Three identical dishes, A, B and C, contain an equal volume of water.

Dish A is outside in sunlight and experiences no wind during the day. Dish B is outside in sunlight and experiences a strong wind during the day. Dish C is in a dark room.

Water evaporates from each dish. After 12 hours, a student measures the volume of water in each dish. Dish C contains the largest volume of water and dish B contains the smallest volume of water.

Explain, in terms of particles, why the three dishes have different volumes of water. [4]

Evaporation is when more energetic (faster-moving) molecules have enough energy to escape from the surface of the liquid.

  • Dish B (smallest volume left): Has the fastest rate of evaporation. The sunlight (energy from the Sun) gives particles more kinetic energy, and the strong wind removes the escaped water vapour molecules from above the surface, allowing more to escape.
  • Dish A (medium volume left): The sunlight increases the rate of evaporation by giving particles energy, but with no wind, the air above the water becomes humid, slowing the rate compared to Dish B.
  • Dish C (largest volume left): Has the slowest rate of evaporation. It is in the dark (lower temperature, less energy) and has no wind.

Therefore, Dish B loses the most water, and Dish C loses the least.

Recap

The rate of evaporation can be increased by:

  1. Increasing the temperature (gives particles more kinetic energy).
  2. Increasing the surface area (more particles are at the surface, able to escape).
  3. Increasing the air flow (or wind) over the surface (removes escaped particles).

(b) Define specific latent heat of vaporisation. [2]

Specific latent heat of vaporisation is the energy required to change 1 kg (or unit mass) of a substance from liquid to gas (or vice versa) without any change in temperature.

Recap

During a change of state (like boiling), the energy being added is used to break the bonds holding the particles together, not to increase their kinetic energy. This is why the temperature remains constant during boiling and melting.

(Note: The provided textbook did not explicitly define "specific latent heat", but this recap is based on its explanation of changes of state.)

(c) Fig. 5.1 shows an insulating beaker, crushed ice, an immersion heater and a thermometer.

The initial temperature of the ice is -6.0 °C.

The immersion heater is switched on and the temperature is recorded at equal intervals of time.

Fig. 5.2 shows the temperature-time graph.

Describe what occurs in each of the sections A, B, C and D. [3]

  • Section A: The temperature of the solid ice is increasing (from -6.0 °C to 0 °C).
  • Section B: The solid ice is melting to form liquid water. This happens at a constant temperature (0 °C).
  • Section C: The temperature of the liquid water is increasing (from 0 °C to 100 °C).

This graph shows the two key moments of change of state:

  1. Melting (B): When energy is transferred, the temperature increases *until* the melting point is reached. Then, the temperature remains constant as the substance melts.

Question 7

Topics: Light, Refraction, Total Internal Reflection

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q7

(a) State what is meant by total internal reflection. [2]

Total internal reflection is when all light is reflected back into the first medium (and no light is refracted out). It occurs when light travels in a more dense medium towards a boundary with a less dense medium, and the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

Recap

When light hits a boundary, it can be reflected, refracted, or both. For total internal reflection (TIR) to occur, two conditions must be met:

  1. Light must be travelling from a more optically dense to a less optically dense medium.
  2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle (c).

(b) Fig. 7.1 shows a ray of light from a light source in a tank containing a liquid.

The ray of light strikes the surface of the liquid at an angle x.

(i) The refractive index of the liquid is 1.5.

Calculate the largest value of x for which total internal reflection can occur. [3]

Note: The diagram is misleading. Angle 'x' is shown as the angle of incidence (to the normal). The question asks for the *largest* value of x for which TIR can occur. TIR occurs when x > c. Therefore, the largest value of x would be 90°. The question is likely flawed and means "What is the critical angle?" or is asking for the angle complementary to the critical angle (as 'x' in the diagram is labelled ambiguously). Following the Mark Scheme's logic (which assumes 'x' is the angle to the *surface*), the calculation is:

1. Find the critical angle (c):

n = 1 ÷ sin(c)

sin(c) = 1 ÷ n = 1 ÷ 1.5 = 0.666...

c = sin-1(0.666...) = 41.8°

2. The angle 'x' (as interpreted by the mark scheme) is the angle to the surface, which is complementary to the angle of incidence (c).

x = 90° - c

x = 90° - 41.8° = 48.2° (or 48° if c=42°)

Recap

The critical angle (c) is the specific angle of incidence (in the denser medium) for which the angle of refraction (in the less dense medium) is 90°.

The relationship between refractive index (n) and the critical angle (c) is:

n = 1 ÷ sin(c)

(ii) The speed of light in air is 3.0 × 108 m/s.

Calculate the speed of light in the liquid. [2]

Calculation:

Refractive index (n) = (speed of light in vacuum/air) ÷ (speed of light in medium)

1.5 = (3.0 × 108 m/s) ÷ v

v = (3.0 × 108 m/s) ÷ 1.5

v = 2.0 × 108 m/s

Recap

The refractive index (n) of a medium is a measure of how much it slows down light. It is defined by the ratio:

n = speed of light in a vacuum ÷ speed of light in the medium

Because light always slows down in a medium, the refractive index is always greater than 1.

Fig. 9.1 shows a circuit with an alternating current (a.c.) supply, a resistor and a diode.

The frequency of the power supply is 50 Hz.

(a) Calculate the time period (time for one complete cycle) of the a.c. supply. [2]

Calculation:

Time Period (T) = 1 ÷ frequency (f)

Frequency (Hz) is the number of complete waves (or cycles) that pass a point per second.

Time Period (s) is the time it takes for one complete wave (or cycle) to pass.

Question 10

Topics: Nuclear Physics, Radioactivity, Isotopes

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q10

(a) A cloud chamber can be used to detect α (alpha)-particles and β (beta)-particles. Alcohol in the cloud chamber exists as a vapour and condenses on ions produced in the air. This forms visible tracks.

Fig. 10.1 shows the tracks when a source of α-particles and β-particles is present in the cloud chamber.

Some of the tracks are short and thick. Other tracks are longer and thinner.

State and explain which tracks are produced by α-particles and which tracks are produced by β-particles. [3]

α-particles: Produce the short and thick tracks. This is because alpha particles are highly ionising, meaning they create many ions in a short distance, which quickly uses up their energy and gives them a short range.

β-particles: Produce the longer and thinner (and more scattered) tracks. This is because beta particles are less ionising than alpha particles, so they create fewer ions along their path, lose their energy more slowly, and have a longer range.

Recap

Properties of nuclear radiation:

  • Alpha (α): A helium nucleus (2 protons, 2 neutrons). Highly ionising. Short range (stopped by paper).
  • Beta (β): A fast-moving electron. Medium ionising. Longer range (stopped by thin aluminium).
  • Gamma (γ): An electromagnetic wave. Weakly ionising. Very long range (reduced by thick lead).

(b) A radioactive isotope of sodium (Na) is used to detect leaks from water pipes. A nucleus of this isotope of sodium contains 11 protons and 13 neutrons. This nucleus decays by emitting a β-particle to form a nucleus of magnesium (Mg).

(i) Describe what is meant by an isotope. [2]

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons in their nucleus.

Recap

For example, Carbon-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons) and Carbon-14 (6 protons, 8 neutrons) are isotopes. They have the same chemical properties (because they have the same number of electrons) but different physical properties (like mass and nuclear stability).

(ii) Write down the nuclide equation for the decay of this isotope of sodium to magnesium. [3]

Sodium nucleus: 11 protons, 13 neutrons. Nucleon number = 11 + 13 = 24. Proton number = 11.

Beta decay: A neutron becomes a proton, so proton number increases by 1. Nucleon number is unchanged.

Equation:

2411Na → 2412Mg + 0-1β

Recap

In beta decay, a neutron in the nucleus decays into a proton and a fast-moving electron (the beta particle). This causes the proton number to increase by 1, while the total nucleon number (protons + neutrons) remains the same.

(iii) This isotope of sodium has a half-life of 15 hours. The isotope of magnesium is stable and does not undergo radioactive decay.

Suggest why these properties of the isotope of sodium and the isotope of magnesium make this isotope of sodium suitable to detect leaks from water pipes. [2]

  • The half-life (15 hours) is long enough to allow the test to be carried out (i.e., for the isotope to be added, circulate, and be detected).
  • It is also short enough that the radioactivity will decay to a safe level relatively quickly, so the water is not hazardous for long.
  • The fact that it decays to a stable isotope (magnesium) is good because it means no further long-term radioactivity is produced.

Recap

When choosing a radioactive tracer for medical or industrial use, the half-life is a critical factor. It must be long enough for the procedure to be completed, but short enough that the substance does not remain dangerously radioactive for a long time.

Question 11

Topics: Electromagnetism, Transformers

Full Paper Code: 0625/43/O/N/22 Q11

(a) Fig. 11.1 shows a solenoid connected to a battery.

On Fig. 11.1, draw the pattern of the magnetic field inside and around the solenoid. Indicate the direction of the magnetic field with an arrow. [3]

The drawing should show:

  • Inside the solenoid: A set of straight, parallel, vertical magnetic field lines, close together (indicating a strong, uniform field).
  • Outside the solenoid: Field lines looping around from one end of the solenoid to the other (curving from top to bottom, or vice versa).
  • Direction: Using the right-hand grip rule (current flows up on the right, down on the left), the North pole is at the top. Arrows should point upwards inside the solenoid and downwards outside (looping from North to South).

Recap

A solenoid (a long coil of wire) creates a magnetic field very similar to a bar magnet. The field inside the solenoid is strong and uniform (the lines are straight and evenly spaced). The direction of the field can be found using the right-hand grip rule: if you curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the current, your thumb points to the North pole.

(b) Electrical power is transmitted at a voltage of 400 kV. A transformer reduces the voltage to 33 kV for use by heavy industry in large factories. The number of turns on the primary coil of the transformer is 11 000.

Calculate the number of turns on the secondary coil of the transformer. [2]

Calculation:

Use the transformer equation: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns

Vp (primary voltage) = 400 kV

Vs (secondary voltage) = 33 kV

Np (primary turns) = 11 000

Ns (secondary turns) = ?

400 ÷ 33 = 11 000 ÷ Ns

Ns = (11 000 × 33) ÷ 400

Ns = 363000 ÷ 400 = 907.5

Answer: 908 or 910 (as turns must be a whole number, 908 is most accurate).

Recap

For an ideal transformer, the ratio of the voltages across the coils is equal to the ratio of the number of turns on the coils. The equation is:

Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns

This is a step-down transformer because the voltage has been reduced (from 400 kV to 33 kV), so the secondary coil must have fewer turns than the primary coil.
