0625/42/M/J/21 Q1

Topic(s): Density, Buoyancy, Dynamics, Motion Graphs

(a) Fig. 1.1 shows a sealed weather balloon which is stationary in still air.

State whether the overall density of the balloon and its instruments is greater than, less than, or the same as the density of the surrounding air.

The overall density is the same as the density of the surrounding air.

Recap

For an object to be stationary (neutrally buoyant) in a fluid, its average density must be equal to the density of the fluid. This means the upward force (upthrust) from the displaced air is exactly equal to the total downward weight of the balloon and its instruments.

(b) At night, the gas inside the balloon cools. The pressure of the air outside the balloon remains the same.

(i) State whether the balloon rises, falls or remains stationary.

The balloon falls.

(ii) Explain your answer.

When the gas inside cools, its molecules lose kinetic energy and move slower, causing the gas to contract and become denser. This increases the overall density of the balloon system. The upthrust (which depends on the volume of displaced air) remains the same, but the balloon's total weight is now greater than the upthrust. This creates a net downward force, causing the balloon to accelerate downwards.

Recap

This combines the Kinetic Theory of Gases with Archimedes' Principle. Cooling a gas reduces the motion of its particles, increasing its density. An object in a fluid sinks if its average density is greater than the fluid's density, because its weight is greater than the buoyant force (upthrust).

(c) An object is released from the balloon. It starts at rest and eventually reaches a constant speed.

(i) On the axes of Fig. 1.2, sketch a speed-time graph to show this motion.

The graph should be a curve that:

  • Starts at the origin (0,0).
  • Has a steep, positive gradient initially.
  • The gradient gradually decreases over time.
  • The line becomes horizontal, indicating a constant speed (terminal velocity).

Recap

The gradient of a speed-time graph represents acceleration. The object initially accelerates due to gravity. As speed increases, air resistance increases, opposing the motion. This reduces the net downward force, so the acceleration decreases (the graph gets less steep). Eventually, air resistance equals the object's weight, the net force is zero, and acceleration becomes zero, so the speed is constant.

(ii) State the values of the initial acceleration and the final acceleration of the object.

Initial acceleration = 10 m/s² (the acceleration of free fall, g).

Final acceleration = 0 m/s².

Recap

Initially, the only significant force is weight, so the object's acceleration is 'g'. At terminal velocity, the forces are balanced (weight = air resistance), so the resultant force is zero, and according to Newton's Second Law (F=ma), the acceleration must be zero.

Topic(s): Moments, Equilibrium

(a) Define the moment of a force.

The moment of a force is the force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

Recap

A moment is a turning effect. The equation is:
Moment = Force × Perpendicular Distance.
The unit of a moment is the Newton-metre (Nm).

(b) Fig. 2.1 shows an object of negligible weight. The object is in equilibrium.

The object is free to rotate about its pivot P. Calculate the value of force F.

1. Apply the Principle of Moments: for an object in equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about a pivot equals the sum of anticlockwise moments.

2. Calculate the clockwise moment caused by the 50 kg mass.
Weight = mass × g = 50 kg × 10 N/kg = 500 N.
Distance = 20 cm = 0.20 m.
Clockwise Moment = 500 N × 0.20 m = 100 Nm.

3. The force F produces an anticlockwise moment.
Distance = 12 cm = 0.12 m.
Anticlockwise Moment = F × 0.12 m.

4. Equate the moments:
F × 0.12 = 100
F = 100 / 0.12 = 833 N.

Recap

The Principle of Moments is a key concept for objects in rotational equilibrium. Always ensure all distances are converted to metres and masses are converted to weights (in Newtons) before calculating moments.

(c) Describe an experiment involving vertical forces to show that there is no net moment on an object in equilibrium. You may draw a diagram in the space provided.

Apparatus: Metre rule, pivot, and a set of known masses (e.g., slotted masses with hangers).

Method:

  1. Balance the metre rule on the pivot at its centre of mass (usually the 50 cm mark).
  2. Hang a known mass (m1) at a known distance (d1) on one side of the pivot. This creates a moment (e.g., clockwise).
  3. Hang a different known mass (m2) on the other side and adjust its distance (d2) until the rule is perfectly balanced (horizontal) again.
  4. Record the values of m1, d1, m2, and d2.

Analysis:

  1. Calculate the clockwise moment: Momentcw = (m1 × g) × d1.
  2. Calculate the anticlockwise moment: Momentacw = (m2 × g) × d2.
  3. Show that, within the limits of experimental accuracy, the clockwise moment equals the anticlockwise moment, demonstrating that the net moment is zero for equilibrium.

0625/42/M/J/21 Q3

Topic(s): Energy, Work, Power, Momentum

Fig. 3.1 shows water flowing at very slow speed over a cliff edge.

The water falls 15m onto the rocks below.

(a) Show that the velocity of the water when it strikes the rocks is 17 m/s.

We can solve this using the conservation of energy. The gravitational potential energy (GPE) at the top is converted into kinetic energy (KE) at the bottom.

GPE = KE

mgh = ½mv²

The mass (m) cancels out from both sides:

gh = ½v²

v² = 2gh

v² = 2 × 10 m/s² × 15 m = 300 m²/s²

v = √300 = 17.32 m/s

Therefore, the velocity is approximately 17 m/s.

Recap

The principle of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one form to another. Here, GPE (mgh) is converted to KE (½mv²). Alternatively, the equation of motion v² = u² + 2as can be used, with u=0, a=g=10, and s=15.

(b) 30 kg of water flows over the cliff edge every second. Calculate the force exerted by the rocks on the falling water. Ignore any splashing.

The force exerted by the rocks causes the water's momentum to change from its impact velocity to zero.

Force is the rate of change of momentum.

F = (mv - mu) / t or F = Δp / t

Here, the mass per unit time (m/t) is 30 kg/s. The change in velocity (Δv) is 0 m/s - 17.32 m/s = -17.32 m/s.

The negative sign indicates the force is in the opposite direction to the water's initial motion (i.e., upwards). The force exerted by the rocks on the water is 520 N.

Recap

Newton's Second Law can be expressed as Force being equal to the rate of change of momentum. When a continuous stream of particles is stopped by a surface, the force is calculated as the mass flow rate multiplied by the change in velocity.

0625/42/M/J/21 Q4

Topic(s): Kinetic Particle Model, Evaporation

(a) Pollen particles are mixed into a liquid. They are seen to move when observed through a microscope.

(i) Describe this movement.

The movement is random, haphazard, and jerky.

Recap

This type of movement is known as Brownian motion. It provides evidence for the kinetic particle theory of matter.

(ii) Explain this movement in terms of the molecules of the liquid and the pollen particles.

The small, invisible molecules of the liquid are in continuous and random motion. They collide with the much larger pollen particles from all sides. At any instant, the number of collisions on one side of a pollen particle may be different from the number on the other side. This creates an unbalanced force (or resultant force) that pushes the pollen particle, causing it to move suddenly. This process repeats, leading to the observed random motion.

Recap

Brownian motion is the key evidence that particles in liquids and gases are in constant, random motion. The observed motion is not because the pollen is alive, but because it is being bombarded by the unseen liquid molecules.

(b) (i) Medical professionals sometimes rub ethanol over the skin of a patient. Ethanol evaporates readily at room temperature and has a high specific latent heat of vaporisation. State whether the patient experiences heating, cooling or neither at the site where the ethanol is applied. Explain your answer.

Statement: The patient experiences cooling.

Explanation: Evaporation requires energy, known as the latent heat of vaporisation. The ethanol absorbs this thermal energy from the patient's skin. As the skin loses heat, its temperature drops, resulting in a cooling sensation. The high specific latent heat of vaporisation means that a large amount of heat is absorbed from the skin for each gram of ethanol that evaporates, making it very effective.

Recap

Evaporation is a cooling process. The most energetic particles escape from the liquid's surface, lowering the average kinetic energy (and thus temperature) of the remaining liquid. The energy required for this phase change is taken from the immediate surroundings.

(ii) State any effect on the rate of evaporation of ethanol when a fan blows air over the patient's skin.

The rate of evaporation increases.

Recap

Blowing air over a surface removes the layer of saturated vapour just above it. This maintains a steep concentration gradient between the liquid surface and the air, allowing more particles to escape the liquid phase more quickly. Other factors that increase the rate of evaporation are increased temperature, increased surface area, and lower humidity.

0625/42/M/J/21 Q5

Topic(s): Heat Transfer, Conduction, Convection, Evaporation, Radiation

(a) A machine delivers a hot drink in a plastic cup, which is uncomfortably hot to hold. Fig. 5.1 shows the cup with the hot drink. Fig. 5.2a shows the cup with a holder, and Fig. 5.2b shows a cross-section of the holder, which has air gaps.

Explain how using the holder makes it more comfortable to hold the cup.

The holder contains trapped pockets of air in its wavy structure. Air is a gas and is a very poor conductor of thermal energy (a good insulator). The holder reduces the rate of heat transfer by conduction from the hot cup to the hand, making it comfortable to hold.

Recap

Heat transfer by conduction involves the transfer of kinetic energy between adjacent particles. It is most effective in solids, especially metals. Gases are poor conductors because their particles are far apart, making collisions less frequent. Trapping air is a common method of insulation (e.g., double-glazed windows, winter clothing).

(b) A student finds that the holder only reduces the rate of cooling slightly. Suggest and explain another action that reduces the rate of cooling more effectively.

Suggestion: Place a lid on the cup.

Explanation: A large amount of thermal energy is lost from the open surface of the drink through evaporation and convection. A lid traps the hot water vapour above the liquid, reducing the rate of evaporation (which is a cooling process). It also prevents the movement of air (convection currents) from carrying heat away from the surface. This is generally more effective than just insulating the sides.

Recap

To keep a hot drink warm, you must reduce all forms of heat transfer:

  • Conduction: Use an insulating cup/holder.
  • Convection: Use a lid to stop air movement.
  • Evaporation: Use a lid to trap vapour.
  • Radiation: Use a cup with a light-coloured, shiny surface.

For an open cup, convection and evaporation are the most significant modes of heat loss.

(c) State the method of thermal energy transfer from a star through the vacuum of space.

Thermal radiation (or electromagnetic radiation/waves, e.g., infra-red).

Recap

Thermal radiation is the only method of heat transfer that does not require a medium (particles). It can travel through a vacuum. This is how the Sun's energy reaches Earth.

Topic(s): Light, Refraction, Dispersion, Waves

(a) Fig. 6.1 shows a ray of green light passing through a prism. A ray of blue light is directed towards the prism on the same path as the ray of green light.

On Fig. 6.1, draw the path of the blue light through and out of the prism.

The path drawn for the blue light should show it bending more than the green light at both surfaces of the prism.

  1. At the first surface, the blue ray should be shown refracting (bending) downwards, at a slightly steeper angle than the green ray.
  2. At the second surface, the blue ray should be shown refracting again (bending downwards), emerging at a greater angle of deviation from its original path compared to the green ray. The blue ray should be below the green ray after exiting the prism.

Recap

Dispersion is the splitting of white light into its constituent colours. This happens because the refractive index of a medium (like glass) is slightly different for each colour of light. The refractive index is highest for violet/blue light and lowest for red light. A higher refractive index means the light travels slower and bends more. Therefore, blue light is deviated more than green light.

(b) The wavelength of the blue light in air is 4.8 × 10–7 m. Calculate the frequency of the blue light.

1. Use the wave speed equation: speed = frequency × wavelength (v = fλ).

2. The speed of light in air (v) is 3.0 × 108 m/s.

3. Rearrange for frequency: f = v / λ

f = (3.0 × 108 m/s) / (4.8 × 10–7 m)

f = 6.25 × 1014 Hz

Recap

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum (and approximately in air), which is the speed of light, c ≈ 3.0 × 108 m/s. The relationship between speed, frequency, and wavelength is fundamental for all waves.

Topic(s): Magnetism, Demagnetisation

(a) Fig. 7.1 shows two magnets and the gap between the N pole of one magnet and the S pole of the other magnet.

On Fig. 7.1, draw three lines to show the pattern and direction of the magnetic field in the gap.

Three lines should be drawn from the North pole to the South pole.

  • The line in the centre should be straight.
  • The lines above and below should curve outwards slightly.
  • All three lines must have an arrow pointing from the N pole towards the S pole.

Recap

Magnetic field lines show the direction of the force that a North pole would experience at that point. They always point away from North poles and towards South poles. The density of the lines indicates the strength of the field.

(b) (i) Fig. 7.2 is a repeat of Fig. 7.1 showing the two magnets. On Fig. 7.2, draw the position of a plotting compass needle when it comes to rest in the gap between the N pole and the S pole.

A small arrow representing the compass needle should be drawn in the gap. The head of the arrow (the N pole of the compass) must point towards the S pole of the magnet (to the right).

(ii) Explain why the needle comes to rest in this position.

The poles of the compass needle experience forces from the magnetic field. The North pole of the compass needle is attracted to the South pole of the permanent magnet, and the South pole of the needle is attracted to the North pole of the permanent magnet. These forces create a turning effect (moment) that aligns the needle with the magnetic field lines.

Recap

The fundamental rule of magnetism is that opposite poles attract and like poles repel. A plotting compass is simply a small, free-moving magnet that can be used to trace the direction of magnetic field lines.

(c) Describe a method of demagnetising a bar magnet.

AC Coil Method: Place the bar magnet inside a solenoid (a coil of wire) connected to an alternating current (a.c.) power supply. Switch on the a.c. supply and then slowly withdraw the magnet from the coil to a large distance away before switching off the current. The rapidly changing and decreasing magnetic field randomises the magnetic domains within the bar magnet, leaving it demagnetised.

Other methods: Heating the magnet until it is red hot, or placing it in an east-west direction and hammering it repeatedly.

Recap

Demagnetisation is the process of scrambling the aligned magnetic domains in a material. This can be achieved using a decaying alternating magnetic field, thermal energy (heating), or mechanical shock (hammering).

Topic(s): Atomic Physics, Radioactivity, DC Motor

(a) Two identical radioactive sources emit α-particles and γ-rays into two vacuum tubes.

(i) Fig. 8.1 shows two electrically charged plates on either side of one of the vacuum tubes. Write the symbol α once in Table 8.1 to indicate any deflection of the α-particles. Write the symbol γ once in Table 8.1 to indicate any deflection of the γ-rays.

α-particle: Deflected towards the bottom of the page (towards the negative plate).

γ-ray: No deflection.

Recap

Alpha (α) particles are helium nuclei, so they have a positive charge (+2e). They are attracted to the negative plate and repelled by the positive plate. Gamma (γ) rays are high-energy electromagnetic waves and have no charge, so they are unaffected by electric fields.

(ii) Fig. 8.2 shows the poles of a very strong magnet on either side of the other vacuum tube. Write the symbol α once in Table 8.2 to indicate any deflection of the α-particles. Write the symbol γ once in Table 8.2 to indicate any deflection of the γ-rays.

α-particle: Deflected out of the page.

γ-ray: No deflection.

Recap

Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule for a positive charge: The Field (Forefinger) points down (N to S). The Current (seCond finger) points to the right. The resulting Thrust/Force (Thumb) is directed out of the page. Gamma rays have no charge and are unaffected by magnetic fields.

(b) Fig. 8.3 shows a simple direct current (d.c.) electric motor with a split-ring commutator.

(i) State and explain the direction of rotation of the coil as seen from point X.

Statement: The coil rotates anticlockwise.

Explanation: On the left side of the coil, the current flows into the page. Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, the magnetic field is from N to S (left to right), and the current is in. This results in a downward force. On the right side, the current flows out of the page, resulting in an upward force. This pair of forces creates an anticlockwise turning effect.

(ii) The coil rotates through 90° from the position shown. State what happens to the moment in this position.

The moment becomes zero. In this vertical position, the forces on the sides of the coil are directed outwards from the pivot and do not provide a turning effect.

(iii) The coil is rotated through 180° from the position shown. By considering the forces on the coil, explain how the split-ring commutator enables the motor to turn continuously.

The split-ring commutator reverses the direction of the current in the coil every half turn (180°). After 180°, the side of the coil that was on the left is now on the right. The commutator ensures that the current in this side is now flowing outwards, so it still experiences an upward force. Similarly, the side now on the left has an inward current and a downward force. This ensures the turning moment is always in the same direction (anticlockwise), allowing for continuous rotation.

Recap

The function of the split-ring commutator is crucial for a DC motor. It acts as a mechanical switch, reversing the current in the armature every time the coil passes the vertical position. This maintains a continuous torque in one direction, converting electrical energy into rotational kinetic energy.

Topic(s): Electricity, Circuits, Current

(a) Fig. 9.1 shows a circuit.

On Fig. 9.1, draw two clearly labelled arrows to show the direction of the electron flow and the direction of the conventional current in the circuit.

Conventional Current: An arrow should be drawn pointing away from the positive (long line) terminal and towards the negative (short line) terminal. This arrow should be labelled "conventional current" and will point clockwise.

Electron Flow: An arrow should be drawn pointing away from the negative terminal and towards the positive terminal. This arrow should be labelled "electron flow" and will point anticlockwise.

Recap

Conventional current is defined as the direction that positive charge would flow. It moves from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. Electron flow is the actual direction that electrons (which are negatively charged) move. It is in the opposite direction, from the negative terminal to the positive terminal.

(b) The current in the motor is 13 A. The charge on an electron is 1.6 × 10–19 C. Calculate the number of electrons that pass through the motor every second.

1. Current is the rate of flow of charge: I = Q / t.

2. Calculate the total charge (Q) that passes in one second (t=1s):
Q = I × t = 13 A × 1 s = 13 C.

3. The total charge is the number of electrons (n) multiplied by the charge on one electron (e): Q = n × e.

4. Rearrange to find the number of electrons:
n = Q / e
n = 13 C / (1.6 × 10–19 C)
n = 8.125 × 1019 electrons.

Recap

The unit of current, the Ampere (A), is defined as one Coulomb of charge passing a point per second (1 A = 1 C/s). By finding the total charge that flows in a given time, you can calculate the number of charge carriers (electrons) by dividing by the elementary charge, e.

Topic(s): Electricity, I-V Graphs, Potential Dividers, Thermistors

(a) Fig. 10.1 shows the potential difference-current graph for a circuit component K.

Calculate the resistance of component K when the current in it is 4.0 mA.

1. From the graph, find the potential difference (V) when the current (I) is 4.0 mA.
Reading from the graph, V = 9.0 V.

2. Convert the current to Amperes: I = 4.0 mA = 0.004 A.

3. Use Ohm's Law: R = V / I.
R = 9.0 V / 0.004 A = 2250 Ω.

Recap

The resistance of a component at any point can be calculated from its V-I graph using R = V/I. It is important to read the values from the graph accurately and use the correct units (Volts and Amperes) in the calculation.

(b) Fig. 10.2 shows a circuit containing component K. At low temperature, component K has a much greater resistance than resistor R. At high temperature, component K has a much smaller resistance than resistor R.

State and explain the effect on the lamp when the temperature changes from very low to very high. Refer to the voltage at point X in your explanation.

Statement: The lamp is initially bright at a very low temperature and becomes dim (or goes off) at a very high temperature.

Explanation: The circuit is a potential divider. The lamp is in parallel with component K, so the voltage across the lamp is the same as the voltage at point X (VX).

  • At low temperature: The resistance of K (RK) is very high. It takes a large share of the supply voltage. Therefore, VX is high, and the lamp is bright.
  • At high temperature: The resistance of K (RK) becomes very low. It takes a much smaller share of the supply voltage. Therefore, VX is low, and the lamp becomes dim or turns off.

Recap

In a potential divider with two series resistors (R1 and R2), the voltage is shared according to the ratio of their resistances. The voltage across R1 is V1 = Vtotal × (R1 / (R1 + R2)). In this circuit, the lamp's brightness depends directly on the voltage across component K.

(c) State the name of component K.

Component K is a thermistor (specifically, an NTC type, as its resistance decreases with increasing temperature).

Recap

A thermistor is a resistor whose resistance is designed to change significantly with temperature. The non-linear V-I graph and the description of its resistance changing from much greater than R to much smaller than R as temperature increases are characteristic properties of a Negative Temperature Coefficient (NTC) thermistor.

Topic(s): Radioactivity, Half-life, Radiation Safety

(a) A student investigates a radioactive substance in a laboratory. Fig. 11.1 is a graph showing the count rate detected as the substance decays for 7.5 minutes.

The background radiation is 20 counts/min.

(i) Determine the half-life of the substance.

1. First, determine the initial count rate from the source alone by subtracting the background radiation from the initial total count rate.
Initial total count rate (at t=0) = 220 counts/min.
Initial source count rate = 220 - 20 = 200 counts/min.

2. After one half-life, the source count rate will be halved:
Source count rate after one half-life = 200 / 2 = 100 counts/min.

3. The detector measures the total count rate, which is the source plus background.
Detected count rate to find = 100 (source) + 20 (background) = 120 counts/min.

4. Find the time on the graph that corresponds to a detected count rate of 120 counts/min.
Reading from the graph, the time is 2.4 minutes. This is the half-life.

(ii) Calculate the count rate detected at time = 9.6 minutes.

1. Determine how many half-lives have passed.
Number of half-lives (n) = Total time / Half-life = 9.6 min / 2.4 min = 4 half-lives.

2. Calculate the source count rate after 4 half-lives.
Initial source count rate = 200 counts/min.
After 1 half-life: 100
After 2 half-lives: 50
After 3 half-lives: 25
After 4 half-lives: 12.5 counts/min.

3. Calculate the total detected count rate by adding the background radiation.
Detected count rate = 12.5 + 20 = 32.5 counts/min (which rounds to 33).

(b) The substance emits α-particles and γ-rays. The student suggests that it is safe to store the substance in a plastic container of thickness 2 mm. State and explain whether the student’s suggestion is correct.

Statement: The student's suggestion is incorrect.

Explanation: The 2 mm plastic container is sufficient to absorb and stop all the α-particles, as they are not very penetrating. However, the substance also emits γ-rays, which are highly penetrating. The thin plastic will have almost no effect on the gamma rays, which will pass through the container. Since gamma rays are highly ionising and dangerous to living cells, this storage method is unsafe. A thick container made of a dense material, such as lead, is required to safely absorb the gamma radiation.
