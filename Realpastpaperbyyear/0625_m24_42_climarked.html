Question Number: 1 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q1 | Topic(s): Kinematics, Energy

(a) Fig. 1.1 is a speed–time graph for the first 5 minutes of a bus journey.

Describe the motion between:

1. t = 0.90 min and t = 2.9 min

2. t = 2.9 min and t = 3.5 min

3. t = 3.5 min and t = 4.5 min

1. Between 0.90 min and 2.9 min the bus moves at a constant speed of 7.5 m / s.
2. Between 2.9 min and 3.5 min the bus undergoes uniform deceleration down to 2.5 m / s.
3. Between 3.5 min and 4.5 min the bus is stationary at 0 m / s.

Recap

The reference guide emphasises that on a speed–time graph a horizontal line signals uniform motion, a sloped line indicates acceleration or deceleration, and a line on the time axis corresponds to the object being at rest.

(b) Another bus travels at a speed of 8.9 m / s. The brakes apply a constant force and the bus stops in a distance of 23 m. This bus has a mass of 18 000 kg.

(i) Calculate the kinetic energy of the bus before the brakes are applied.

The kinetic energy is 7.1 × 105 J, obtained from EK = ½mv² = ½ × 18 000 kg × (8.9 m / s)².

Recap

Kinetic energy depends on the mass and the square of the speed, so halving the speed quarters the energy according to the formula provided in the textbook.

(ii) Calculate the force applied to stop the bus.

The braking force is 3.1 × 104 N (31 kN), found from F = W ÷ d = 7.1 × 105 J ÷ 23 m.

Recap

Work done by a constant force equals force × distance, so rearranging allows us to compute the force when the stopping distance and energy change are known.

Question Number: 2 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q2 | Topic(s): Dynamics

(a) Define impulse.

Impulse is the product of force and the time for which that force acts.

Recap

The textbook links impulse to momentum change, showing that Impulse = Δp; this is useful when analysing collisions or rocket thrust.

(b) Fig. 2.1 shows a rocket and its exhaust gases. The exhaust gases are emitted from the rocket with a velocity of 1400 m / s and at a rate of 2800 kg / s.

(i) Show that the force exerted on the rocket by the exhaust gases is 3900 kN. State the equation you use.

Using FΔt = Δp, the thrust is F = (Δp) / Δt = (2800 kg × 1400 m / s) / 1 s = 3.9 × 106 N, i.e. 3900 kN.

Recap

Rocket propulsion relies on the conservation of momentum; expelling mass backwards generates a thrust force forwards of equal magnitude.

(ii) Calculate the maximum mass that this force can lift from the ground. Ignore air resistance.

At lift-off the thrust equals the weight, so m = F ÷ g = 3.9 × 106 N ÷ 9.8 N / kg = 4.0 × 105 kg.

Recap

A rocket leaves the ground when thrust exceeds weight, so equating the two allows us to find the maximum lift-off mass for a given engine.

Question Number: 3 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q3 | Topic(s): Pressure, Gases

(a) A car has a weight of 13 000 N. The car is supported by 4 tyres. The area of each tyre in contact with the road is 0.016 m².

(i) Calculate the pressure on the road due to the weight of the car.

The pressure is 2.0 × 105 Pa, obtained from P = F / A = 13 000 N ÷ (4 × 0.016 m²).

Recap

Pressure equals force divided by area, so distributing the load over four contact patches reduces the value felt by the road.

(ii) Explain, in terms of particles, why the air pressure in the tyres increases when the car travels along the road.

As the tyres flex, friction heats the air so the particles move faster, collide more frequently and more forcefully with the tyre walls; for the same area this raises the pressure.

Recap

The kinetic theory description in the guide links temperature to particle motion: higher temperatures mean greater average kinetic energy, which leads directly to a higher pressure in a fixed volume.

(b) A gas cylinder contains helium gas at a pressure of 2.0 × 10⁶ Pa. A volume of 0.026 m³ of the compressed gas is released into balloons. Each balloon contains 0.015 m³ of helium at atmospheric pressure (1.0 × 10⁵ Pa). The temperature remains constant. Calculate the maximum number of balloons that can be filled.

The cylinder can fill 34 balloons because p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ gives a total expanded volume of 0.52 m³, which divided by 0.015 m³ per balloon equals 34.

Recap

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, the textbook states Boyle’s law: pV = constant, enabling scaling between high-pressure storage and low-pressure use.

Question Number: 4 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q4 | Topic(s): Thermal Physics

(a) Define specific heat capacity.

Specific heat capacity is the energy transferred per unit mass per unit temperature change.

Recap

The text highlights that c links thermal energy and temperature via ΔE = mcΔθ, so it measures how resistant a material is to heating.

(b) A volume of 0.0024 m³ of oil is heated in a pan for 7.0 min. The temperature of the oil increases from 20 °C to 180 °C. The density of the oil is 910 kg / m³. The specific heat capacity of the oil is 2000 J / (kg °C).

(i) Calculate the mass of oil in the pan.

The mass is 2.2 kg, found from m = ρV = 910 kg / m³ × 0.0024 m³.

Recap

The density relationship ρ = m / V is frequently used to convert provided volumes into masses before applying thermal formulas.

(ii) Calculate the energy required to increase the temperature of the oil.

The heating requires 7.0 × 105 J using ΔE = mcΔθ = 2.2 kg × 2000 J / (kg °C) × 160 °C.

Recap

Large specific heat capacity materials store more thermal energy for the same rise in temperature; oils typically have higher c than air, making them good heat-transfer media.

(iii) Calculate the power required to supply the energy calculated in (b)(ii).

The required power is 1.7 × 103 W, since P = E / t = 7.0 × 105 J ÷ (7.0 min × 60 s).

Recap

Power describes the rate of energy transfer; the guide stresses converting heating times into seconds before substitution.

(ii) State the approximate speed of radio waves in air.

Radio waves travel at 3.0 × 108 m / s in air.

Recap

All electromagnetic waves propagate through a vacuum (and air) at the speed of light, a key constant repeatedly highlighted in the guide.

(b) Fig. 5.1 shows successive crests of a wave after a plane wave has passed through a gap.

(i) On Fig. 5.1 draw three successive crests before the wave reaches the gap.

The missing crests are three parallel straight lines, equally spaced and parallel to the barrier.

Recap

The diffraction chapter stresses that straight plane waves approaching a slit remain parallel until they interact with the aperture.

(ii) Fig. 5.2 shows a much wider gap. A plane wave of the same wavelength is incident from the left. On Fig. 5.2, draw three successive crests of the wave after passing through the gap.

The emergent crests stay nearly straight with slight curvature at the edges, keeping the same wavelength as the incident wave.

Recap

According to the textbook, diffraction effects are small when the gap is much wider than the wavelength, so the transmitted wavefronts barely spread.

Question Number: 6 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q6 | Topic(s): Lenses

Fig. 6.1 shows a full-scale diagram of an object O and its image I produced by a converging lens. The lens and its position on the principal axis are not shown.

(a) On Fig. 6.1 draw:

• a single ray to locate the position of the centre of the converging lens

• a line to represent the position of the lens and label the line L.

The ray should pass through the centre of the lens without deviation and the line L must be drawn perpendicular to the principal axis at that intersection.

Recap

Principal rays for a converging lens include the undeviated central ray; sketching these accurately helps identify the optical centre.

(b) Determine the focal length of the lens by drawing another ray on Fig. 6.1.

The focal length is 2.1 cm, measured from the lens to the point where a ray parallel to the axis meets after refraction.

Recap

The focal length is defined as the distance from the optical centre to the principal focus, found using a ray initially parallel to the principal axis.

(c) The object is moved 2.0 cm closer to the lens. State two changes to the characteristics of the image.

The image becomes virtual and upright.

Recap

When an object is inside the focal length of a converging lens, the image cannot be projected and appears upright on the same side as the object.

Question Number: 7 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q7 | Topic(s): Electricity

(a) Draw the circuit symbol for a potential divider.

The symbol is two resistors in series with a tapping point; it is sufficient to draw a potential divider box with three terminals showing Vout across the lower resistor.

Recap

A potential divider splits the supply voltage in proportion to the resistances, a key idea used for sensors and audio controls.

(b) Fig. 7.1 shows a circuit.

(i) Calculate the value of Vout when the value of R is 3.0 kΩ.

Using the divider ratio Vout = Vin × Rout / (R + Rout) with R = 3.0 kΩ and Rout = 1.0 kΩ gives 1.5 V.

Recap

The textbook derives Vout / Vin = Rout / (R + Rout) for two resistors in series, so increasing the variable resistor reduces the output voltage.

(ii) The value of R is adjusted until the current in the circuit is 1.7 mA. Calculate the charge that flows through the circuit in 300 s.

The charge is 0.51 C because Q = It = 1.7 × 10-3 A × 300 s.

Recap

Charge equals current times time, a relationship used repeatedly when counting electron flow or sizing capacitors.

Question Number: 8 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q8 | Topic(s): Magnetism, Power Transmission

(a) Fig. 8.1 shows a wire carrying a large current. Fig. 8.2 shows the square card viewed from above.

(i) On Fig. 8.2 draw three magnetic field lines that indicate the direction of the magnetic field and how its strength varies with distance from the wire.

The field lines must be concentric circles centred on the wire, with spacing that increases with radius, and with arrows showing the clockwise direction.

Recap

Right-hand-rule reasoning from the reference text shows that current produces circular magnetic fields whose density reflects field strength.

(ii) The current in the wire increases and the direction of the current is reversed. State how these changes affect the magnetic field.

An increased current makes the field stronger, and reversing the current reverses the field’s direction.

Recap

Field strength around a wire is proportional to current, while the curl direction swaps when the current direction swaps.

(b) Electricity is transmitted at high voltage. Explain why a high voltage increases the efficiency of transmission even with thinner wires.

Power losses follow P = I²R, so using a high voltage allows a much lower current for the same delivered power, meaning even thin high-resistance wires dissipate less energy.

Recap

The National Grid discussion in the book stresses that minimising current is more effective than marginally changing resistance when trying to limit transmission losses.

Question Number: 9 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q9 | Topic(s): Radioactivity

(a) An experiment directs alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

(i) Most of the alpha particles pass through the thin foil in a straight line. State the conclusion about atoms from this observation.

The observation shows that most of an atom is empty space.

Recap

The Rutherford section emphasises that undeflected alpha particles imply minimal obstruction, revealing the atom’s largely empty structure.

(ii) Some of the alpha particles are deflected through angles less than 90° and a few are deflected through 180°. State and explain two conclusions about the nuclei of atoms from this observation.

Conclusion 1: The nucleus is very small; Explanation: only a few particles pass close enough to feel a strong deflection.
Conclusion 2: The nucleus is positively charged and very massive; Explanation: the repulsive force between positive alpha particles and the nucleus can reverse their direction, showing the nucleus carries most of the atom’s mass.

Recap

Scattering evidence described in the textbook established the nuclear model: tiny, massive, positively charged centres surrounded by mostly empty space.

(b) A source contains a radioactive isotope of strontium that decays by β-emission. The half-life is 29 years.

(i) State the change in the nucleus which occurs when a β-particle is emitted.

A neutron changes into a proton and an electron (the β-particle) which is emitted.

Recap

The nuclear decay chapter notes that β- emission increases the proton number by one while leaving the nucleon number unchanged.

(ii) The initial mass of this isotope is 25 µg. Calculate the mass of the isotope that decays in 87 years.

In 87 years (three half-lives) 7/8 of the sample decays, so the decayed mass is 22 µg.

Recap

Every half-life halves the remaining mass. Tracking fraction after each half-life is an efficient method for such calculations.

Question Number: 10 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q10 | Topic(s): Astronomy

(a) Fig. 10.1 represents different positions A–H of the Moon as it rotates around the Earth.

(i) State a position of the Moon where an observer on Earth sees:

1. there is a quarter Moon

2. there is a full Moon.

A quarter Moon is seen at position A or E; a full Moon occurs at position G.

Recap

Lunar phase diagrams in the book show that the phase depends on the angle between the Sun, Earth and Moon. Quadrature corresponds to quarter phases, while opposition gives a full Moon.

(ii) State the approximate time taken for the Moon to orbit the Earth.

The orbital period is about 1 month.

Recap

The Moon’s sidereal period is just over 27 days, so a calendar month is often used as an approximation.

(b) The average distance of the Earth from the Sun is 1.5 × 10⁸ km.

(i) Calculate the average orbital speed of the Earth in km / h.

The speed is 1.1 × 105 km / h, using v = 2πr / T with r = 1.5 × 10⁸ km and T = 365 × 24 h.

Recap

Circular orbital speed depends on radius and period; the textbook provides the same expression for planets and satellites.

(ii) The speed of light in a vacuum is 3.0 × 10⁸ m / s. Calculate the time taken for light from the Sun to reach the Earth.

Light takes about 500 s (8 min 20 s) to reach Earth, obtained from t = s / v = 1.5 × 10¹¹ m ÷ 3.0 × 10⁸ m / s.

Recap

Solar-system calculations often convert astronomical distances into metres before dividing by the constant speed of light.

Question Number: 11 | Paper Code: 0625/42/F/M/24 Q11 | Topic(s): Cosmology

(a) State the condition required for a protostar to become a stable star.

For stability, the inward gravitational force must be balanced by the outward pressure of fusion reactions.

Recap

The stellar evolution chapter calls this state hydrostatic equilibrium, where gravity and radiation pressure cancel.

(b) (i) Define the Hubble constant.

The Hubble constant is the ratio of a galaxy’s recessional speed to its distance from the observer.

Recap

Hubble’s law in the course text is written v = H0d, linking cosmic expansion speed with distance.

(ii) The current estimate for the Hubble constant is 2.2 × 10-18 s⁻¹. State the equation which gives an estimate for the age of the Universe.

The age can be approximated by age = 1 / H0.

Recap

This simple reciprocal assumes that the expansion rate has been roughly constant since the Big Bang.

(iii) Calculate an estimate for the age of the Universe.

Using the reciprocal gives an age of approximately 4.5 × 1017 s.

Recap

Converting seconds to years (≈ 1.4 × 1010 years) matches the order of magnitude quoted in the textbook for the age of the Universe.
