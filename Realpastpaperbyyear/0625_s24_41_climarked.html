Question 1 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q1 | Topics: Kinematics, Dynamics

A long tube contains oil. A small ball is held at rest at the surface of the oil. At time t = 0, the ball is released and begins to fall vertically through the oil.

As the ball begins to fall through the oil, it accelerates.

(a) Define acceleration.

Acceleration is the rate of change in velocity.

Recap

Velocity is a vector quantity describing speed in a specific direction. Acceleration occurs with any change in velocity—this could be a change in speed, a change in direction, or both. It is measured in metres per second squared (m/s²).

(b) The mass of the ball is 0.0075 kg. Calculate the resultant force acting on the ball when it is accelerating downwards at 2.8 m/s².

The resultant force is 0.021 N. This is calculated using the formula F = ma (Force = mass × acceleration), so F = 0.0075 kg × 2.8 m/s².

Recap

Newton's Second Law of Motion states that the resultant force on an object is proportional to the rate of change of its momentum. For an object of constant mass, this simplifies to the equation F = ma. The force is always in the same direction as the acceleration.

(c) As the ball falls, its speed v is recorded. Fig. 1.2 is the speed-time graph for the falling ball.

(i) Describe what happens to the acceleration between t = 0 and t = 0.040s. Explain why this happens.

Between t = 0 and t = 0.040s, the acceleration decreases from its initial maximum value, eventually reaching zero. This occurs because as the ball's speed increases, the resistive force (drag) from the oil also increases. This opposing force counteracts the ball's weight, causing the resultant force to decrease. When the resistive force balances the weight, the resultant force is zero, and the ball stops accelerating, reaching a constant speed known as terminal velocity.

Recap

Terminal velocity is the maximum constant velocity achieved by a falling object when the resistive force (e.g., drag) equals the force of gravity. At this point, the net force is zero, and the object no longer accelerates.

(ii) By drawing a tangent on Fig. 1.2, determine a value for the acceleration of the ball at t = 0.010s.

To find the acceleration from a speed-time graph, one must calculate the gradient of the tangent to the curve at that specific point. By drawing a tangent at t = 0.010s, the acceleration is found to be approximately 1.5 m/s². (The acceptable range in the mark scheme is 1.2 m/s² to 1.8 m/s²).

Recap

The gradient of a speed-time graph represents acceleration. If the graph is a curve, the instantaneous acceleration at a point is determined by drawing a tangent to the curve at that point and then calculating the gradient of that tangent line (Δy / Δx).

Question 2 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q2 | Topics: Mechanics, Momentum, Energy

Fig. 2.1 shows two identical trolleys, P and Q, held at rest on a frictionless horizontal surface. A load is fixed to trolley P.

There is a compressed spring between trolley P and trolley Q. The trolleys are released. As the spring expands, it pushes the trolleys apart. Trolley Q moves to the right at a constant speed of 0.36 m/s. The mass of each trolley is 1.2 kg. The mass of the load on trolley P is 1.5 kg. The spring has negligible mass.

(a) Calculate:

(i) the speed at which trolley P moves to the left

The speed of trolley P is 0.16 m/s. This is calculated using the principle of conservation of momentum. The total momentum before release is zero, so the total momentum after must also be zero. The momentum of P (m_p * v_p) must be equal and opposite to the momentum of Q (m_q * v_q).

Recap

The Principle of Conservation of Linear Momentum states that for a system of interacting objects, the total momentum remains constant, provided no external forces act on the system. In an explosion or collision, Total Momentum Before = Total Momentum After.

(ii) the kinetic energy of trolley Q when it moves at 0.36 m/s.

The kinetic energy of trolley Q is 0.078 J. This is calculated using the formula for kinetic energy, KE = ½mv². So, KE = 0.5 × 1.2 kg × (0.36 m/s)².

Recap

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses due to its motion. It depends on the mass of the object and the square of its speed. The formula is KE = ½mv², where KE is measured in Joules (J).

(b) State the energy transfer that takes place as the spring expands.

The energy is transferred from elastic potential energy stored in the compressed spring to kinetic energy of the moving trolleys.

Recap

The Principle of Conservation of Energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed from one form to another. Here, the potential energy stored in the deformed spring (elastic potential energy) is converted into energy of motion (kinetic energy).

Question 3 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q3 | Topics: Thermal Physics

Fig. 3.1 shows a small block of ice floating in a beaker of warm water.

(a) State one way in which the motion of the particles in ice differs from the motion of the particles in water.

The particles in ice vibrate about a fixed position, whereas the particles in water are able to move throughout the liquid.

Recap

In a solid (like ice), particles are held in a fixed lattice and can only vibrate. In a liquid (like water), particles are still in contact but can slide past one another, allowing the liquid to flow.

(b) Energy is transferred from the water to the block of ice.

(i) State the name of the thermal process that transfers energy from the water to the ice.

The process is conduction.

Recap

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through direct contact, where vibrating particles pass on their energy to neighbouring particles.

(ii) Calculate the energy transferred from this water as its temperature decreases from 28°C to 10°C. (c=4200 J/(kg°C), m=0.34kg)

The energy transferred is 2.6 × 10⁴ J. This is calculated using the formula ΔE = mcΔθ, where ΔE = 0.34 kg × 4200 J/(kg°C) × (28°C - 10°C).

Recap

Specific heat capacity (c) is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1kg of a substance by 1°C. The formula ΔE = mcΔθ relates energy transferred (ΔE), mass (m), specific heat capacity (c), and change in temperature (Δθ).

(iii) Explain how convection causes the temperature of all the water in the beaker to decrease.

The water near the ice cools and its density increases. This colder water sinks. The warmer water from the bottom then rises to take its place, gets cooled by the ice, and also sinks. This process creates a convection current which circulates the water, distributing the cooling effect throughout the beaker.

Recap

Convection is the transfer of heat in a fluid (liquid or gas) through the movement of the fluid itself. When part of a fluid is heated or cooled, its density changes, causing it to move and create a convection current.

(iv) State what happens to the internal energy of the water as the temperature of the water decreases.

The internal energy decreases because the average kinetic energy of the particles decreases.

Recap

The internal energy of a substance is the sum of the kinetic and potential energies of its particles. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles, so a decrease in temperature means a decrease in internal energy.

Question 4 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q4 | Topics: Optics

The lens in a magnifying glass is a converging lens.

(a) Fig. 4.1 shows the lens of the magnifying glass, its two focal points, F₁ and F₂, and its principal axis.

(i) State what is meant by 'focal point'.

The focal point is the point on the principal axis where rays of light travelling parallel to the principal axis converge after passing through the lens.

Recap

A converging lens is thicker in the middle than at the edges. Its focal length is the distance from the center of the lens to the focal point. This point is crucial for determining the nature and position of images formed by the lens.

(iii) Underline two words in the list that describe the image produced in (a)(ii).

inverted, real, upright, virtual

The image is virtual and upright.

Recap

A magnifying glass produces a magnified image when the object is placed between the lens and its focal point. The image is upright (the same orientation as the object) and virtual (it cannot be projected onto a screen, as the light rays only appear to diverge from it).

(b) The refractive index of the glass used to make the lens is 1.5.

(i) Calculate the speed of light in the glass. (Speed of light in air = 3.0 x 10⁸ m/s)

The speed of light in the glass is 2.0 x 10⁸ m/s. This is found using the formula n = c / v, where n is the refractive index, c is the speed of light in air, and v is the speed of light in the medium. So, v = c / n = (3.0 x 10⁸) / 1.5.

Recap

The refractive index (n) of a material is a measure of how much the speed of light is reduced inside that material. A higher refractive index means a slower speed of light in the medium.

(ii) State what happens to the wavelength of light as it passes into the lens.

The wavelength decreases.

Recap

When light enters a denser medium, its speed decreases. Since the frequency of the light wave remains constant, and wave speed = frequency × wavelength (v = fλ), a decrease in speed must result in a proportional decrease in wavelength.

Question 5 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q5 | Topics: Waves

(a) Describe how a longitudinal wave differs from a transverse wave.

In a longitudinal wave, the oscillation of particles is parallel to the direction of energy transfer, consisting of compressions and rarefactions. In a transverse wave, the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer, consisting of crests and troughs.

Recap

Sound waves are a common example of longitudinal waves. Light and other electromagnetic waves are transverse waves. While many transverse waves require a medium, electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum.

(b) Fig. 5.1 represents a seismic wave produced by an earthquake.

(i) State whether this seismic wave is a P-wave (primary) or an S-wave (secondary). Justify your choice.

This is a P-wave because it is a longitudinal wave, as shown by the areas of compression and rarefaction.

Recap

Seismic waves include Primary (P-waves) and Secondary (S-waves). P-waves are longitudinal and can travel through solids and liquids. S-waves are transverse and can only travel through solids.

(iii) The wave travels at 4600 m/s and completes 5 oscillations as it passes a point. Calculate the time it takes for the wave to pass.

The time taken is 13 s. First, find the frequency using v = fλ, so f = 4600 / (1.2 x 10⁴) = 0.383 Hz. The time for one oscillation (period, T) is 1/f = 2.6s. The time for 5 oscillations is 5 × 2.6s.

Recap

The period (T) of a wave is the time it takes for one complete oscillation to pass a point, and it is the reciprocal of frequency (T = 1/f). The wave equation, v = fλ, links wave speed (v), frequency (f), and wavelength (λ).

Question 6 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q6 | Topics: Electricity & Magnetism

Fig. 6.1 shows an isolated metal sphere suspended by an insulating thread from the ceiling.

(a) The charge on the sphere produces an electric field in the surroundings.

(i) State what is meant by 'electric field'.

An electric field is a region where an electric charge experiences a force.

Recap

Electric field lines are used to represent an electric field. The direction of the line at any point gives the direction of the force on a positive charge, and the spacing of the lines indicates the field's strength.

(b) An earthed metal wire is touched against the surface of the sphere and the sphere is discharged.

(i) State what happens in the wire as the sphere is discharged.

Since the sphere is negatively charged, electrons move from the sphere, through the wire, to the Earth.

Recap

Earthing provides a path for charge to flow to or from a very large reservoir (the Earth). A negatively charged object has an excess of electrons, which will flow to the Earth to neutralize the object. A positively charged object will have electrons flow from the Earth to neutralize it.

(ii) It takes a time of 0.14 ns for the sphere to discharge completely. Calculate the average current. (Charge = 3.5 x 10⁻¹⁰ C)

The average current is 2.5 A. This is calculated using the formula I = Q / t, where I is current, Q is charge, and t is time. So, I = (3.5 x 10⁻¹⁰ C) / (0.14 x 10⁻⁹ s).

Recap

Electric current (I) is the rate of flow of electric charge (Q). One Ampere (A) is defined as one Coulomb of charge passing a point in one second. Remember to convert units like nanoseconds (ns) to seconds (1 ns = 10⁻⁹ s).

Question 7 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q7 | Topics: Electricity

The electromotive force (e.m.f.) of a battery is 7.5 V.

(a) Define the term electromotive force.

Electromotive force (e.m.f.) is the work done in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit. It is the energy supplied by the source per unit charge.

Recap

E.m.f. is measured in Volts (V), where 1 Volt = 1 Joule per Coulomb. It represents the total energy the battery gives to each coulomb of charge. This is different from potential difference, which is the energy converted by the charge across a component.

(b) The battery is connected in series with a variable resistor and a 30Ω resistor.

(ii) Draw a graph to show how the current varies with the resistance of the variable resistor as it increases from 0Ω to 150Ω.

The graph is a curve with a negative gradient of decreasing magnitude, starting from a maximum current and not reaching the x-axis. The maximum current (when variable resistance is 0) is I = V/R = 7.5V / 30Ω = 0.25 A. As resistance increases, the total resistance (R_total = 30 + R_variable) increases, causing the current to decrease according to Ohm's Law.

Recap

For a series circuit, the total resistance is the sum of individual resistances. Ohm's Law (V=IR) states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it, provided physical conditions remain constant. As total resistance increases, the current must decrease for a constant e.m.f.

Question 8 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q8 | Topics: Atomic Physics

The isotope thallium-208 is radioactive. It decays by β-decay.

(a) Thallium-208 decays to an isotope of lead (Pb).

(i) Complete the equation for this decay.

20881Tl → 20882Pb + 0-1β

In beta decay, a neutron turns into a proton, so the proton number increases by 1 (81 to 82), while the nucleon number (208) remains the same. A beta particle (β), which is an electron, is emitted.

Recap

In nuclear equations, both the total nucleon number (top number) and the total proton number (bottom number) must be conserved on both sides of the reaction. A beta particle is represented as 0-1e or 0-1β.

(ii) Explain why γ-emission does not affect the numbers in the equation.

γ-emission consists of electromagnetic waves or rays. It has no mass and no charge, so it does not change the nucleon or proton number of the nucleus.

Recap

Gamma rays (γ) are high-energy photons emitted from a nucleus, usually after alpha or beta decay, as the nucleus transitions from a high-energy state to a lower one. They are a form of electromagnetic radiation, not particles with mass or charge.

(iii) Suggest one reason why a nucleus of thallium-208 is unstable.

The nucleus is unstable because it has too many or an excess of neutrons compared to protons.

Recap

Nuclear stability depends on the neutron-to-proton ratio. For heavy elements, stable nuclei have more neutrons than protons. If there is a significant imbalance, the nucleus becomes unstable and undergoes radioactive decay to reach a more stable configuration.

(b) On Fig. 8.1, draw lines to indicate the paths of β-particles and γ-radiation in the magnetic field.

The γ-radiation is unaffected by the magnetic field and continues in a horizontal line. The β-particles are negatively charged, so they are deflected. Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, they follow a smooth curve downwards, towards the bottom of the page.

Recap

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule helps determine the direction of the force on a moving charge in a magnetic field. The thumb represents Force, the Forefinger represents the magnetic Field, and the Centre finger represents the Current (direction of positive charge flow). Since beta particles are negative, the force is in the opposite direction to that predicted for conventional current.

Question 9 | 0625/41/M/J/24 Q9 | Topics: Astrophysics, Waves

The Sun is one of many billions of stars in the Milky Way. The Sun emits a very large quantity of energy as electromagnetic radiation.

(a) State the three regions of the electromagnetic spectrum in which the Sun emits the most energy.

The three main regions are ultraviolet, visible light, and infrared.

Recap

The electromagnetic spectrum is a continuous range of wavelengths. The regions, in order of increasing wavelength, are: Gamma rays, X-rays, Ultraviolet, Visible Light, Infrared, Microwaves, and Radio waves. All travel at the speed of light in a vacuum.

(b) Calculate the distance from the Sun to the Earth if radiation takes 500s to reach Earth at a speed of 3.0 x 10⁸ m/s.

The distance is 1.5 x 10¹¹ m. This is calculated using the formula distance = speed × time (s = vt). So, distance = (3.0 × 10⁸ m/s) × 500 s.

Recap

The relationship between speed, distance, and time is fundamental in physics. For an object moving at a constant speed, speed = distance / time. This can be rearranged to find any of the three variables.

(c) Approximately 4.6 billion years ago, the Sun formed from an interstellar cloud of gas and became a stable star.

(i) Describe and explain what happens as an interstellar cloud of gas forms a protostar.

The cloud of gas and dust (nebula) collapses due to gravitational attraction between its particles. As the particles pull together, their gravitational potential energy is converted into kinetic energy, causing the internal temperature and pressure to increase, forming a hot, dense protostar.

Recap

A nebula is a giant cloud of dust and gas in space. Star formation begins when the gravitational force within a nebula becomes strong enough to pull the material inward, creating a dense, hot core known as a protostar.

(ii) Describe and explain what happens as a protostar becomes a stable star.

As the protostar's core becomes extremely hot and dense, nuclear fusion begins, where hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing vast amounts of energy. This energy creates an outwards force (radiation pressure) that counteracts the inwards gravitational force. A stable star is formed when these two forces are balanced.

Recap

A stable star, or main-sequence star, exists in a state of hydrostatic equilibrium. The outward thermal pressure from the energy released during nuclear fusion in the core perfectly balances the inward pull of gravity. Our Sun is currently in this stable phase.
