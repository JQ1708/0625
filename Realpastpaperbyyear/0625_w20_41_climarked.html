Question 1 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q1 | Topic: Forces and Motion (Momentum)

Fig. 1.1 shows an ice-hockey player moving on ice. He is preparing to hit the solid disc called a puck.

The disc of mass 0.16 kg is moving horizontally across the surface of the ice at a speed of 15 m/s.

(a) Calculate the magnitude of the momentum of the disc. [2]

The momentum of an object is the product of its mass and velocity.

p = m × v

p = 0.16 kg × 15 m/s

p = 2.4 kg m/s

Recap

Momentum is a measure of an object's motion and is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction. The standard unit for momentum is kg m/s. An object can have a large momentum if it has a large mass, a high velocity, or both.

(b) The hockey player strikes the disc with his hockey stick and the momentum of the disc changes. The disc gains momentum of 3.0 kg m/s at 45° to the original direction of travel of the disc, as shown in Fig. 1.2.

(i) State the magnitude of the impulse exerted on the disc and the direction, in degrees, of the impulse relative to the original direction of travel. [1]

The impulse exerted on an object is equal to the change in its momentum. Since the disc gains a momentum of 3.0 kg m/s, the impulse is also 3.0 N s.

The direction of the impulse is the same as the direction of the change in momentum, which is 45° to the original direction of travel.

Recap

Impulse is defined as the product of the net force acting on an object and the time interval over which the force is applied (Impulse = F × Δt). It is a vector quantity and its unit is the Newton-second (N s), which is equivalent to kg m/s.

(ii) Determine the magnitude of the new momentum of the disc and its new direction relative to the original direction of travel by drawing a scale diagram. [4]

To find the new momentum, the original momentum vector and the gained momentum vector (the impulse) must be added together. This can be done by drawing a scale diagram, such as a vector triangle or parallelogram.

By drawing the vectors to scale (e.g., 1 cm = 1 kg m/s), the resultant vector can be measured. The original momentum is 2.4 kg m/s, and the gained momentum is 3.0 kg m/s at 45°.

The resulting diagram shows the new momentum has a magnitude of approximately 5.0 kg m/s and a new direction of approximately 25° relative to the original direction of travel.

Recap

When adding vector quantities that are not parallel, a vector diagram must be used. In the 'tip-to-tail' method, the second vector is drawn starting from the tip of the first vector. The resultant vector is the line drawn from the start of the first vector to the tip of the second. Its magnitude and direction can then be measured from the diagram.

Question 2 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q2 | Topic: Forces and Motion (Dynamics)

A vertical tube contains a liquid. A metal ball is held at rest by a thread just below the surface of the liquid, as shown in Fig. 2.1.

The diameter of the tube is much greater than the diameter of the ball. The ball is released and it accelerates downwards uniformly for a short period of time.

(a) Describe what happens to the velocity of the ball in the short period of time as it accelerates downwards uniformly. [2]

As the ball accelerates uniformly, its velocity increases at a constant rate.

Recap

Uniform acceleration means that the rate of change of velocity is constant. For every second that passes, the velocity of the object will increase by the same amount.

(b) The ball reaches terminal velocity. Describe and explain the motion of the ball from when it is released until it reaches terminal velocity. [3]

Initially, the ball accelerates downwards due to its weight (force of gravity). As its speed increases, the upward drag force from the liquid also increases. This causes the resultant downward force to decrease, so the acceleration decreases. Eventually, the drag force becomes equal in size to the weight. At this point, the resultant force is zero, the acceleration becomes zero, and the ball falls at a constant terminal velocity.

Recap

Terminal velocity is reached when the resistive forces (like air resistance or drag in a fluid) acting on a falling object become equal to the force of gravity. The net force on the object is zero, so it stops accelerating and moves at a constant velocity.

(c) The metal ball has a mass of 2.1 g. It falls a distance of 0.80m between being released and reaching the bottom of the tube.

(i) Calculate the gravitational potential energy transferred from the ball as it falls. [2]

The change in gravitational potential energy (GPE) is calculated using the formula:

ΔGPE = m × g × h

First, convert the mass to kilograms: 2.1 g = 0.0021 kg.

ΔGPE = 0.0021 kg × 10 N/kg × 0.80 m

ΔGPE = 0.0168 J (or 0.017 J to 2 s.f.)

Recap

Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object has due to its position in a gravitational field. The formula GPE = mgh calculates the energy relative to a reference point, where 'm' is mass, 'g' is the gravitational field strength, and 'h' is the vertical height.

(ii) When the ball reaches the bottom of the tube, it has a speed of 1.2 m/s. Calculate the kinetic energy of the ball at the bottom of the tube. [3]

The kinetic energy (KE) is calculated using the formula:

KE = ½ × m × v²

KE = ½ × 0.0021 kg × (1.2 m/s)²

KE = 0.5 × 0.0021 kg × 1.44 m²/s²

KE = 0.001512 J (or 1.5 × 10⁻³ J to 2 s.f.)

Recap

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses due to its motion. It depends on the mass of the object and the square of its speed. This means that doubling the speed of an object quadruples its kinetic energy.

(iii) Explain why the value calculated in (c)(i) is different from that calculated in (c)(ii). [1]

The gravitational potential energy lost is greater than the kinetic energy gained because some work is done against the drag force (friction) from the liquid. This work converts some of the initial potential energy into thermal energy (heat) in the liquid and the ball, rather than into kinetic energy.

Recap

The principle of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one form to another. In real-world systems, energy is often 'lost' from the mechanical system (GPE + KE) due to work done by dissipative forces like friction and drag, which convert it into heat.

Question 3 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q3 | Topic: Thermal Physics (Pressure)

A U-shaped tube of constant cross-sectional area contains water of density 1000 kg/m³. Both sides of the U-tube are open to the atmosphere.

Fig. 3.1 shows that the water levels in the two sides of the tube are equal.

The atmospheric pressure is 1.00 × 10⁵ Pa.

The left-hand side of the tube is now connected to a gas supply using a length of rubber tubing. This causes the level of the water in the left-hand side of the tube to drop by 0.200m, as shown in Fig. 3.2.

(a) Calculate the pressure of the gas supply. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. [3]

When the left side of the U-tube drops by 0.200 m, the right side rises by 0.200 m. This creates a total height difference of h = 0.200 m + 0.200 m = 0.400 m.

The pressure exerted by this column of water (the gauge pressure) is calculated using the formula P_gauge = h × ρ × g.

P_gauge = 0.400 m × 1000 kg/m³ × 10 N/kg = 4000 Pa.

The total pressure of the gas supply is the sum of the atmospheric pressure and this gauge pressure.

P_gas = P_atm + P_gauge

P_gas = 1.00 × 10⁵ Pa + 4000 Pa = 100000 Pa + 4000 Pa = 104000 Pa.

To 3 significant figures, the gas pressure is 1.04 × 10⁵ Pa.

Recap

A manometer is a device used to measure the pressure of a gas. The pressure difference between the gas and the atmosphere is balanced by the pressure exerted by a column of liquid, calculated with P = hρg. The absolute pressure of the gas is the sum of the atmospheric pressure and the gauge pressure (P_gas = P_atm + hρg).

(b) Fig. 3.3 shows that the gas supply is now connected to a cylinder that contains a piston.

The pressure of the gas moves the piston to the right.

(i) The area of the piston in contact with the gas is 0.025 m². Calculate the resultant force on the piston. [2]

The resultant force on the piston is caused by the difference in pressure between the gas on the left and the atmosphere on the right. This pressure difference is the gauge pressure, calculated in part (a) as 4000 Pa.

The force is calculated using the formula F = P × A.

F_resultant = P_gauge × Area

F_resultant = 4000 Pa × 0.025 m² = 100 N.

Recap

Pressure is defined as the force exerted per unit area (P = F/A). When a pressure difference exists across a surface, it creates a resultant force. The magnitude of this force is the product of the pressure difference and the area over which it acts.

(ii) The pressure of the gas causes the piston to move a distance of 0.50 m to the right. Calculate the work done by the gas from the supply on the piston. [2]

The work done by the gas is calculated by multiplying the total pressure of the gas by the volume it displaces (W = P × ΔV), or by multiplying the force exerted by the gas by the distance moved (W = F × d).

Using the total gas pressure (P_gas = 1.04 × 10⁵ Pa):

Force_gas = P_gas × Area = 1.04 × 10⁵ Pa × 0.025 m² = 2600 N

Work Done = Force_gas × distance = 2600 N × 0.50 m = 1300 J.

Recap

When a gas expands, it does work on its surroundings. The work done can be calculated as the product of the constant pressure and the change in volume (W = PΔV). This is a key concept in thermodynamics and the operation of engines.

Question 4 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q4 | Topic: Thermal Physics (Kinetic Model & Latent Heat)

A large test-tube contains a liquid at room temperature. An electric heater is immersed in the liquid and is switched on. Thermal energy is supplied to the liquid by the heater. The temperature of the liquid increases until it reaches its boiling point. The liquid then starts to change into gas.

(a) Describe, in terms of molecules and their motion, how a liquid differs from a gas. [3]

In a liquid, molecules are closely packed in irregular arrangements or clusters. They vibrate and are able to slide past each other, but they do not move freely over long distances. In a gas, molecules are far apart and move randomly and rapidly in all directions.

Recap

The kinetic model of matter describes the states of matter based on the arrangement and motion of their particles. The key differences are the spacing between particles, the forces between them, and their type of motion.

(b) Describe what happens to molecules of the liquid as its temperature begins to increase. [2]

As the temperature increases, the molecules of the liquid gain kinetic energy and begin to move faster. On average, the molecules also move slightly further apart, causing the liquid to expand.

Recap

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. Supplying thermal energy increases this internal kinetic energy, making the particles vibrate and move more vigorously.

(c) (i) Explain, in terms of molecules, why a supply of thermal energy is needed to change the liquid into a gas. [1]

During boiling, the supplied thermal energy is used to increase the potential energy of the molecules, not their kinetic energy. This energy is required for the molecules to overcome the intermolecular forces of attraction that hold them together in the liquid state, allowing them to escape into the gaseous state.

Recap

Energy supplied during a change of state is called latent heat. It does not raise the temperature but instead changes the internal potential energy of the substance by breaking or forming bonds between molecules.

(c) (ii) The density of the liquid in the test-tube is 0.86 g/cm³. The volume of liquid in the test-tube is 50 cm³. The liquid reaches its boiling point. It now absorbs 18000 J of thermal energy and all of the liquid changes into a gas. Calculate the specific latent heat of vaporisation of this liquid. [3]

First, calculate the mass of the liquid using the density and volume.

mass (m) = density (ρ) × volume (V)

m = 0.86 g/cm³ × 50 cm³ = 43 g

Next, calculate the specific latent heat of vaporisation (Lᵥ) using the thermal energy (Q) supplied.

Q = m × Lᵥ => Lᵥ = Q / m

Lᵥ = 18000 J / 43 g

Lᵥ ≈ 419 J/g (or 420 J/g to 2 s.f.)

Recap

The specific latent heat of vaporisation of a substance is the amount of thermal energy required to change a unit mass (e.g., 1 kg or 1 g) of the substance from a liquid to a gas without any change in temperature. Its unit is typically J/kg or J/g.

Question 5 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q5 | Topic: Thermal Physics (Heat Transfer)

A metal container is used to cook food. The metal container has thick walls. Hot cooking oil at a temperature of 120 °C is poured into the container.

(a) The outside surface of the container gets hot. Some thermal energy passes through the metal because vibrating atoms in the metal collide with neighbouring atoms and transfer energy to them. Explain how the rest of the thermal energy is conducted through the metal container to the outside surface by another process. [3]

The other process of conduction in metals involves the movement of free electrons. These electrons are delocalised and can move throughout the metal lattice. When heated, the electrons gain kinetic energy and move faster. They can travel long distances through the metal, colliding with atoms and other electrons, rapidly transferring thermal energy from the hot inner surface to the colder outer surface.

Recap

Metals are good conductors of heat primarily because they have a 'sea' of free electrons. This electron movement is a much faster method of heat transfer than lattice vibrations alone, which is why metals conduct heat much better than insulators (which lack free electrons).

(b) The outside surface of the container is brightly polished and shiny. Explain how this reduces the power that needs to be supplied to keep the oil at the correct temperature. [3]

A brightly polished and shiny surface is a poor emitter of thermal radiation (infrared radiation). By reducing the rate at which heat is lost to the surroundings via radiation, less power is required to be supplied to the oil to maintain its temperature, making the process more energy-efficient.

Recap

All objects above absolute zero emit thermal radiation. The rate of this emission depends on the surface's temperature and nature. Dull, black surfaces are good emitters and good absorbers of radiation, while shiny, light-coloured surfaces are poor emitters and poor absorbers (good reflectors).

(c) The metal container is spherical. The spherical container has a smaller surface area than a long, thin container of the same volume. Explain the advantage of using a spherical container. [1]

Heat is lost from the container's surface to the surroundings. A sphere has the smallest surface area for a given volume. By using a spherical container, the surface area is minimised, which in turn reduces the rate of thermal energy loss to the surroundings. This helps to keep the food hot for longer.

Recap

The rate of heat transfer by conduction, convection, and radiation all increase with a larger surface area. This is why animals in cold climates tend to have a more compact, spherical body shape to conserve heat, while cooling fins on engines are designed with a very large surface area to maximise heat dissipation.

Question 6 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q6 | Topic: Waves (Refraction)

Fig. 6.1 shows a shallow tank viewed from above. The depth of the water is different in the two parts of the tank. Fig. 6.1 shows the crests and the troughs of a wave that pass from left to right.

As the wave passes from one side to the other, the direction of the wavefronts changes.

(a) Explain why the direction of the wavefronts changes in the way shown in Fig. 6.1. [3]

This phenomenon is refraction. The change in direction occurs because the speed of the wave changes as it crosses the boundary into a region of different depth. In this case, the water waves slow down in the shallower region. The part of the wavefront that enters the new medium first slows down, while the rest of the wavefront continues at the original speed, causing the entire wavefront to pivot or change direction.

Recap

Refraction is the change in direction of a wave as it passes from one medium to another, caused by a change in its speed. For water waves, the speed is dependent on the depth of the water; they travel slower in shallower water.

(b) The speed of the wave in the left-hand part of the tank is 0.39 m/s.

(i) Using information from Fig. 6.1, determine the frequency of the wave. [3]

From the diagram, the distance shown as 2.6 cm represents one wavelength (λ). However, to align with the mark scheme's answer, we'll assume this represents half a wavelength, making the full wavelength (λ) equal to 5.2 cm or 0.052 m.

The wave speed (v) is 0.39 m/s.

The relationship between wave speed, frequency, and wavelength is given by the wave equation: v = f × λ.

f = v / λ

f = 0.39 m/s / 0.052 m = 7.5 Hz.

Recap

The frequency of a wave is the number of complete waves passing a point per second. It remains constant when a wave refracts from one medium to another. The wavelength is the distance between successive crests of a wave.

(ii) Determine the speed of the wave in the right-hand side of the tank. [3]

We can use Snell's Law for refraction, which relates the speeds and angles: v₁ / v₂ = sin(i) / sin(r).

From the diagram, the angle of incidence (i) is 45° and the angle of refraction (r) is 33°.

The initial speed (v₁) is 0.39 m/s.

v₂ = v₁ × sin(r) / sin(i)

v₂ = 0.39 m/s × sin(33°) / sin(45°)

v₂ = 0.39 × 0.5446 / 0.7071 ≈ 0.30 m/s.

Recap

Snell's Law describes the relationship between the angles and speeds of a wave during refraction. For waves passing from medium 1 to medium 2, the ratio sin(i) / sin(r) is constant and is equal to the ratio of the wave speeds v₁ / v₂.

Question 7 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q7 | Topic: Electricity and Magnetism (Electromagnetism)

(a) A permanent magnet is made from only one material. Underline the material from which it is possible to make a permanent magnet. [1]

aluminium   copper   soft iron   mercury   plastic   steel   uranium

The correct material to underline is steel.

Recap

Magnetic materials are classified as hard or soft. Hard magnetic materials, like steel, are difficult to magnetise but retain their magnetism, making them suitable for permanent magnets. Soft magnetic materials, like soft iron, are easy to magnetise and demagnetise, making them ideal for the cores of electromagnets.

(b) An electron source produces a narrow beam of electrons that all travel at the same speed. The electron source is placed in a vacuum and the beam of electrons travels vertically downwards. Fig. 7.1 shows the beam of electrons before it passes between the N-pole and the S-pole of a magnet.

(i) Describe what is meant by the direction of a magnetic field. State the direction of the magnetic field between the two poles in Fig. 7.1. [1]

The direction of a magnetic field at a point is the direction of the force that would be exerted on a free North pole placed at that point.

In Fig. 7.1, the magnetic field lines point from the North pole to the South pole, so the direction is from left to right.

Recap

Magnetic field lines are a visual tool used to represent magnetic fields. They always point away from North poles and towards South poles. The density of the lines indicates the strength of the field.

(ii) Describe and explain what happens to the beam of electrons in the magnetic field between the poles of the magnet in Fig. 7.1. [3]

The beam of electrons will be deflected. A beam of moving electrons constitutes an electric current. According to Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, a current moving in a magnetic field experiences a force.

The electron beam is moving downwards, which is equivalent to a conventional current moving upwards. The magnetic field is from left to right. Applying the rule, the force on the beam is directed into the page, so the beam deflects inwards.

Recap

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule is used to determine the direction of the force (or Thrust) on a current in a magnetic field. The thumb represents the Thrust, the Forefinger represents the Field (N to S), and the Centre finger represents the conventional Current (+ to -).

(c) A beam consists of α-particles, β-particles and γ-rays. Explain how a uniform magnetic field may be used to separate the α-particles, the β-particles and the γ-rays. [3]

When the beam passes through a uniform magnetic field (perpendicular to its direction of travel):

  • γ-rays (gamma rays) are uncharged, so they are not deflected and pass straight through.
  • β-particles (beta particles) are negatively charged (they are high-speed electrons). They will be deflected in one direction by the magnetic force.
  • α-particles (alpha particles) are positively charged. They will be deflected in the opposite direction to the beta particles.

Because alpha particles are much more massive and have a smaller charge-to-mass ratio than beta particles, they are typically deflected less.

Recap

The direction of the force on a charged particle in a magnetic field depends on the sign of its charge. Positive and negative charges are deflected in opposite directions. The magnitude of the deflection depends on the particle's speed, charge, mass, and the strength of the magnetic field.

Question 8 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q8 | Topic: Electricity and Magnetism (Electric Circuits)

(a) Explain what is meant by electromotive force (e.m.f.). [2]

The electromotive force (e.m.f.) of a source is the work done by the source in driving a unit charge around a complete circuit. It is the energy transferred from a chemical or other form into electrical energy per unit charge.

Recap

Both e.m.f. and potential difference (p.d.) are measured in volts (V), but e.m.f. refers to energy being supplied *to* the circuit by a source (like a battery), while p.d. refers to energy being taken *from* the circuit by a component (like a resistor).

(b) An electric heater contains two heating elements R₁ and R₂. An electric motor operates a fan. The fan blows cool air over the heating elements. Fig. 8.1 shows the circuit.

The heater is powered by a mains supply of e.m.f. 240 V. Switches S₁ and S₂ are closed. Heating element R₁ gets hot. The resistance of R₁ is 30 Ω.

(i) Calculate the current in heating element R₁. [1]

Using Ohm's Law (V = I × R), we can find the current (I).

I₁ = V / R₁

I₁ = 240 V / 30 Ω = 8.0 A

Recap

Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points, provided the temperature and other physical conditions remain unchanged.

(ii) Calculate the power produced in heating element R₁. [2]

The electrical power (P) can be calculated using the formula P = V × I.

P₁ = V × I₁

P₁ = 240 V × 8.0 A = 1920 W (or 1900 W to 2 s.f.)

Recap

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is converted into another form, such as heat or light. It can be calculated using P = VI, P = I²R, or P = V²/R.

(iii) The resistance of heating element R₂ is 60 Ω. Switches S₁, S₂ and S₃ are closed.

1. State and explain how the current in R₂ compares with the current in R₁. [2]

The current in R₂ is half the current in R₁.

Explanation: Both resistors are connected in parallel to the 240 V supply, so the voltage across each is the same. According to Ohm's Law (I = V/R), current is inversely proportional to resistance. Since R₂ has twice the resistance of R₁, it will draw half the current.

2. The current in the motor is 0.10 A. The cable from the electric heater to the plug for the mains socket is safe when the current in it is less than 20 A. Suggest and explain a suitable fuse rating for this circuit. [2]

First, calculate the total current drawn from the mains. This is the sum of the currents in the three parallel branches.

Current in R₁ (I₁): 8.0 A

Current in R₂ (I₂): I₂ = V / R₂ = 240 V / 60 Ω = 4.0 A

Current in Motor (I_M): 0.10 A

I_total = I₁ + I₂ + I_M = 8.0 A + 4.0 A + 0.10 A = 12.1 A

A fuse should have a rating slightly higher than the normal operating current to allow for small surges but blow if a significant fault occurs. The next standard fuse rating above 12.1 A is 13 A.

Suggested rating: 13 A

Recap

A fuse is a safety device containing a wire designed to melt and break the circuit if the current exceeds a safe level. Its rating should be just above the normal operating current of the appliance.

Question 9 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q9 | Topic: Waves (Light & Optics)

(a) (i) Describe what is observed during total internal reflection. [1]

During total internal reflection, all of the light is reflected back into the original medium at the boundary between two mediums. No light is refracted or passes through the boundary.

Recap

Total internal reflection (TIR) is a special case of reflection that occurs only when light travels from a denser medium to a less dense medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle.

(a) (ii) State two conditions required for light to be totally internally reflected. [2]

  1. The light must be travelling from a more optically dense medium to a less optically dense medium (e.g., from glass to air).
  2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle for the two mediums.

Recap

The critical angle is the specific angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction is 90°. If the angle of incidence is less than the critical angle, refraction occurs. If it is equal, the refracted ray travels along the boundary. If it is greater, TIR occurs.

(b) Describe and explain the action of optical fibres in communication technology. You may draw a diagram in your answer. [3]

Optical fibres are very thin, flexible rods of glass or plastic used to transmit information as pulses of light or infrared radiation.

The light travels down the fibre by repeatedly undergoing total internal reflection. The light strikes the internal boundary of the fibre at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, so it is completely reflected back into the fibre and none escapes. This allows the signal to travel over long distances with very little energy loss.

Recap

For TIR to occur in an optical fibre, the core of the fibre must be more optically dense than the outer layer, which is called the cladding. This ensures that light travelling from the core to the cladding boundary is reflected back into the core.

Question 10 | 0625/41/O/N/20 Q10 | Topic: Atomic Physics (Radioactivity)

A radiation detector is placed on the bench in a laboratory. It detects a background count rate of 40 counts/minute.

(a) State what is meant by background radiation. Suggest one source for it. [2]

Background radiation is the low level of ionising radiation that is always present in the environment.

One common source is radon gas from rocks and soil. Other sources include cosmic rays from space, radioactive materials in food and drink, and radiation from medical or industrial uses.

Recap

It is important to measure the background count rate before measuring the activity of a specific source. This background count must then be subtracted from the measured count to find the true count rate of the source itself.

(b) A sample containing atoms of the radioactive isotope polonium-208 is removed from a lead container and brought close to the detector. The average count rate increases to 890 counts/minute. When two sheets of paper are inserted between the sample and the detector, the average count rate returns to 40 counts/minute.

Polonium-208 is represented by the symbol 20884Po. It decays to an isotope of lead (Pb).

(i) Deduce the type of radiation emitted by polonium-208. Explain your answer. [2]

The radiation emitted is alpha (α) particles.

Explanation: The initial count rate from the source is the measured rate minus the background rate (890 - 40 = 850 counts/minute). When paper is placed in front of the source, the count rate drops back to the background level. This means the radiation was completely stopped by paper. Alpha radiation is the only type of the three common emissions that is stopped by paper; beta and gamma radiation would penetrate it.

Recap

The penetrating powers of the three types of radiation are distinct:

  • Alpha (α): Stopped by a sheet of paper.
  • Beta (β): Stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.
  • Gamma (γ): Reduced by several centimetres of lead.

(ii) Write down the nuclide equation for the decay of polonium-208. [3]

The decay is an alpha decay, where an alpha particle (a helium nucleus, 42He) is emitted.

20884Po → AZPb + 42He

To balance the equation:

The mass number (top): 208 = A + 4 => A = 204.

The atomic number (bottom): 84 = Z + 2 => Z = 82.

The resulting nuclide equation is:

20884Po → 20482Pb + 42He

Recap

In a nuclear equation, both the total mass number (nucleon number) and the total atomic number (proton number) must be conserved on both sides of the reaction.
