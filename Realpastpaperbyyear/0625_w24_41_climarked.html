Question 1 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q1 | Topic: General Physics

A spring is suspended from a clamp. Fig. 1.1 shows a pointer attached to the lower end of the spring.

A student suspends loads of different weights from the spring and records the readings on the metre ruler.

Fig. 1.2 is the reading-weight graph that the student obtains.

(a) (i) Using Fig. 1.2, determine the reading on the metre ruler when 1. no weight is attached to the spring 2. a weight of 5.6 N is attached to the spring

1. When no weight is attached, the reading on the ruler is 43 cm.

2. With a weight of 5.6 N, the reading on the ruler is 63 cm.

Recap

This experiment investigates Hooke's Law, which states that the force needed to extend or compress a spring by some distance is directly proportional to that distance. The initial reading is the spring's unstretched length.

(a) (ii) Calculate the extension of the spring when the weight attached is 5.6N.

The extension is the difference between the stretched length and the original length. The calculation is 63 cm - 43 cm, which gives an extension of 20 cm.

Recap

The extension of a spring is the increase in its length from its natural, unloaded length. It is a crucial value in spring constant calculations.

(b) Using the values found in (a), calculate the spring constant of the spring.

The spring constant (k) is calculated using the formula k = F / x. Using the given values, k = 5.6 N / 20 cm, which results in a spring constant of 0.28 N/cm.

Recap

The spring constant (k) is a measure of a spring's stiffness. A higher spring constant means a stiffer spring, requiring more force for the same extension.

(c) (i) An object of mass 0.50 kg is attached to the spring. Calculate the weight of the object.

The weight of the object is calculated by multiplying its mass by the acceleration of free fall (9.8 m/s²). The weight is 0.50 kg x 9.8 N/kg, which equals 4.9 N.

Recap

Weight is the force of gravity on an object (W = mg), whereas mass is the amount of matter in an object. Weight is a force measured in Newtons (N).

(c) (ii) The object is pulled downwards until the tension in the spring is 6.5N. The object is released. Calculate the acceleration of the object immediately after it is released.

First, find the resultant force: F_resultant = Tension - Weight = 6.5 N - 4.9 N = 1.6 N. Then, using Newton's second law (F=ma), the acceleration is a = F / m = 1.6 N / 0.50 kg, which gives an acceleration of 3.2 m/s².

Recap

Newton's Second Law of motion states that the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass (F=ma). The resultant force is the vector sum of all forces acting on the object.

Question 2 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q2 | Topic: General Physics

A drag car accelerates uniformly from rest until it reaches the finishing line. The engine is then switched off and a parachute opens. The car decelerates until it stops.

This drag car has a mass of 1400 kg. Fig. 2.2 is the speed-time graph for the car during a race on a straight horizontal track.

(a) (i) Calculate the maximum momentum of the car during the race.

The maximum momentum is calculated using the formula p = mv, where m is mass (1400 kg) and v is maximum velocity (130 m/s). The result is 1.8 x 10⁵ kg m/s.

Recap

Momentum is the product of an object's mass and velocity. It is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction.

(a) (ii) State the feature of Fig. 2.2 that represents the distance travelled by the car.

The distance travelled by the car is represented by the area under the speed-time graph.

Recap

For any speed-time graph, the area under the line or curve represents the total distance travelled. The gradient of the graph represents the acceleration.

(a) (iii) Determine the distance travelled by the car in the first 6.5s.

The distance is the area of the triangle on the graph, calculated as 1/2 x base x height. This is 1/2 x 6.5 s x 130 m/s, which equals 422.5 m (or 420m to 2 s.f.).

Recap

The area of a triangle is given by the formula Area = 1/2 × base × height. This is frequently used to find the distance from speed-time graphs during periods of uniform acceleration.

(b) Describe how Fig. 2.2 shows that, after 6.5s: (i) the car decelerates (ii) the deceleration of the car is not constant.

(i) The car decelerates because the gradient of the graph is negative, indicating that the speed is decreasing.

(ii) The deceleration is not constant because the gradient is changing; the line is a curve, not a straight line.

Recap

Deceleration is a negative acceleration, meaning the velocity of an object is decreasing. On a speed-time graph, a straight downward-sloping line indicates constant deceleration, while a curve indicates non-constant (changing) deceleration.

(c) Describe the energy transfer that takes place as the car slows down.

As the car slows down, its kinetic energy is transferred to thermal energy (heat) in the brakes, tyres, and air due to air resistance and friction.

Recap

The principle of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one form to another. Here, the energy of motion (kinetic energy) is dissipated as heat.

Question 3 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q3 | Topic: General Physics

(a) Define the moment of a force and describe the effect that it measures.

The moment of a force is the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. It measures the turning effect of a force.

Recap

The concept of moments (or torque) is fundamental to understanding levers and rotational motion. The unit of a moment is the Newton-metre (Nm).

(b) A large rectangular block of stone has a square base of side 3.4 m. Fig. 3.1 shows the block at rest on a horizontal surface.

(i) Explain what is meant by centre of gravity.

The centre of gravity is the single point where the entire weight of an object can be considered to act.

Recap

For a uniform object, the centre of gravity is at its geometric center. An object is stable as long as the vertical line passing through its centre of gravity falls within its base of support.

(ii) The weight of the block is 1.3 × 10⁷ N. Calculate the moment of the weight of the block about corner X.

The perpendicular distance from the centre of gravity to corner X is half the base width (3.4 m / 2 = 1.7 m). The moment is Force × Distance = 1.3 × 10⁷ N × 1.7 m = 2.2 × 10⁷ Nm.

Recap

To calculate a moment, you must use the perpendicular distance from the pivot. If the force is not perpendicular to the lever arm, you must find the component of the distance that is.

(c) The block shown in Fig. 3.1 is in equilibrium. State the two different conditions that apply when an object is in equilibrium.

1. The resultant force acting on the object must be zero.

2. The resultant moment about any pivot must be zero.

Recap

An object in equilibrium is not accelerating; it is either at rest (static equilibrium) or moving at a constant velocity (dynamic equilibrium). Both conditions (no net force, no net moment) must be met.

Question 4 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q4 | Topic: Thermal Physics

(a) Describe an experiment to determine the specific heat capacity of aluminium. You may draw a diagram.

To find the specific heat capacity of aluminium, you would use an aluminium block with two holes. An electric heater is placed in one hole and a thermometer in the other. You measure the mass of the block, the initial and final temperature, and the energy supplied by the heater (E = IVt). The specific heat capacity (c) is then calculated using the formula c = E / (mΔθ).

Recap

Specific heat capacity is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1kg of a substance by 1°C. It's a measure of how well a substance stores thermal energy.

(b) An aluminium dish is initially at room temperature. Boiling water is poured into the aluminium dish as shown in Fig. 4.1.

(i) Explain why, after a short time, the dish and the water are the same temperature.

There is a net transfer of thermal energy from the hotter water to the cooler dish via conduction. This continues until they reach the same temperature, a state known as thermal equilibrium, where there is no longer a net flow of energy.

Recap

Heat always flows from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature. Conduction is the transfer of heat through direct contact, primarily in solids.

(ii) Explain, in terms of its particles, why the aluminium expands as the boiling water is poured into the dish.

The particles in the aluminium gain kinetic energy from the hot water, causing them to vibrate more vigorously. This increased vibration causes the average separation between the particles to increase, resulting in the expansion of the dish.

Recap

This phenomenon is known as thermal expansion. Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled. The extent of expansion depends on the material and the temperature change.

(iii) The water in the dish evaporates. Explain, in terms of the water molecules, what is meant by evaporation.

Evaporation is the process where the most energetic molecules at the surface of a liquid gain enough energy to overcome intermolecular forces and escape from the liquid to become a gas, even at temperatures below the boiling point.

Recap

Evaporation is a surface phenomenon and causes cooling, as the average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid molecules decreases. It differs from boiling, which occurs throughout the liquid at a specific temperature.

Question 5 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q5 | Topic: Waves

(a) A loudspeaker produces a sound wave in air. The distance between the centre of a compression and the centre of a neighbouring rarefaction is 0.10m. Calculate the wavelength of the sound wave.

The distance between a compression and a rarefaction is half a wavelength. Therefore, the full wavelength (λ) is 2 × 0.10 m = 0.20 m.

Recap

A sound wave is a longitudinal wave consisting of alternating areas of high pressure (compressions) and low pressure (rarefactions). The wavelength is the distance between two consecutive compressions or rarefactions.

(b) State a typical value for the speed of sound in air.

A typical value for the speed of sound in air is between 330 m/s and 350 m/s.

Recap

The speed of sound varies with the medium it travels through, as well as factors like temperature and humidity. It travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, and slowest in gases.

(c) (i) Calculate the frequency of the sound from the loudspeaker.

Using the wave equation v = fλ, the frequency is f = v / λ. Using a speed of 340 m/s, the frequency is 340 m/s / 0.20 m = 1700 Hz.

Recap

The wave equation (v = fλ) relates the speed (v), frequency (f), and wavelength (λ) of a wave. Frequency is the number of waves passing a point per second, measured in Hertz (Hz).

(c) (ii) Explain whether the sound from the loudspeaker is audible to a human with normal hearing.

Yes, the sound is audible because its frequency of 1700 Hz falls within the typical range of human hearing, which is 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz.

Recap

The range of human hearing is limited. Sounds with frequencies below 20 Hz are called infrasound, and those above 20 000 Hz are called ultrasound. Many animals can hear frequencies outside this range.

(d) Another loudspeaker produces a sound of wavelength 0.40m. Sound from the loudspeaker reaches a sound absorbing surface with a gap of width 0.80m at the centre.

Explain whether it is possible to detect sound from the loudspeaker at either point J or at point K.

Sound will be heard at point K but little or no sound will be heard at point J. This is because diffraction (the spreading of waves) occurs as the sound passes through the gap. Since the gap width (0.80m) is larger than the wavelength (0.40m), the diffraction effect is limited, and the sound does not spread out significantly into the geometric shadow region where point J is located.

Recap

Diffraction is the bending of waves as they pass around an obstacle or through an aperture. The effect is most significant when the size of the gap or obstacle is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength of the waves.

Question 6 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q6 | Topic: Electricity and Magnetism

A potential divider is made by connecting a light-dependent resistor (LDR) and a thermistor in series.

(a) Define potential difference (p.d.).

Potential difference is the work done per unit charge as the charge moves between two points in a circuit. It is also defined as the energy transferred per unit charge.

Recap

Potential difference (or voltage) is measured in Volts (V). One Volt is equal to one Joule of energy per Coulomb of charge (V = J/C).

(b) The electromotive force (e.m.f.) of the supply is E. Describe how the p.d. across the thermistor can be determined using the reading on the voltmeter.

In a series circuit, the sum of the potential differences across the components equals the supply e.m.f. Therefore, the p.d. across the thermistor is the supply e.m.f. (E) minus the reading on the voltmeter across the LDR.

Recap

Kirchhoff's Second Law states that for any closed loop in a circuit, the sum of the e.m.f.s is equal to the sum of the potential drops. This is a statement of the conservation of energy.

(c) The resistance of the LDR decreases and the resistance of the thermistor increases. (i) State what has happened to the light intensity incident on the LDR and the temperature of the thermistor.

The light intensity on the LDR has increased. The temperature of the thermistor has decreased.

Recap

An LDR's resistance decreases as light intensity increases. A standard (NTC) thermistor's resistance increases as temperature decreases.

(c) (ii) Explain what happens to the reading on the voltmeter.

The reading on the voltmeter decreases. In a potential divider, the voltage is shared in proportion to resistance. Since the resistance of the LDR has decreased while the thermistor's resistance has increased, the LDR takes a smaller proportion of the total voltage.

Recap

A potential divider circuit is used to produce a specific output voltage by splitting a source voltage across two resistors in series. The output voltage depends on the ratio of the resistances.

Question 7 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q7 | Topic: Electricity and Magnetism

A solid bar is inside a copper solenoid.

(a) Suggest a suitable material for the bar.

A suitable material for the bar is soft iron.

Recap

A soft iron core is used in electromagnets because it can be easily magnetised and demagnetised, concentrating the magnetic field lines to create a much stronger magnet when current flows.

(b) The right-hand end of the bar is the S pole. (i) On Fig. 7.2, draw at least six field lines to show the pattern and direction of the magnetic field surrounding the bar.

The drawing should show at least one complete field line from the N pole to the S pole. The lines should not cross and should have arrowheads pointing towards the S pole.

Recap

Magnetic field lines are used to represent a magnetic field. They always point from the North pole to the South pole outside the magnet, and the density of the lines indicates the strength of the field.

(b) (ii) The resistance of the variable resistor increases. Explain what happens to the magnetic field surrounding the bar...

Increasing the resistance decreases the current in the coil. This causes the magnetic field strength to decrease. As a result, the magnetic field lines become further apart.

Recap

The strength of an electromagnet is directly proportional to the current flowing through its coil and the number of turns in the coil. Changing the current is a common way to control the magnet's strength.

(c) A square coil of many turns is placed close to the bar. The resistance of the variable resistor is alternately increased and decreased. Explain what happens in the wires of the square coil.

Alternating the resistance causes a changing current in the solenoid, which produces a changing magnetic field. This changing magnetic field cuts through the square coil, which induces an e.m.f. (and a current) in the coil. This process is called electromagnetic induction.

Recap

Faraday's Law of Induction states that a changing magnetic field in a coil of wire induces an electromotive force (e.m.f.). This is the principle behind electric generators and transformers.

Question 8 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q8 | Topic: Atomic Physics

The nuclide notation for the radioactive isotope carbon-14 is ¹⁴₆C.

(a) Using the symbols shown in Fig. 8.1, draw a diagram to show the number of electrons, neutrons and protons in a neutral atom of carbon-14 and how they are arranged.

The diagram should show a nucleus containing 6 protons and 8 neutrons. There should be 6 electrons orbiting the nucleus in shells.

Recap

In nuclide notation (ᴬzX), 'A' is the mass number (protons + neutrons), and 'Z' is the atomic number (protons). In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

(b) Describe how the composition of a neutral atom of carbon-14 is different from the composition of a neutral atom of nitrogen-14 (¹⁴₇N).

Carbon-14 has one fewer proton and one fewer electron than Nitrogen-14. Carbon-14 has one more neutron than Nitrogen-14.

Recap

Atoms of different elements are defined by their number of protons (atomic number). Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Isobars are atoms of different elements with the same mass number, like C-14 and N-14.

(c) Carbon-14 decays by beta (β) emission. (i) State the name of a particle that is identical to a beta-particle.

A beta-particle is identical to an electron.

Recap

Beta decay is a type of radioactive decay where a beta particle (a high-energy electron or positron) is emitted. This process changes a neutron into a proton or vice versa.

(c) (ii) Describe the change that takes place in carbon-14 as a beta-particle is emitted.

During beta emission, a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton and an electron (the beta particle) is emitted.

Recap

When Carbon-14 undergoes beta decay, its atomic number increases by one (from 6 to 7) but its mass number remains the same (14). It transforms into Nitrogen-14.

(d) The half-life of carbon-14 is 5700 years. A very old object is made of wood. It contains 1.2 × 10¹¹ atoms of carbon-14. When it was manufactured, it contained 9.6 × 10¹¹ atoms of carbon-14. Determine the time that has passed since it was manufactured.

The fraction of remaining C-14 is (1.2 × 10¹¹) / (9.6 × 10¹¹) = 1/8. Since 1/8 is (1/2)³, three half-lives have passed. The total time passed is 3 × 5700 years = 17100 years.

Recap

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. This property is the basis for radiometric dating, including carbon dating.

Question 9 | 0625/41/O/N/24 Q9 | Topic: Astrophysics

(a) State what a galaxy is.

A galaxy is a vast collection of stars, gas, and dust held together by gravity.

Recap

Galaxies come in various shapes and sizes, such as spiral (like our Milky Way), elliptical, and irregular. They are the fundamental building blocks of the large-scale structure of the universe.

(b) The Milky Way has a diameter that is approximately equal to 100 000 light-years. Determine this distance in kilometres (km).

This question requires converting light-years to kilometers. (Note: The mark scheme does not provide the conversion factor, so a precise answer cannot be generated from the provided materials. A typical exam would provide this.)

Recap

A light-year is a unit of distance, not time. It is the distance that light travels in one year in a vacuum, which is approximately 9.46 trillion kilometres (9.46 x 10¹² km).

(c) (i) State one observation that allows the speed at which a galaxy is moving away to be determined.

The observation is redshift in the light from the galaxy.

Recap

Redshift is an example of the Doppler effect for light. As a light source moves away from an observer, the wavelength of its emitted light is stretched, shifting it towards the red end of the electromagnetic spectrum.

(c) (ii) State one different observation that is used to determine the distance to a far galaxy.

The distance can be determined by observing the brightness of a standard candle, such as a Type Ia supernova, within that galaxy.

Recap

A standard candle is an astronomical object that has a known, standard luminosity. By comparing this known luminosity to the object's observed brightness, astronomers can calculate its distance.

(c) (iii) State how the speeds of galaxies and their distances from the Earth are related.

The recessional speed of a galaxy is directly proportional to its distance from us. The further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away.

Recap

This relationship is known as Hubble's Law. It is the key observation that supports the theory of an expanding universe and the Big Bang model.

(c) (iv) The best estimate for the Hubble constant H₀ is 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ per second. Use this value to calculate an estimate for the age of the Universe.

The age of the Universe can be estimated as the reciprocal of the Hubble constant (Age ≈ 1/H₀). Age ≈ 1 / (2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹) = 4.5 × 10¹⁷ s.

Recap

The Hubble constant (H₀) represents the rate of expansion of the universe. Its reciprocal gives an estimate of the time since the expansion began, which is an estimate for the age of the Universe.
